Understanding Your NJ Seller Costs

Home sellers in New Jersey face specific costs that don't exist in other states. Here's what each line item is and what you can control.

Agent Commission (Typically 4-6% of sale price)

Post-2024 NAR settlement, commission is fully negotiated in the listing agreement. In most NJ markets, sellers pay a total commission that covers both listing and buyer agents — typically 4.5-6% combined.

NJ Realty Transfer Fee (about 0.86% at $600K, 0.96% at $1M)

State transfer tax paid at closing, charged in marginal bands. On a $500,000 sale it is $4,175; at $600,000, $5,185; at $1,000,000, $9,575. Sales above $350,000 use a higher schedule than sales below it, so the effective rate climbs with price rather than staying flat. Not negotiable. Senior, blind, disabled and low-and-moderate-income sellers qualify for a reduced rate on this fee.

Attorney Fees ($1,500 – $3,500)

NJ is an attorney state. Your lawyer handles contract review, attorney-review negotiation, title clearance, and closing. Flat fee is typical.

Mortgage Payoff (Exact dollar amount)

Your loan balance at closing, plus a per-diem interest charge through the closing date. Request a payoff statement from your lender when you go under contract.

Prorated Property Tax

You pay NJ property taxes through the closing date. The buyer takes over from closing forward. This shows up as either a debit or credit depending on when your town's tax bills come due.

Closing Costs (Recording, title clearance, etc.)

Typically $500-$1,500 in miscellaneous costs: deed recording, municipal smoke cert, title endorsements, overnight courier fees, etc.

The NJ “Exit Tax” — Most Sellers Owe Nothing

Not a separate tax, and probably not yours. New Jersey requires an estimated income tax payment at closing from some sellers — 2% of the price or 10.75% of the gain, whichever is greater. But the residency form (GIT/REP-3) carries sixteen numbered boxes — box 1 is a resident certification and boxes 2 through 16 are exemptions, which apply to residents and non-residents. Box 2 exempts a principal residence, so a homeowner selling the home they actually lived in usually has nothing withheld even when they are leaving the state. It bites mainly on rentals, second homes and inherited property never lived in. This calculator does not deduct it. Jorge coordinates the form with your attorney and CPA.

NJ Graduated Percent Fee (Sellers Above $1,000,000)

The big one, and new. Until July 2025 the buyer paid a flat 1% “mansion tax” on consideration in excess of $1,000,000. P.L. 2025, c. 69 moved that fee to the seller and made it graduated, for deeds submitted for recording on or after July 10, 2025. It is now a seller cost, and the calculator above includes it.

It is a cliff, not a bracket. Each rate applies to the entire sale price, not just the portion above the threshold:

  • Over $1,000,000 through $2,000,000 — 1% of the full price
  • Over $2,000,000 through $2,500,000 — 2%
  • Over $2,500,000 through $3,000,000 — 2.5%
  • Over $3,000,000 through $3,500,000 — 3%
  • Over $3,500,000 — 3.5%

That makes the boundaries unusually consequential. A $2,000,000 sale owes $20,000. A sale one dollar higher owes just over $40,000 — the same house, twice the fee. And because the statute applies to consideration in excess of $1,000,000, a sale at exactly $1,000,000 owes nothing at all, which is a real pricing consideration just under the line.

Stacked on the standard transfer fee, the state takes $30,625 on a $1.5M sale, $77,725 on $2.5M, and $167,035 on $3.6M — 2.0%, 3.1% and 4.6% of the price respectively, before a dollar of commission. The transfer fee calculator breaks it out line by line.

One trap worth knowing: the senior, blind, disabled and low-and-moderate-income reduction applies to the standard transfer fee only. It does not touch the Graduated Percent Fee. A 72-year-old selling at $1.5M gets a reduced base fee and still owes the full $15,000.

Legal responsibility sits with the seller, though the Division of Taxation notes the parties can apportion or shift the burden in contract negotiations. On a normal resale, expect it to be yours. Above $1,000,000 it is worth confirming the figure with your closing attorney or title company before you budget from it.

What This Calculator Leaves Out

Deliberately, because these vary too much to estimate honestly: pre-listing repairs and staging, capital gains tax on any gain above the federal exclusion, payoff of a second mortgage or HELOC you have not entered, HOA transfer and document fees, municipal smoke and carbon-monoxide certification, and any credits you agree to after inspection. Inspection credits are the one sellers most often forget — they quietly give back 1–2% at a point in the deal when the price already feels settled.
