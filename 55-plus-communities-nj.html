55+ Communities in New Jersey

Named age-restricted communities by town and county, how the rules really work, what the HOA covers, and how to time the sale of the single-family home against the purchase.

Updated August 6, 2026 · by Jorge Ramirez

New Jersey's 55+ communities are age-restricted under federal law: at least 80% of occupied units must house someone 55 or older. Monroe Township in Middlesex County alone lists sixteen adult communities on its municipal site. Most downsizers sell the single-family home first, then buy in — that order protects your equity.

Every community named on this page was verified against the community's own website or the municipality's own website. Sources are listed at the bottom. Where a price could not be verified from a builder or the community itself, the price column is left blank rather than guessed.

How does the age restriction in a 55+ community actually work?

The restriction is federal, not a New Jersey invention. Under 24 CFR 100.305, housing qualifies as housing for older persons when "at least 80 percent of its occupied units must be occupied by at least one person 55 years of age or older." That is the floor. It is not the whole rule.

Three things follow from the 80% math, and they surprise people:

  • A younger spouse is usually fine. The rule counts units with at least one qualifying resident, not every resident. Rossmoor in Monroe Township states on its own site that residents must be "at least 55 years young (spouses/companions, at least 48)."
  • Each community writes its own rules on top. The federal floor lets a community be stricter. Minimum ages for a non-qualifying spouse, rules on adult children living at home, guest-stay limits and rental restrictions all live in the association's governing documents, not in federal law.
  • The community has to be able to prove it. Associations document resident ages so they can demonstrate compliance. That is why buying in involves an application and age verification, not just a signed contract.

Read the actual bylaws and rules for the specific community before you write an offer. Two communities on the same road can have meaningfully different rules about who may live with you.

What does the HOA fee cover in a New Jersey 55+ community?

Broadly, the outdoor work you are trying to stop doing, plus the shared amenities. Toll Brothers states that residents at Regency at West Windsor "receive lawn care and snow removal as part of their maintenance-free living arrangement." Regency at Denville describes exteriors "designed to be low maintenance," with "lawn mowing and snow removal … all taken care of."

The amenity half of the fee is what varies most. At Greenbriar at Whittingham the association's own site describes a clubhouse of more than 30,000 square feet with a ballroom, exercise room, indoor and outdoor pools, tennis, pickleball, bocce, outdoor shuffleboard, an on-site restaurant and a golf course. Rossmoor describes a 30,000-square-foot clubhouse with golf on 418 acres. Stonebridge describes a 41,000-square-foot clubhouse. Those facilities are wonderful and they are not free — the bigger the amenity package, the bigger the monthly number.

What the fee almost never covers: your property taxes, your homeowners or HO-6 insurance, your utilities, and anything inside your walls. And the fee is not fixed for life. Before you commit, get these in writing:

  • The current monthly dues and the last three years of dues history.
  • The association's current operating budget and reserve study.
  • Any special assessment already voted or under discussion.
  • Exactly where the association's responsibility stops and yours begins — roofs, siding, decks, driveways and windows are the usual arguments.
  • Rental and resale restrictions, in case your plans change.

What's the difference between an active-adult community and continuing care?

This is the distinction that costs people the most when they get it wrong, and both types can appear on the same municipal list of "adult communities."

An active-adult community sells you real estate. You buy a house, townhome or condo, you hold the deed, you pay taxes on it, you can sell it, and you can leave it to your children. No medical care is bundled in. Rossmoor, Clearbrook, Stonebridge, Covered Bridge and the Regency and Four Seasons communities in the table above are all this type.

A continuing care retirement community sells you a plan of care. A CCRC — often marketed today as a Life Plan Community — is a campus designed to move you across levels of care as your needs change. Monroe Village in Monroe Township is a working example: it is a CCRC operated by Springpoint, and its own site lists independent living, assisted living, memory care, home care, skilled nursing and rehabilitation.

The practical difference for a downsizer: an active-adult purchase is a real estate transaction with an agent, a mortgage option, a title search and an asset at the end of it. A CCRC is typically a contract with an entrance fee and monthly service fees, reviewed by an elder-law attorney rather than negotiated like a house. If long-term care planning is the actual driver of your move, you want the CCRC conversation, not the 55+ community conversation.

Home downsizing: do you sell the single-family home first, or buy into the community first?

For most New Jersey downsizers, sell first. Two reasons, and the second one matters more than people expect.

The first is leverage. Age-restricted communities are a thin, specific market — you may be one of a small number of qualified buyers for a particular model, but so is everyone else, and a seller comparing two offers will take the one that isn't contingent on unloading a four-bedroom colonial. Cash in hand from a closed sale is the strongest position you can be in.

The second is that you cannot actually plan the purchase until you know your net number. Not your sale price — your net, after commission, transfer fees, payoff and any credits you negotiate. That number determines whether you are shopping a $674,000 new build or a resale in an established community, and whether you carry a mortgage into retirement. Run it before you fall in love with a floor plan: start with a home valuation, then work it through the net proceeds calculator.

The tool that makes "sell first" survivable is the timing clause, not a second mortgage. On the sale side, negotiate an extended closing or a post-closing rent-back that gives you 60 to 90 days in the house after you have the money. That window is what lets you close on the new place and move once instead of twice. In a new-construction community the builder's delivery date is fixed, so the sale timeline gets built backward from it.

Where sell-first breaks down is when the right unit appears before you are ready. That happens, and the answer is preparation: have the single-family home genuinely list-ready — cleared out, photographed, priced — so that "we found it" can turn into "we're live on the market" in days rather than months. The full sequence is laid out in the downsizing guide.

What is Stay NJ, and does it lower property taxes for seniors?

Downsizing into a 55+ community does not, by itself, change your tax rate — property tax rates are set by the municipality and apply to age-restricted and non-restricted homes alike in the same town. What does change your bill is which state relief programs you qualify for, and New Jersey now has a senior-specific one.

Per the New Jersey Division of Taxation, Stay NJ requires that you were age 65 or older during 2025, that you "owned and lived in a home for the full 12 months of 2025," and that your income does not exceed $200,000. The state publishes these maximum benefit amounts by income:

  • Income $0 – $100,000: maximum benefit $6,500
  • Income $100,000.01 – $150,000: maximum benefit $5,000
  • Income $150,000.01 – $200,000: maximum benefit $4,000
  • Income over $200,000: not eligible

Two mechanics matter for anyone planning a move. First, seniors now file one combined application — Form PAS-1 — that covers Senior Freeze, ANCHOR and Stay NJ together, and the Division states that "the deadline to apply for the 2025 PAS-1 is November 2, 2026." Second, renters and mobile home owners are not eligible for Stay NJ, which is worth knowing if your downsizing plan involves renting for a year between homes.

The full-year ownership requirement is the piece that interacts directly with a move: a year in which you sold one home and bought another is a year to review carefully with your tax preparer. Verify your own eligibility with the Division of Taxation or a tax professional — this page is a real estate guide, not tax advice, and the program's figures and deadlines can change.

What to verify before you sign anything

  • The governing documents. Bylaws, rules and regulations, and any recent amendments. This is where the real age rules, guest rules, pet rules and parking rules live.
  • The budget and reserves. A community with a thin reserve fund and aging roofs is a special assessment waiting to happen.
  • The resale certificate. Current dues, pending assessments, and any litigation the association is involved in.
  • Whether it is truly age-restricted. "Active adult" is a marketing phrase. Age-restricted is a legal status. Confirm the community actually operates under the federal 55-or-older exemption.
  • Single-level access. The reason most buyers are here. Check the primary bedroom, the laundry, the entry steps and the garage step — a "first-floor primary" can still come with three steps at the front door.
  • Financing. Some condominium associations have project-approval issues that affect conventional or FHA financing. Find out before, not during, the mortgage process.
