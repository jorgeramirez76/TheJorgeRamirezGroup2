Why Bidding Wars Are Still Happening in NJ

I hear the same thing from buyers every fall: "Isn't the market slowing down? Shouldn't it be easier to buy now?" The NJ market is more balanced than 2021. It is not easy. In the towns I work — Summit, Westfield, Maplewood, Chatham, Livingston, Morristown — well-priced homes under a million dollars are still getting multiple offers the first weekend they're on the market.

The reason is simple. Inventory is still tight. Sellers who don't have to move aren't moving. The people who listed in 2020 and 2021 locked in 3% mortgages and aren't giving those up. So every time a good house in a good school district comes to market at a reasonable price, five buyers who've been circling for six months show up at the same open house.

Knowing how to compete isn't optional in this market. It's the difference between getting a house and watching someone else move into it.

The Five Things That Actually Win Competitive Offers

1. Pre-underwriting, not just pre-approval. A pre-approval letter means a loan officer looked at your credit score and income. An underwriter has not touched your file. In a competitive situation, listing agents know this. Pre-underwriting means an actual underwriter has reviewed your tax returns, bank statements, and pay stubs and issued a conditional commitment. That transforms your financing contingency from a risk into a near-certainty. Several of my buyers have won against higher-priced offers because their financing was bulletproof.

2. A smart escalation clause. An escalation clause tells the seller you'll beat any competing offer by a set increment, up to a cap. Done right, it keeps you competitive without overbidding blindly. Done wrong — with a cap too low, an increment too small, or with a listing agent who dislikes them — it can hurt you. Before I submit an escalation clause for a client, I call the listing agent to understand how they're running the offer process and whether they'd prefer a clean offer instead.

3. A modified inspection contingency. Waiving inspection entirely is risky in New Jersey, where housing stock in Essex, Union, and Morris County runs old. A better play is inspection for information only: you agree not to ask the seller to repair anything below a defined threshold (typically $10,000-$15,000), but you retain the right to walk if something catastrophic surfaces. Sellers get certainty. You get protection against a hidden oil tank or failed electrical. Everyone wins.

4. Flexible closing terms. Sellers have lives. Some need to close in 30 days because they're already under contract on their next place. Others need 90 days because they haven't found anywhere to go. Before I submit an offer, I ask the listing agent what the seller's ideal timeline looks like. Matching your closing to what the seller actually needs has won deals where we weren't the highest price.

5. A clean offer. Fewer contingencies. No requests for seller credits on a multiple-offer situation. No asking the seller to leave the washer/dryer/garage fridge in a competitive situation if it's not already listed. Every item you add to your offer is a reason to pick someone else's cleaner package.

Jorge's take: Price matters. But in my experience, sellers with two offers within $10,000 of each other almost always pick the one with less risk, not more money. Certainty has real value. Build your offer around that.

What Won't Help You

Personal letters to sellers have largely fallen out of practice in New Jersey, and for good reason. Fair housing concerns mean listing agents increasingly advise sellers to disregard them. A well-crafted letter about your family isn't going to overcome a $20,000 price gap or a mortgage contingency with a weak lender. Focus on the terms, not the story.

Lowball offers "to leave room to negotiate" don't work in multiple-offer situations. You won't get a counter. You'll get passed over, and the house will go to someone else. If you want a house in this market, you have to put your real number in front.

Know What Town You're Buying In

The bidding war calculus changes by town. In Summit and Maplewood, expect multiple offers on anything priced right under $1.2 million. In Cranford and Scotch Plains, the competition is real but slightly less intense. In some Middlesex County markets, you'll have room to negotiate. I know the difference because I've been in offer situations in all of them. That local knowledge is what you're paying your agent for.

If you're buying in fall 2026 and you keep losing offers, the problem usually isn't your budget. Call me. We'll figure out what's going wrong.

Questions Buyers Ask Me About Bidding Wars

Does waiving the home inspection contingency make sense in NJ?

Waiving inspection entirely is usually too aggressive in New Jersey, where older housing stock — especially in Essex, Union, and Morris County — carries real risk. A better move is an inspection for information only: you do the inspection, you agree not to ask the seller to fix anything below a threshold ($10,000-$15,000), but you retain the right to walk if something catastrophic shows up. That signals seriousness while protecting you from a house with a failed HVAC, knob-and-tube wiring, or a buried oil tank.

What is pre-underwriting and how is it different from pre-approval?

A pre-approval letter means a lender reviewed your credit and income and thinks you qualify. Pre-underwriting means an underwriter has already reviewed your full file — tax returns, pay stubs, bank statements — and issued a conditional commitment. In a competitive NJ offer situation, pre-underwriting turns your mortgage contingency into something closer to a formality. Sellers know a pre-underwritten buyer is very unlikely to fall through on financing. That matters more than price in some situations.

How do escalation clauses work in New Jersey?

An escalation clause says you'll pay X over the highest competing offer, up to a maximum price. For example: "We offer $750,000 and will escalate $5,000 over any other bona fide offer up to $820,000." NJ listing agents vary on how they view escalation clauses — some sellers love the clarity, some find them presumptuous. Your agent should call the listing agent before submitting to find out which approach the seller prefers.

Does a personal letter to the seller help in NJ?

In New Jersey, personal letters to sellers have largely fallen out of practice. Fair housing concerns mean listing agents increasingly advise sellers to disregard them to avoid any appearance of discrimination. A compelling offer with clean terms does more than a letter.

How much over asking price should I offer in NJ's fall 2026 market?

It depends entirely on the town, the property, and how it's priced. In competitive Midtown Direct towns like Summit, Maplewood, and Chatham, well-priced homes are still seeing 5-15% over asking. In slower Middlesex County markets, you may win at asking or below. The honest answer requires a comparative market analysis on that specific property. Anyone quoting you a blanket percentage without looking at the data is guessing.
