The Refrigerator Door Shelf Mistake and What NJ Buyers Learn From Your Fridge

In most New Jersey sales the refrigerator goes with the house, and buyers open it. A fridge that smells, sweats or runs warm on the door shelf tells them something about how the rest of the house was kept.

Why a refrigerator shelf ends up in a real estate blog

House Outlook’s newsletter linked a Better Report piece this week on the one refrigerator shelf that sabotages groceries. It is the door. The door shelves swing out into a warm kitchen every time someone opens the fridge, so they run warmer than the interior, and milk, eggs and dairy stored there spoil sooner. Condiments, juice and anything with vinegar or salt belong on the door. The perishable stuff belongs on the interior shelves toward the back.

That is good grocery advice. For a seller in Westfield, Cranford or Montclair it is also listing advice, because in most New Jersey contracts the refrigerator stays with the house, and buyers open it during showings. Whatever they find in there becomes part of their opinion of the house.

The refrigerator is usually part of the sale in New Jersey

The standard NJ contract lists appliances that are included, and the refrigerator is almost always on it unless the seller excludes it in writing. Buyers and their attorneys look at that list during attorney review. An included fridge that is failing becomes a repair request, a credit, or a line in the inspection report. My guide to what stays with the house in NJ covers the rest of the list.

If you plan to take a fridge you love to the next house, say so in the listing and the contract from day one.

Jorge’s tip: Put a cheap appliance thermometer on the middle shelf and one in the door bin the week before photos. If the door bin reads well above the interior, the gasket is leaking or the fridge is being overloaded. Fix that before a buyer finds warm milk on a Saturday showing.

What a buyer notices when they open your fridge

I have stood in kitchens in Summit and Maplewood and watched buyers open the refrigerator before they open a single cabinet. Here is what registers in the first three seconds.

  • Smell. A sour or fishy smell is the one thing a buyer cannot unsee. It comes from spilled liquid under the crisper drawers, old produce, or an open container in the door.
  • The gasket. A torn, stiff or moldy door seal reads as an old appliance. Close a dollar bill in the door; if it slides out with no resistance, the seal is done. It is the same dollar bill test I use on ovens.
  • Frost and sweat. Ice built up in the freezer or condensation on the door shelves means air is getting in or the fridge is overloaded.
  • Noise. A compressor that clicks, buzzes or runs constantly gets noticed during a quiet showing.
  • Overload. A door packed with gallons and jars sags on the hinges and blocks the cold.

Age, energy and what you can honestly say

Most refrigerators run about ten to fifteen years. The manufacture date is printed on the sticker inside the door or on the back panel. Look it up so you can answer the question instead of guessing. Do not claim it is newer than it is, and do not promise it is energy efficient if you do not know. The NJ seller disclosure statement asks about appliance condition, and a known problem belongs on it.

If the fridge is failing, you have two honest choices. Replace it with a basic, matching-finish model and put the receipt in the listing file, or disclose the problem and expect the buyer to ask for a credit. My post on appliance maintenance and home value walks through the same decision for the rest of the kitchen, and the utility bill guide covers what buyers in Edison and Woodbridge ask about running costs.

The pre-listing fridge routine

  1. Empty it completely, including the door bins, and throw out anything expired or half used.
  2. Pull the crisper drawers and wash the floor under them, where the smell lives. Wipe every shelf with warm water and a little dish soap, then dry.
  3. Wipe the gasket with soapy water, dry it, and run the dollar bill test at the top, side and bottom of each door.
  4. Vacuum the condenser coils, at the back or behind the bottom grille, so the compressor runs quieter and cooler.
  5. Replace the water filter if it has one and keep the receipt.
  6. Restock light. Interior shelves for milk, eggs and dairy, door bins for condiments only, and an open box of baking soda in the back.

What it does and does not do

A clean, quiet, correctly loaded refrigerator will not raise the price of a colonial in Scotch Plains or a condo in Hoboken. It removes one of the fastest ways a buyer decides a house was not looked after, it keeps an included appliance from turning into an inspection credit. Pair it with the odor walk-through, the plan for cooking while the house is listed and the kitchen staging checklist, and the kitchen will hold up to a buyer who opens everything.

Questions about refrigerators, showings and selling a NJ home

Is the refrigerator included when you sell a house in New Jersey?

Usually, yes. The standard NJ contract lists included appliances and the refrigerator is on it unless the seller excludes it in writing before the offer. If you plan to take it, state that in the listing and the contract from the start rather than pulling it after attorney review.

Why is the refrigerator door the worst shelf for milk and eggs?

The door bins swing into a warm kitchen every time the fridge opens, so they run warmer than the interior shelves. Milk, eggs and dairy stored there spoil sooner. Keep condiments and juice on the door and perishables on the interior shelves toward the back.

Do buyers really open the refrigerator during a showing?

Yes, and often before they open a cabinet. They notice smell, a torn or moldy gasket, frost in the freezer, a noisy compressor and an overloaded door. Any of those tells them the appliance is tired, and they carry that impression into the rest of the house.

Should I replace an old refrigerator before listing my NJ home?

If it is failing, either replace it with a basic model in a matching finish and keep the receipt, or disclose the problem and expect a credit request. If it works, clean it, fix the gasket and vacuum the coils instead. A working fridge does not need to be new to pass a showing.

How do I get a smell out of a refrigerator before showings?

Empty it, pull the crisper drawers and wash the floor underneath them, where spilled liquid collects. Wipe every shelf and the gasket with warm soapy water, dry everything, and leave an open box of baking soda in the back. Throw out anything expired or half used before restocking lightly.
