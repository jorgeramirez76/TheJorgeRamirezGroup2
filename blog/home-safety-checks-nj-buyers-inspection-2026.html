Why Safety Issues Hit Differently in a Real Estate Transaction

I've walked through hundreds of inspection reports across Union, Essex, Morris, Middlesex, and Hudson counties. The items that cause the most friction at the negotiation table are rarely the obvious cosmetic problems. Buyers expect the carpet to be worn. They do not expect to learn that your smoke detectors are expired, your generator is parked next to the garage door, or your basement space heater is plugged into an extension cord that's been running through the winter for six years.

Life-safety issues carry a different weight than dated kitchen cabinets. Buyers use them differently. Their attorneys use them differently. And in NJ, the state's Certificate of Smoke Detector and Carbon Monoxide Alarm Compliance — the CSCD — means these issues have a legal dimension that shows up at closing whether you've addressed them or not.

The fix for most of these is $30 and an hour of your time. The cost of not fixing them is handing the buyer a line item to negotiate against after inspection. That's a bad trade every time.

Smoke Detectors: The Most Common Inspection Finding in NJ Homes

What NJ Law Requires

Smoke detectors are required on every level of the home (including the basement), in every bedroom, and in hallways outside sleeping areas. Detectors older than 10 years must be replaced — the manufacture date is printed on the back. A detector that chirps or doesn't respond to the test button is a dead unit in the inspector's report.

Inspection Exposure: High

In the older housing stock I work with across Cranford, Maplewood, Montclair, and similar towns — homes from the 1920s through 1980s — smoke detector placement is often grandfathered to older standards. That matters because NJ requires sellers to bring the home into current compliance for the CSCD certificate before closing. If your local fire department flags placement issues during the CSCD inspection, you're making changes on a deadline with a buyer waiting.

Do the walkthrough before you list. Every level, every bedroom, every hallway. Replace anything older than 10 years. Test what's in place. It costs less than one round-trip tank of gas.

Check every level, every hallway, every bedroom.

Carbon Monoxide Detectors: The NJ Certificate You Cannot Close Without

The CSCD Certificate

NJ requires a Certificate of Smoke Detector and Carbon Monoxide Alarm Compliance before the transfer of title. Your local fire department issues it after an inspection. Without it, you cannot close. CO detectors must be installed within 10 feet of every sleeping area. Combination smoke/CO units count if they're properly rated and positioned.

Closing Risk: Critical

Carbon monoxide is the issue that still surprises sellers. They think of CO as a fuel-burning appliance problem — relevant if you have a gas furnace, water heater, or generator. What they don't always account for is that attached garages are also a CO source. A car warming up in an attached garage, even with the garage door open, can push CO into a home. NJ inspectors know this and buyers' agents know to ask.

If you have a gas furnace, gas water heater, or attached garage — and most homes in Summit, Chatham, Madison, and Westfield have at least two of those three — you need functioning CO detectors near every sleeping area. Not in the basement where the furnace lives. Near the bedrooms where people sleep.

Generator Safety: The Issue NJ Sellers Get Wrong Most Often

The 20-Foot Rule

Portable generators must be placed at least 20 feet from any door, window, or vent. Carbon monoxide from a generator can reach lethal concentrations inside a home in minutes. Generators stored in garages — even with the garage door open — have killed people. NJ buyers and their inspectors will ask where you store and operate yours. Have an honest answer and a correct setup.

Safety Risk: Severe

After every significant NJ storm — and we've had enough of them — I see listings where the generator is stored inside the garage with an extension cord running under the garage door. This is a documented cause of death and a documented inspection flag. If you own a generator and that's how you've been using it, change the setup before any buyer or inspector walks through the property.

Standby generators wired into the home's electrical system through a transfer switch are a different matter. They're typically installed correctly and can be marketed as a selling feature — buyers in Morris County and parts of Essex County value them highly after repeated power outages.

Portable Heaters and Fuel-Burning Appliances

Space heaters left plugged into extension cords in basements and garages are one of the more common inspection flags I see in older NJ homes. The issue is threefold: extension cords are not rated for continuous high-draw use, heating elements left near combustibles create fire risk, and inspectors document both when they see them.

If you have portable heaters in the home, unplug them before any showing or inspection. If you use fuel-burning appliances — kerosene heaters, propane units — confirm they're stored away from the home and that venting on any permanently installed appliances is clear and serviced. A $150 HVAC service call to confirm your furnace and water heater are venting correctly is cheap insurance against an inspection finding that costs you $2,000 in buyer credits.

The Pre-Listing Safety Walkthrough: What to Do This Week

  • Test every smoke detector in the home. Replace any that don't respond or are older than 10 years.
  • Confirm CO detectors are within 10 feet of every sleeping area and are functioning.
  • Contact your local fire department to schedule the CSCD certificate inspection before the listing goes live.
  • Check that your generator is stored at least 20 feet from any door, window, or vent — never inside the garage.
  • Unplug and remove portable space heaters or confirm they're not sitting on extension cords.
  • Schedule a furnace and water heater service to confirm venting is clear — especially if appliances haven't been serviced in over a year.
  • Check GFCI outlets in kitchens, bathrooms, garages, and exterior locations. If they don't click and reset, replace them before inspection.

None of this is expensive. All of it shows up in inspection reports when it's wrong. In a $750K to $1.5M sale — the range most of my listings in Union and Morris counties fall into — every documented safety issue the buyer's attorney can point to is leverage on the final number. Take it off the table before the listing goes live.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do NJ home inspectors check smoke and carbon monoxide detectors?

Yes. NJ home inspectors test every smoke detector and carbon monoxide alarm. Missing, dead-battery, or expired units show up in the inspection report and give buyers negotiating leverage. NJ law requires smoke detectors in every bedroom, in hallways outside sleeping areas, and on every level including the basement. Carbon monoxide detectors are required within 10 feet of every sleeping area.

How far does a portable generator need to be from a NJ home?

Portable generators must be at least 20 feet from any door, window, or vent. This is a life-safety code requirement. NJ inspectors and buyers flag generators stored inside garages or positioned close to the house. If you own a generator, confirm its storage location and document correct placement before any showing.

What is the NJ CSCD certificate and do I need it to sell my home?

The Certificate of Smoke Detector and Carbon Monoxide Alarm Compliance (CSCD) is required by NJ law before any transfer of residential title. Your local fire department issues it after confirming detectors are in place and functioning. You cannot close without it. Schedule the inspection before your listing goes live to avoid delays at closing.

What home safety issues do NJ buyers negotiate on most after inspection?

The most common safety items NJ buyers negotiate after inspection: missing or expired smoke and CO detectors, outdated electrical panels (Federal Pacific and Zinsco especially), missing GFCI outlets in kitchens and bathrooms, fuel-burning appliances with unclear venting, and generators stored improperly. These are treated as material defects, not cosmetic issues — and buyers' attorneys use them accordingly.

How should NJ sellers prepare for a home safety inspection?

Test every smoke detector and replace units older than 10 years. Install CO detectors within 10 feet of each sleeping area. Confirm your generator is stored at least 20 feet from the house. Have fuel-burning appliances serviced and venting cleared. Check GFCI outlets in kitchens, bathrooms, garages, and exterior locations. Then contact your local fire department to schedule the CSCD certificate inspection before you list.
