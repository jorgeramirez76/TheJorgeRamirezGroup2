Wi-Fi Dead Zones Before an NJ Home Sale: What to Check and Document

A weak signal in the back bedroom is a fixable, documentable detail. Sort it out before a buyer tests it from the room they plan to use as an office.

Why connectivity comes up during showings

Buyers who work from home ask about internet the way earlier buyers asked about the furnace. On showings in Summit, Chatham and Montclair I have watched people open a laptop in the room they intend to use as an office, or hold up a phone in the third-floor bedroom to see what the signal does. It is a practical question about their own daily routine, not a judgment about the house.

You cannot control the plan a buyer will choose, but you can remove the surprises that make a room feel like a dead zone and have accurate answers ready. Pair this checklist with the home office preparation guide if a workspace is part of your listing.

Household items and materials that weaken the signal

Most dead zones come from where the router sits and what stands between it and the room. Walk your house with this list before you assume the service itself is the problem.

  • Router location: a router in a basement corner, a closet or behind the television has to push through floors and furniture before it reaches anyone.
  • Plaster, lath and brick: many pre-war homes in Westfield, Cranford and Maplewood have plaster-and-lath walls, and a brick chimney through the center of the house is a solid barrier.
  • Metal and mirrors: radiators, steel doors, refrigerators, filing cabinets and large mirrors reflect or absorb the signal.
  • Kitchen electronics: a running microwave and some older cordless phones interfere with the crowded 2.4 GHz band.
  • Water: a large aquarium or a water heater between the router and a room is more of a barrier than people expect.
  • Neighbors: in Hoboken and Jersey City buildings, dozens of nearby networks compete on the same channels.
  • Extenders placed too far away: an extender sitting in the dead zone repeats a weak signal. It needs to sit where the signal is still strong.

None of these are defects, and none belong on a disclosure form. They explain why one bedroom drops calls and give you something concrete to adjust.

A room-by-room signal test before photos

Take the phone you already own and open a speed-test app. Stand in each room, including the basement, garage entry and any finished attic, and write down the result next to the room name and the time of day. Repeat once in the evening, when the household and the neighbors are all online. That single sheet tells you where the real gaps are.

Then try the cheap fixes first: move the router to a central, elevated, open spot; restart it; check for a firmware update; and connect the television or desktop computer with an ethernet cable if a jack already exists. Repeat the test in the rooms that scored lowest. If the gap remains in a room a buyer is likely to care about, decide whether a mesh system is worth it for you, not just for the showing.

Document what buyers actually ask

Most questions I hear fall into four groups, and each has a factual answer you can prepare in advance.

  • Providers: search the address on the FCC National Broadband Map, then confirm with the provider. Do not say fiber is available because a neighbor has it.
  • Your current service: the provider and plan you pay for today, stated as your plan rather than a promise about speed.
  • Wiring: where the coaxial, fiber or ethernet jacks are, and which rooms have them. Note anything you added or removed.
  • Equipment: the router, modem and mesh units you own, and whether each stays or leaves. The smart-home inventory guide covers the connected devices that depend on them.

Put the answers on one page with your other property documents. Discuss the inclusion language with your agent and attorney before the listing goes live, because equipment you own is personal property unless the contract says otherwise.

What not to do

Do not hide the router in a cabinet for the photographs and leave it there through the showings. Do not buy a new system the week before listing unless you intend to include it and can show the receipt. Do not quote a download speed to a buyer that you have not measured on your own plan. Accurate, modest information holds up better than a number that fails the buyer’s first test after closing.

The FCC Household Broadband Guide is a neutral reference if a buyer asks what speed a household like theirs might need. Point them to it rather than guessing.

Questions about this checklist

Does a Wi-Fi dead zone lower my home’s value?

This article does not claim a price effect. Coverage is a convenience question buyers ask, and a documented answer about providers, wiring and equipment usually settles it.

Should I include my mesh system or router in the sale?

Decide before listing and state it clearly. Equipment you own is personal property unless the contract says otherwise, so confirm the wording with your agent and attorney.

How do I find which internet providers serve my address?

Search the address on the FCC National Broadband Map, then confirm availability and current plans directly with the provider before you tell a buyer anything.

Do older NJ homes have worse Wi-Fi?

Plaster and lath, brick chimneys, metal ductwork and radiators can weaken a signal, but that is a layout fact rather than a defect. Router placement and wired connections address most of it.

Should I run a speed test for buyers?

You can record your own results with the date, room and plan you pay for. Do not promise speeds for a plan or equipment the next owner has not chosen.
