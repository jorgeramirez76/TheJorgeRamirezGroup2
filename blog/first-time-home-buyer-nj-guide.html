Your First NJ Home, One Verified Step at a Time

Start with the documents

The useful answer is specific to you and the property

A headline rate, generic closing-cost percentage, online affordability result, or old assistance amount cannot tell you what a purchase will require. Build the decision from current written loan offers, the live NJHMFA materials, your brokerage agreement and purchase contract, property records, professional reports, insurance quotes, title work, appraisal, and final disclosures.

Educational scope: This guide is general education, not legal, tax, financial, insurance, or lending advice. Your attorney, lender, housing counselor, inspector, insurer, title professional, tax adviser, and other licensed professionals should address your facts.
  • Budget the complete ownership cost
  • Compare written loan offers
  • Verify NJHMFA and HUD options
  • Understand agency and compensation
  • Build a property-specific offer
  • Protect attorney-review deadlines
  • Inspect and investigate
  • Check appraisal, title, and insurance
  • Review the closing package

Step one

Build a budget around the complete monthly and cash picture

Start with a payment you can carry alongside existing obligations, savings goals, maintenance, and an emergency reserve. A lender's maximum and your own comfortable limit answer different questions. Keep them separate.

Recurring ownership costs

Model principal and interest, current parcel taxes, homeowners and any flood insurance, mortgage insurance when applicable, association dues, utilities, and a repair reserve. Obtain a property-specific insurance quote instead of copying a lender's placeholder.

Cash before and at closing

Track the planned down payment, deposits, lender and third-party charges, inspections, prepaid items, initial escrow, moving costs, and cash reserves. Replace every planning estimate with the Loan Estimate, contract, vendor quote, and later the Closing Disclosure.

Verification checkpoint

  • Confirm the current parcel assessment and tax record with the municipality or the State's official property-tax resources.
  • Read association financial, insurance, fee, rule, and special-assessment documents when the property is in a condominium or association.
  • Stress-test changes in taxes, insurance, association costs, and repairs; a fixed loan rate does not freeze the complete housing payment.

Step two

Compare lenders with the same scenario and written forms

Ask several lenders to evaluate the same price, down payment, loan type, term, occupancy, and timing. Compare offers issued close together because rates and market pricing can change. A preapproval is conditional; it is not a final loan approval, property approval, rate promise, or guarantee that a transaction will close.

Read beyond the headline rate

  • Compare the interest rate and APR, loan term, fixed or adjustable structure, points, lender credits, origination charges, and any prepayment feature.
  • Compare total monthly payment, mortgage insurance, tax and insurance assumptions, estimated cash to close, and the services you may shop for.
  • Ask what is locked, for how long, what can change, what documentation remains, and what happens if the property or timeline changes.
  • Ask whether the lender will service the loan and how assistance, gifts, seller credits, or other funding must be documented.

Verification checkpoint

Keep each Loan Estimate and compare matching sections side by side. Ask the lender to explain every difference in writing before selecting an offer.

Step three

Verify public programs on the current page before you plan around them

NJHMFA publishes first-mortgage and down-payment-assistance programs for qualified buyers, including options with distinct definitions and conditions. Award amounts, funding, participating products, income limits, purchase-price limits, property rules, education requirements, and other terms can change. This guide deliberately does not reproduce those changing figures.

Questions for an NJHMFA participating lender

  • Does the current program apply to this borrower, household, property address, property type, purchase price, occupancy plan, and first mortgage?
  • Which income and purchase-price table is current for the property, and is any Urban Target Area rule relevant?
  • Is counseling or homebuyer education required, when must it be completed, and which providers qualify?
  • How is assistance documented, recorded, forgiven or repaid, and what happens after a sale, refinance, transfer, or occupancy change?
  • Which costs may the funds cover, and what additional cash and reserves must the buyer document?

HUD's FHA resources explain FHA-insured financing, while HUD-approved housing counselors can provide independent pre-purchase, budget, and credit counseling. FHA insurance, counseling, or an NJHMFA inquiry does not itself approve a loan.

Verification checkpoint

Open NJHMFA's current program page and linked fact sheets on the day you evaluate eligibility. Then have a participating lender document the program version used for your file.

Step four

Read the brokerage relationship and compensation agreement

New Jersey's current consumer-protection framework requires brokerage service agreements to identify the business relationship and key terms. Before sharing confidential strategy, understand who the licensee represents and what duties follow from that relationship.

  • Read the services, duration, exclusivity or non-exclusivity, termination terms, and any consent concerning dual or designated agency.
  • Read how compensation is calculated, who may pay it, whether it may be shared, and how the agreement handles a gap between what another party offers and what the buyer agreed to pay.
  • Ask what is and is not included: property search, showings, offer preparation, inspection coordination, and communication through closing.

In New Jersey, broker compensation is fully negotiable and not set by law. Do not assume representation is free or that another party will cover the agreed amount. The signed brokerage service agreement controls the buyer's obligation.

Verification checkpoint

Keep the signed Consumer Information Statement and brokerage service agreement. Ask for written answers to compensation or agency questions before signing.

Step five

Write the offer around the property and your risk decisions

Price is only one contract term. Before signing, identify the deposit arrangement, included or excluded items, financing and appraisal terms, inspection scope, property-sale contingency if any, title expectations, proposed closing, occupancy, and the dates for each required action.

Investigate the address, not a town stereotype

  • Review the seller's property-condition disclosure, but do not treat it as a warranty or substitute for independent investigation.
  • Check municipal tax, assessment, permit, zoning, certificate, and open-application records relevant to the parcel and intended use.
  • Review current flood information, insurance availability, utilities, access, boundaries, easements, and association documents as applicable.
  • Test transportation, parking, and other location needs using official, date-specific information and the buyer's actual schedule.

Verification checkpoint

Ask the agent and attorney to identify every deadline, notice method, deposit term, contingency, and document still missing. Record the dates from the signed agreement rather than a generic online timeline.

Step six

Send the signed contract to your attorney immediately

The New Jersey consumer guide says a contract prepared by a real estate licensee must contain an attorney-review clause providing three business days from delivery of the fully signed contracts to consult an attorney. During that period, an attorney may propose changes or disapprove the agreement under the clause.

An agreement prepared by an attorney may not use the same review arrangement, and facts about delivery or later communications can matter. Real estate licensees cannot give legal advice. The contract controls. Ask your New Jersey attorney when review began, when it ends, what communications are required, and whether you may act before review concludes.

Verification checkpoint

Do not calculate the deadline from this page. Send the complete signed package to your attorney as soon as it is delivered and obtain the deadline and strategy from that attorney.

Step seven

Use an independent home inspection and property-specific follow-up

The State's consumer guide recommends a qualified, independent home inspection soon after the contract becomes binding. The inspection helps evaluate visible structure, systems, and conditions; it does not guarantee future performance or cover every specialized risk.

Match the investigation to the property

With the inspector and attorney, decide whether the age, construction, utilities, history, location, disclosure, and planned use call for additional evaluation. Depending on the property, that may include radon, wood-destroying insects, underground tanks, sewer scope, well or septic systems, lead, asbestos, mold, flood exposure, boundary or survey issues, chimney, roof, structural, electrical, plumbing, HVAC, condominium records, or permits.

  • Attend when practical, read the complete report, and ask which items require a licensed specialist.
  • Give notices and reports in the form and time required by the contract.
  • Separate material-condition questions from cosmetic preferences and ask your attorney about contractual options.
  • Re-inspect agreed repairs or request documentation when appropriate; a receipt alone does not establish condition.

Verification checkpoint

Make a written issue list with the report page, proposed follow-up, responsible professional, contract deadline, and final resolution.

Step eight

Keep underwriting, appraisal, title, and insurance in separate lanes

After contract, the lender verifies the borrower and property under the selected loan. Respond to document requests through verified channels and ask before making a material credit, employment, asset, debt, or funding change. The lender determines whether the file meets its requirements.

Appraisal is not inspection

An appraisal is an independent opinion of value used in the lending process; it is not a condition inspection. For a typical covered home loan, CFPB says the borrower is entitled to a copy. Read it for the property identity, features, assumptions, comparable data, conditions, and value conclusion.

Read CFPB's appraisal guidance →

Title work answers ownership questions

Ask the attorney or title professional to explain the search, survey, exceptions, liens, judgments, easements, deed, and resolution of open items. Lender's title insurance protects the lender; owner's coverage is a separate decision.

Read CFPB's owner's title insurance guidance →

Insurance must fit the address

Obtain a property-specific insurance quote early enough to evaluate coverage, exclusions, deductible, replacement-cost assumptions, claim or inspection requirements, and separate flood coverage where relevant. A lender estimate is not an insurance binder or coverage opinion.

Conditions can change the file

A low appraisal, title issue, insurance restriction, property defect, changed credit profile, or missing document can affect financing or contractual choices. Ask the lender and attorney what the signed documents allow; do not rely on a general promise.

Step nine

Reconcile the final documents before you sign

For most covered mortgages, CFPB says the lender must provide the Closing Disclosure at least three business days before closing; listed exceptions use other disclosures. Compare the final form with the latest Loan Estimate and ask why the rate, payment, cash to close, loan costs, credits, prepaids, escrow, taxes, insurance, or other entries changed.

  • Ask for the other closing documents in advance, including the note, mortgage or security instrument, and deed, and review them with the appropriate professional.
  • Complete the agreed final walk-through and document unresolved repair, condition, occupancy, or included-item questions.
  • Treat emailed wire changes as suspicious. Confirm instructions through a telephone number independently verified with the attorney, title company, or settlement agent.
  • Do not sign a blank, incorrect, or unexplained document. Ask the lender about loan terms and the attorney or settlement professional about legal and title documents.
  • Save the full closing package and update the post-closing budget with actual payment, tax, insurance, association, utility, and maintenance obligations.

Verification checkpoint

Create a final variance list showing each difference between the Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure, who explained it, the supporting document, and whether it was corrected.

First-time buyer FAQ

Questions to answer with current documents

How much down payment does a first-time buyer need in New Jersey?

There is no single down-payment requirement for every New Jersey buyer. The loan product, property, occupancy, borrower profile, and any assistance program determine the applicable terms, so compare written Loan Estimates and verify the current program documents before relying on a number.

How do I check current NJHMFA assistance?

Start with NJHMFA's current program page and fact sheets, then use an NJHMFA participating lender to test the exact property, household, income, purchase, occupancy, education, and first-mortgage requirements. Confirm how any subordinate assistance loan is treated if you sell, refinance, or stop using the home as your principal residence.

How does attorney review work in a New Jersey home purchase?

The New Jersey consumer guide says a contract prepared by a real estate licensee must contain an attorney-review clause providing three business days from delivery of the fully signed contracts to consult an attorney. An attorney-prepared agreement may differ. The contract controls, so send it to your attorney immediately and ask for your exact deadline and options.

What should a buyer verify before closing?

Compare the Closing Disclosure with the latest Loan Estimate, review title and appraisal documents, confirm a property-specific insurance quote and coverage, complete the agreed final walk-through, verify wire instructions through a known telephone number, and resolve unexplained changes before signing.
