7 Fire Hazards You Should Never Put in the Trash When Clearing Out a NJ Home Before a Sale

House Outlook published a list of things that should never go in the garbage because they start fires, in the can, at the curb or in the truck. Lithium-ion batteries and aerosol cans lead it. I read it as a seller’s article, because the week before a New Jersey house is listed is when the garage, the basement and the junk drawer get emptied into contractor bags without anyone looking. Here is the list, the New Jersey disposal routes, and the reason your buyer cares what you left behind.

The House Outlook list

The article’s point is that a garbage truck is a compactor, and a compactor crushes whatever is inside. A lithium-ion battery that gets crushed can short and ignite; a half-empty aerosol can releases flammable propellant when it ruptures. The rest of the list follows the same logic: anything that is pressurized, still burning, soaked in solvent or chemically reactive does not belong in a bag that will be squeezed. Most communities, including every county I work in, run drop-off programs for these items. The problem is that people do not use them when they are in a hurry.

Why the seller’s clear-out is the risky moment

Decluttering for a listing is not ordinary trash day. It is twenty years of a garage in Union or Woodbridge going out in a weekend, an estate in Montclair being emptied by relatives who do not know what is in the cans on the shelf, a dumpster at the curb in Edison that the whole street can see. The items on this list are concentrated in exactly the spaces sellers empty last: the garage, the shed, the basement workbench, the pool equipment closet. And whatever does not make it out gets found by the buyer’s inspector or at the final walkthrough, which is its own problem.

Seven items, and where they go in New Jersey

  1. Lithium-ion batteries and the devices holding them. Old phones, laptops, cordless tool packs, vapes, toothbrushes. Tape the terminals and take them to your county’s household hazardous waste day or a retailer drop-off bin. The junk-drawer battery guide covers the smaller ones.
  2. Aerosol cans that are not completely empty. Spray paint, bug killer, hair spray, starter fluid. Fully empty cans usually go in recycling; anything with product left goes to hazardous waste collection.
  3. Propane and butane cylinders. The grill tank, the camp stove canisters, the torch bottle. Exchange the grill tank at a retailer; small cylinders go to the county program. Never leave them at the curb or in a dumpster.
  4. Fireplace and grill ash. Ash can hold live embers for days. Shovel it into a metal can with a tight lid, keep the can outside on concrete away from the house, and only bag it once it has been cold for several days.
  5. Oily rags, stain-soaked cloths and solvent containers. Rags from a deck stain or a furniture refinish can self-heat in a pile. Lay them flat outdoors to dry fully, or keep them in a water-filled metal can; liquids go to hazardous waste.
  6. Pool and spa chemicals. Chlorine, shock and acid react with each other and with moisture. Keep them in original containers, never mix them in one bag, and bring them to the county collection.
  7. Lighters, matches and fireworks. Butane lighters go in the hazardous waste box. For leftover fireworks, ask your local police or fire department how they want them handled rather than guessing.

Jorge’s tip: Put one labeled box by the garage door on day one of the clear-out and call it the hazardous waste box. Every county I work in, from Union to Middlesex, posts its household hazardous waste collection dates online; write the next date on the box. That single step keeps a well-meaning relative in Chatham from bagging a shelf of spray cans and old paint thinner for Tuesday pickup.

What the inspector and the walkthrough find

The buyer’s inspector opens the garage cabinets and the utility closet. A shelf of gas cans, an open bag of pool shock next to the water heater, and a pile of stained rags in the corner go into the report as safety observations, and they change the tone of everything else in it. At the final walkthrough, most New Jersey contracts expect the house broom-clean and free of the seller’s belongings; a half-full propane tank and six cans of old paint left in the shed are not a gift to the buyer, they are a disposal bill and an argument on closing day. If you are not sure what the contract requires you to remove, ask your attorney before the last week.

For buyers: ask what is in the shed

At a second showing in Scotch Plains or Jersey City, open the shed, the garage cabinets and the space under the basement stairs. Ask the seller what will be removed and what they intend to leave. If there is an old oil tank, a stack of chemical containers or an obviously overstuffed workbench, put the removal in writing through your attorney rather than assuming it will be handled.

Questions about disposal, clear-outs and inspections

Why can’t lithium-ion batteries go in the regular trash?

House Outlook’s explanation is that a crushed or punctured lithium-ion battery can short-circuit and ignite, in the bin, at the curb or in the collection truck. Tape the terminals and bring them to a county household hazardous waste collection or a retailer drop-off bin.

Where do New Jersey homeowners take hazardous waste?

Each county I work in, including Union, Morris, Essex, Hudson and Middlesex, runs household hazardous waste collection days or a permanent drop-off, and the schedule is posted on the county’s website. Check the current list of accepted items before you load the car.

Can I leave old paint, chemicals or a propane tank for the buyer?

Generally no. Most New Jersey contracts expect the house delivered broom-clean and free of the seller’s belongings, and hazardous items left behind become a disposal cost and a closing-day dispute. Confirm what must be removed with your attorney.

What should I do with fireplace ashes while the house is listed?

Shovel them into a metal can with a tight lid, keep the can outside on a non-combustible surface away from the house and deck, and only dispose of the ash once it has been completely cold for several days. Never vacuum ash or bag it warm.

Does a buyer’s inspector really look at what is stored in the garage?

Yes. Gas cans, open chemical containers, propane cylinders indoors and piles of oily rags are routinely noted as safety observations in the inspection report, and they color how a buyer reads the rest of the house. Clear them before the inspection, not after.
