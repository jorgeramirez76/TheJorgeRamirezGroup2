9 Things You Should Never Store in a NJ Attic, and What Buyers and Inspectors See When You Do

House Outlook’s list of nine things you should never store in an attic is mostly about the items: paper, wood furniture, electronics and vinyl records do not survive a summer at 130 degrees and a winter below freezing. I care about the other half of the problem. In a New Jersey sale the attic gets opened by the home inspector, and sometimes by the buyer, and what they find stacked on the joists shapes what they think about the rest of the house. Here is the list, what belongs in it for a New Jersey house, and what to clear before the first showing.

The House Outlook list

The article’s point is simple. An unconditioned attic swings from well over 100 degrees in July to below freezing in January, and the humidity swings with it. Paper yellows and molds, wood furniture cracks at the joints, electronics corrode, vinyl warps. The fix is to move those things to a closet or a conditioned basement shelf. I agree with every item, and for a house that is about to be listed I would add a few the article does not mention.

Why the attic matters in a New Jersey sale

Every buyer’s inspector I have worked with in the last nine years goes into the attic. In a 1920s colonial in Westfield, Maplewood or Montclair that means a pull-down ladder or a hatch in a closet ceiling. In a cape in Cranford, Clark or Union it means the knee-wall doors in the upstairs bedrooms. The inspector is looking at the underside of the roof deck for staining, at the bathroom fans to see whether they vent outside, at the insulation depth, at the wiring, and for mouse droppings. Boxes stacked on the joists block all of that, and an inspector who cannot see writes “unable to inspect, recommend further evaluation.” That line costs sellers more than a repair would.

Nine things to get out of the attic

  1. Paper documents and photographs. Tax records, closing papers, family photos. Heat and humidity ruin them, and a buyer should never see your personal records anyway.
  2. Wooden furniture. Joints loosen and veneer lifts. If it is worth keeping, it is worth a conditioned space.
  3. Electronics and media. Old computers, cameras, vinyl, tapes and film. Corrosion and warping, plus the weight.
  4. Candles, cosmetics and anything that melts. They soften, run and stain the insulation below.
  5. Paint, aerosols, propane and gas cans. Fire and pressure risk in a space that hits 130 degrees. An inspector who finds a propane cylinder in an attic writes it up.
  6. Food, pet food and birdseed. It is a buffet for mice and squirrels, and the droppings and chewed boxes are the first thing an inspector photographs.
  7. Fabric, clothing and mattresses. Moths, mildew and the smell that comes downstairs with them when the heat kicks on.
  8. Anything heavy stacked on the ceiling joists. Most New Jersey attics were framed to hold insulation, not a library. Weight shows up as cracks in the bedroom ceilings below.
  9. Anything sitting on the insulation. Boxes compress blown-in or batt insulation and cut what it does. A buyer looking at the heating bills will notice before you do.

Jorge’s tip: After you clear the attic, go up with a flashlight and look at what the inspector will see: the roof deck for dark stains, the bathroom fan ducts for where they end, the insulation for depth, and the floor for droppings. Fix what you can and ask your agent and attorney how to disclose what you cannot. Finding it first is cheaper than reading it in the inspection report.

What can stay

Holiday decorations in sealed plastic bins, luggage, and empty boxes for electronics you plan to move. Keep them on a plywood platform over the joists, away from the hatch, away from the eaves, and off the insulation. Leave a clear path from the hatch to the furnace or air handler if one is up there, and to the bathroom fan ducts. A clear attic with a plywood deck tells the inspector someone took care of the house.

For buyers: ask to see the attic

If the listing agent says the attic is “just storage,” ask your inspector to go up anyway. In a house in Morristown or Chatham with a finished attic room, check whether the room was permitted. In any older house, ask about knob-and-tube wiring, vermiculite insulation and whether the bathroom fans vent to the outside or into the attic. The roof condition and inspection red flags guides cover what each finding means and what it costs.

What this does for the sale

An empty, decked attic does not raise the appraisal. It keeps the inspection report short, which keeps the buyer calm, which keeps the deal together. In my experience the deals that fall apart in attorney review fall apart over a long list of small findings, not one big one. The attic is where that list starts.

Questions about attic storage and home inspections

Do home inspectors in New Jersey really go into the attic?

Yes, whenever there is safe access. They check the roof deck for leaks, the insulation depth, the bathroom fan venting, the wiring and signs of pests. If storage blocks the view, the report says the attic could not be inspected, which worries buyers.

What is safe to keep in an attic?

Sealed plastic bins of holiday decorations, luggage and empty boxes, placed on a plywood platform over the joists and off the insulation. Keep a clear path from the hatch to any equipment and ducts up there.

Will attic storage hurt my home’s value?

Not directly. The risk is a longer inspection report, a buyer who wonders what else was neglected, and compressed insulation that shows up in the heating bills. Clearing the attic is one of the cheapest ways to keep the report short.

Should I disclose a past attic leak that was repaired?

In New Jersey, known material defects should be disclosed, and a repaired leak is often worth stating with the repair records. Ask your agent and your attorney how to word it. Hiding it behind boxes is the wrong answer.

Can I store things on top of the attic insulation?

No. Boxes compress blown-in or batt insulation and reduce what it does. If you need the space, have a contractor add a raised plywood platform above the insulation so the air gap stays intact.
