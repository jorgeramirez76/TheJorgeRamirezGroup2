New Jersey Attorney Review: What Buyers and Sellers Need to Know

Quick answer: In a qualifying New Jersey residential transaction using a contract prepared by a real-estate licensee, the required attorney-review clause provides three business days for an attorney for either party to review and disapprove the agreement. The count begins from delivery of the signed contract to both buyer and seller; Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays are excluded. The parties may extend the period in writing. Your contract, delivery facts, and attorney's advice—not a general article—determine your actual deadline and rights.

Attorney review sits between a signed offer package and the later work of inspections, financing, title, and closing. It moves quickly, but the phrase “three days” can be misleading if no one confirms what was signed, who received it, and when delivery occurred.

This guide explains the standard process without interpreting any particular contract. It is general educational information, not legal, tax, financial, lending, insurance, title, or contract advice. A real-estate licensee can coordinate documents and communication but cannot give either party legal advice.

What Is Attorney Review in New Jersey Real Estate?

New Jersey's rule is narrower than the common shorthand “every home sale has attorney review.” The New Jersey Supreme Court's 2022 Sullivan opinion, discussing N.J.A.C. 11:5-6.2(g), explains that the clause applies to certain contracts prepared by licensed real-estate brokers or salespersons. The covered property is generally a residential property with one to four dwelling units or a vacant one-family lot, and the licensee must have a commission or fee interest in the transaction.

Attorney-drafted agreements, owner-written contracts, auctions, and other transaction types may follow different rules. In Sullivan, the Supreme Court held that the standard attorney-review requirement did not apply to the residential absolute auction at issue. That is one reason to read the actual agreement instead of relying on a slogan about New Jersey practice.

The New Jersey Department of Banking and Insurance homebuyer guide says many buyers choose attorney representation, but it is not a requirement. It also explains that an agent or broker cannot advise a consumer on legal matters. Only an attorney retained by the buyer represents the buyer's interests; an attorney acting for a lender or as a general closing agent may not.

When Does the Three-Business-Day Clock Start?

The regulatory clause counts from delivery of the signed contract to both buyer and seller—not merely from an oral acceptance, the time an agent announces “accepted offer,” or the time the last signature is added. In Conley v. Guerrero, the New Jersey Supreme Court discussed this delivery rule and the notice methods used for attorney disapproval.

The standard clause excludes Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays. Buyer and seller may agree in writing to extend the period. Delivery can occur at different times, and the document trail matters, so avoid calculating the deadline from memory or a text message summary.

Practical rule: As soon as the fully signed contract is circulated, send it to your own New Jersey real-estate attorney and ask for the deadline in writing. Keep the delivery email or other transmission record. If the review overlaps a holiday, do not assume the new deadline—ask counsel to calculate it.

What Can Happen During Attorney Review?

The clause allows an attorney for the buyer or seller to review and disapprove the contract within the stated period, using the required notice procedure. An attorney may also suggest revisions that would make the agreement acceptable. Those revisions are often handled through attorney letters or riders, but the legal effect depends on what the attorneys and parties actually send and agree to.

Buyer Checklist Before the Contract Is Signed

  • Select counsel early. Confirm that the attorney handles New Jersey residential transactions and can review promptly if your offer is accepted.
  • Share your priorities. Flag financing, appraisal exposure, inspection scope, inclusions, closing timing, occupancy, association documents, and any property-specific concern.
  • Keep your evidence. Save the fully signed contract and the message showing when it was delivered.
  • Do not merge deadlines. Attorney review, inspections, mortgage, appraisal, title, and condominium-document review can have separate clocks.
  • Ask before spending. Confirm with counsel when to schedule inspections, deposits, appraisal payments, movers, or other nonrefundable work.

For the broader offer sequence, see the New Jersey buyer offer guide. First-time purchasers can also use the NJ first-time homebuyer guide to organize financing, inspections, and closing steps without confusing them with attorney review.

Seller Checklist When an Offer Is Accepted

  • Choose your attorney before listing or early in marketing. Waiting until a signed contract arrives can consume part of a short review window.
  • Send one complete package. Give counsel the signed contract, addenda, disclosure forms, inclusions and exclusions, requested closing date, and known occupancy arrangements.
  • Identify unusual terms. Flag credits, sale contingencies, appraisal language, inspection limits, lease-backs, solar obligations, estate authority, or tenants.
  • Route legal questions correctly. The listing agent can explain transaction logistics, but contract interpretation and disapproval decisions belong with the seller's attorney.
  • Avoid declaring the deal final too soon. Confirm the end of review and the next written deadlines before changing plans or making commitments.

The New Jersey home-selling timeline places attorney review within the larger listing-to-closing sequence. Sellers should also organize material property information using the NJ seller-disclosure guide; attorney review does not erase separate disclosure duties or contract terms.

Attorney Review Is Not the Inspection or Mortgage Period

Once attorney review ends, the transaction may still be subject to other negotiated provisions. A home inspection can reveal physical conditions. A lender may order an appraisal and complete underwriting. A title company or attorney reviews ownership, liens, and closing requirements. Condominium or homeowners-association documents may also have their own review rules.

Each step answers a different question and may have a different notice method. Finishing attorney review does not mean every contingency is satisfied, the buyer's loan is approved, title is clear, or closing is guaranteed. Keep a written deadline list prepared by the professionals responsible for each item.

Questions to Ask Your Attorney

  2. When was the signed contract delivered to each party, and what is the calculated deadline?
  3. Is attorney review currently open, extended, concluded, or subject to unresolved proposed revisions?
  4. Which terms deserve attention based on my priorities and the property?

Frequently Asked Questions

When does attorney review start in New Jersey?

For a qualifying licensee-prepared contract containing the New Jersey attorney-review clause, the stated three-business-day period is counted from delivery of the signed contract to both buyer and seller. The actual contract and delivery record control, so each party should ask their attorney to confirm the deadline.

Do weekends and legal holidays count during New Jersey attorney review?

The standard regulatory clause says not to count Saturdays, Sundays, or legal holidays in the three-business-day period. The parties may agree in writing to extend it. An attorney should calculate the deadline from the actual delivery facts and contract.

Is a real-estate attorney required to buy a home in New Jersey?

NJDOBI's current buyer guide says many buyers choose attorney representation, but it is not a requirement. A real-estate agent or broker cannot provide legal advice, and only an attorney retained by a party represents that party's legal interests.

Can New Jersey attorney review last longer than three business days?

Yes. The clause provides that buyer and seller may agree in writing to extend the period. Attorneys may also exchange proposed revisions, so the practical review process can continue while terms are being resolved. Ask counsel whether review is still open and what is binding in the specific transaction.

Is attorney review the same as the home-inspection period?

No. Attorney review concerns the contract and permitted attorney action under its clause. Inspection, mortgage, appraisal, title, and other contingencies have their own language and deadlines. Do not assume one deadline extends another.
