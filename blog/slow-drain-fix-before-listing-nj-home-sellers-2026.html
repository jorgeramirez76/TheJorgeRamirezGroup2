Slow Drains Before Listing: The Easy Fix and What a Clog Tells NJ Buyers

A slow drain is one of the few defects a buyer can find in a ten-minute showing. The fix takes a quarter of an hour, and doing it before the photos keeps a maintenance note from turning into an inspection credit.

Why a slow drain matters more the day the house is listed

House Outlook sent out a note this week on an easy way to clear a minor clog without calling a plumber. For a seller in Westfield or Maplewood it is a listing problem, because a slow drain is one of the few defects a buyer can find in a ten-minute showing. Buyers run the faucet. When the water sits, they do not think “hair in the trap.” They think “old pipes,” and they carry that thought into every other room.

I have watched a beautifully staged bathroom in Summit lose a buyer’s confidence over a sink that took a full minute to empty. The fix would have taken a quarter of an hour. This is the version I give clients, and I want it done before the photographer comes, not after the inspection.

The easy fix for a minor clog

Most slow drains I see in listed homes are grease in the kitchen line or hair at the bathroom stopper. Neither needs a plumber.

  • Kitchen sink: Boil a kettle, add a squirt of dish soap to the drain, and pour the hot water in slowly. The soap loosens the grease the hot water softens. Do this twice if the first pass only helps a little.
  • Bathroom sink and tub: Pull the pop-up stopper. Most unscrew or lift straight out. Fish the hair out with a plastic drain strip or a bent coat hanger. What comes out is almost always the whole problem.
  • The film that is left: Pour in a cup of baking soda, follow with a cup of white vinegar, cap the drain for fifteen minutes and flush with hot water. It will not cut a real blockage, but it clears the soap-scum coating that keeps a drain sluggish after the hair is gone.
  • A stubborn one: A cup plunger with the overflow hole covered by a wet rag. Six firm pumps. If that does not move it, stop and call a plumber rather than escalating.

Jorge’s tip: Put a mesh hair catcher in every tub and shower drain the week the house goes on the market. Buyers do not see it, showings happen daily, and it is the cheapest insurance against the one drain that decides to back up the afternoon a serious buyer comes through.

What not to pour down the drain in an older New Jersey home

A large share of the homes I sell in Montclair, Cranford, Union and Elizabeth were built before 1960 and still carry some cast iron or galvanized drain line. Caustic drain cleaners were not designed for that pipe. They sit in the low spots, eat at the walls and leave a chemical soup that makes the job dangerous for the plumber who snakes the line afterward. Plumbers ask whether you used one before they start, and the answer changes the price.

If the hot water, the hair strip and the plunger do not solve it, that is the point to make the call. A plumber’s receipt for a cleared line belongs in your listing file next to the leak checks and the smoke certificate. Buyers trust paper more than a promise.

What a slow drain tells a buyer’s inspector

A home inspector runs water at every fixture. One slow sink is a maintenance note most buyers ignore. Two slow drains on the same floor, a tub that gurgles when the toilet flushes, or a basement floor drain with a ring around it are a different report. Those point past the trap to the branch line or the main, and the inspector will recommend a sewer camera. In older towns with mature street trees, like Westfield, Chatham and Scotch Plains, that camera regularly finds roots in a clay sewer lateral. The buyer then asks for a credit sized to the worst case.

The seller who has already scoped the line controls that conversation. You either fixed it and have the invoice, or you priced the house knowing about it, and the buyer is not negotiating from a surprise. A pre-listing drain checklist

  1. Run every sink, tub and shower for a full minute and time how long each takes to empty.
  2. Clear grease and hair at any fixture that lags, using the steps above, then retest.
  3. Flush every toilet while the nearest tub drains and listen for gurgling.
  4. Check the basement floor drain and the area around the main cleanout for stains or a tide line.
  5. If anything gurgles, backs up or lags after the easy fix, book a plumber and, in an older home, a sewer scope, before the listing goes live.

What it does and does not do

Clearing a slow drain will not raise the appraisal on a house in Edison or Woodbridge. It removes one of the easiest reasons a buyer finds to doubt the plumbing, it keeps a small maintenance note from becoming an inspection negotiation, and it costs almost nothing. Add it to the same weekend as the seller maintenance list and the water damage walk-through, and you have handled the questions an inspector asks first.

Questions about slow drains and selling a NJ home

What is the easiest way to clear a slow drain before a showing?

For a kitchen sink, a kettle of hot water with a squirt of dish soap softens grease. For a bathroom sink or tub, pull the stopper and use a plastic drain strip or a bent wire to lift out the hair. A cup of baking soda followed by a cup of white vinegar, capped for fifteen minutes and flushed with hot water, handles the film that is left behind.

Should I use a chemical drain cleaner in an older NJ home?

I tell sellers not to. Caustic cleaners sit in older cast iron and galvanized pipe, eat at it and can make a later snaking dangerous for the plumber. If the easy fix does not work, call a plumber and get the receipt for your listing file.

Will a home inspector notice a slow drain?

Yes. Inspectors run water at every fixture and watch it drain. One slow sink goes in the report as a maintenance item. Several slow drains on the same floor or gurgling from another fixture make the inspector question the main line, and that becomes a buyer request.

Should I get a sewer scope before listing my NJ home?

If the house is older than the 1970s, has mature trees near the front yard, or has ever backed up, yes. A camera inspection costs little compared with a buyer’s credit request after their own scope finds roots in a clay line. You can then fix it or price it knowingly.

Do I have to disclose past drain or sewer problems in New Jersey?

The NJ seller disclosure statement asks about plumbing and sewer conditions, and a known, unrepaired problem is a material fact. Fix it, keep the invoice, and disclose the repair. A documented fix reassures buyers; a surprise in the inspection report does the opposite.
