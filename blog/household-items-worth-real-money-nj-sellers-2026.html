The Pre-Listing Walkthrough Nobody Talks About

Every seller I work with in Union, Essex, and Morris County gets the same advice before we go to market: walk every room of your house as if you're seeing it for the first time. Not because the staging matters yet — we'll get there — but because you may have valuable items mixed in with the ordinary stuff that you haven't thought about in years.

I've seen it happen too many times. A seller clears out the basement and leaves a box of original pressing vinyl at the curb. A family donates a silver tea service to Goodwill because they assumed it was plated. An estate clears out a garage and donates cast iron pans worth $400 each because they looked old and grimy.

This walkthrough isn't about hoarding. It's about being deliberate. When you're preparing to sell your biggest asset, spend two hours looking at everything else in the house before you start throwing things in donation bags. Here's what to look for.

1 Original Vinyl Records

Original pressings of jazz, blues, soul, folk, and classic rock from the 1950s through the 1970s can be worth real money — especially if they're still in good condition. A first pressing of a classic album on a major label can fetch anywhere from $50 to several hundred dollars, and genuinely rare pressings go much higher. If you have a collection in the attic or basement, take it to a record shop — there are several in Montclair, Westfield, and Morristown that will buy or appraise on the spot — before you donate the whole lot.

2 Sterling Silver Items

Look for trays, candlesticks, tea sets, flatware, ice buckets, and serving utensils. Real sterling is marked "925," "Sterling," or has a lion passant (for English silver). Silver-plated pieces will say "EPNS" or "Silver Plate." The difference in value is significant — sterling scrap value alone is meaningful, and pieces from known silversmiths with provenance can fetch considerably more at auction. If you inherited a sideboard full of silverware and assumed it wasn't real, have it checked before the estate sale.

3 Original Artwork

Not every painting hanging in a Union County Colonial is an original worth thousands. But some are. Oil paintings, especially landscapes and portraits from the 19th and early 20th centuries, can have real value if signed and attributable. Before you let the estate sale company price it at $75, take a photo of the signature and back of the canvas and run it by an appraiser. Regional NJ auction houses like Nye & Company in Bloomfield handle exactly these kinds of evaluations.

4 Fine Crystal

Hand-cut crystal from Waterford, Baccarat, Heisey, or Saint-Louis carries both sentimental and real market value — particularly full sets of stemware or decanters in good condition without chips. If you have a china cabinet full of crystal you've never used, don't send it to a box lot at a tag sale without checking what it is first. The brand name is usually etched into the base of each piece.

5 First Edition Books

First editions, signed copies, and antiquarian books can be valuable. The key indicators are the copyright page (a first edition will typically say "First Published" with a date, and won't have a number line that starts above 1), condition, and whether the original dust jacket is present. Mystery, science fiction, and literature from the mid-20th century are particularly sought after. A book dealer in Summit or Morristown can identify a first edition in about thirty seconds.

6 Vintage Watches

A Rolex, Omega, Longines, or Patek Philippe from the 1950s–1980s sitting in a jewelry box or nightstand drawer can be worth from several hundred to several thousand dollars depending on condition and model. Even non-running vintage watches can have collector value. Don't guess — take it to a watch dealer or jeweler before it disappears into an estate sale box.

7 Antique Furniture With Maker's Marks

Furniture from known American makers — Stickley, Eastlake, Duncan Phyfe, or signed pieces from well-known 18th- and 19th-century craftsmen — can be worth substantial money. Look on the underside of drawers, the backs of mirrors, and the underside of table tops for signatures, stencils, or stamps. A mass-produced "antique-style" dresser from the 1970s is not the same as a genuine Mission oak piece from 1910. An appraiser can tell the difference in minutes.

8 Old Coins and Currency

A collection of old coins in a jar on a shelf isn't always worth face value — and sometimes it's worth many times more. Silver Roosevelt and Mercury dimes pre-1965 have silver content value. Morgan and Peace dollars are collectibles. Paper currency from the 19th or early 20th century can be very valuable in good condition. If you have a collection and don't know what it's worth, the American Numismatic Association has an online directory of certified coin dealers in NJ who offer evaluations.

9 Vintage Cameras

Film photography has had a real collector renaissance. Leica rangefinders, early Nikons, Rolleiflex twin-lens reflexes, and certain vintage Canons and Minoltas have developed serious secondary markets. A Leica M3 in good condition can be worth $500–$1,500+. Even more common 35mm SLRs from the 1970s move quickly to younger buyers at fair prices. Check what you have before it goes to the electronics donation bin.

10 Cast Iron Cookware

This one surprises people every time. Old Griswold, Wagner, and Lodge cast iron pans are actively sought by collectors and cooks. A Griswold No. 8 skillet in good condition can fetch $75–$200+. Older, rarer pieces go higher. The irony is that sellers assume cast iron pans look "old and gross" and toss them, when the age and provenance are exactly what makes them valuable. If you see a skillet with a logo you don't recognize, look it up before donating.

11 Vintage Toys and Board Games

Original, complete board games from the 1950s–1970s in good condition have a real collector market, as do early Hot Wheels, Barbie dolls in original packaging, Lionel train sets, and early action figures. The two keywords here are "original packaging" and "complete." A Game of Life from 1960 with all pieces and original box isn't the same as a missing-spinner version in a water-damaged box. The difference between those two can be $20 versus $200.

The practical step for NJ sellers: Before the photographer arrives and before the estate sale company walks through, do your own inventory. Remove or clearly designate any items that are not included in the sale. If you're unsure about value, a local appraiser or specialty dealer will often do a walkthrough for a reasonable flat fee — and it's almost always worth it on a home that's been in the family for 20+ years.

Why This Matters More Than You Think for NJ Home Sales

There's a practical real estate dimension to this conversation beyond just finding money you didn't know you had. In New Jersey, buyers and sellers occasionally end up in disputes at closing over what's included in the sale. That dining room chandelier. Those built-in bookcases. The mounted TV.

Personal property — furniture, art, silver, records — is not included in the sale of the home unless you specifically negotiate it in. But if those items are sitting in the home during showings and open houses, buyers sometimes assume they're part of the package. Clarify in your listing what's excluded. Remove items before photography. And identify your valuables before the stage-and-shoot process begins, not after.

I've had closings get bumpy because a buyer assumed the grandmother clock in the entry was staying, or because a seller didn't realize the vintage bar set in the basement had been photographed as a selling feature. These are avoidable conversations when you're deliberate upfront.

Frequently Asked Questions

What household items are worth real money when selling a home in NJ?

When selling a home in New Jersey, check for original vinyl records (especially 1950s–1970s pressings), sterling silver items, original artwork, fine crystal (Waterford, Baccarat), first edition books, vintage watches, antique furniture with maker's marks, old coins and currency, vintage cameras, cast iron cookware, and vintage toys. Any of these found in storage or overlooked rooms could be worth hundreds to thousands of dollars.

Should I sell valuable items before or after listing my NJ home?

Before listing. Once your home goes on the MLS, buyers assume that what they see at a showing may be included in the sale or negotiated. Identify and remove valuable items before photos are taken. Selling them beforehand through an estate sale company, local auction house, or online platforms also declutters the home and can fund pre-listing improvements.

How do I know if the silver in my NJ home is real sterling?

Look for hallmarks stamped on the piece — "925," "Sterling," or a lion passant mark on English silver. Silver-plated items are marked "EPNS" (electroplated nickel silver) or "Silver Plate" and have much lower value. A local jeweler or antique dealer in Union County or Essex County can test pieces quickly and inexpensively.

Do NJ home sellers have to disclose what they're taking from the house?

In New Jersey, personal property (furniture, art, family silver, records) is not included in the sale of the home unless specifically negotiated. However, buyers sometimes assume staging items are included. Clarify in writing what's excluded before going to contract to avoid disputes at closing.

Where can NJ homeowners sell valuable items before listing their home?

NJ homeowners can sell valuables through estate sale companies (popular in Union, Morris, and Essex counties), regional auction houses such as Nye & Company in Bloomfield, local antique dealers, consignment shops, and online platforms like eBay, Etsy, or 1stDibs for higher-end pieces. For vinyl records, specialty stores in Montclair, Westfield, and Morristown often buy collections directly.
