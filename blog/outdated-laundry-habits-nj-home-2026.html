6 Outdated Laundry Habits Quietly Damaging Your NJ Home

The Laundry Room Is a Selling Point Now — Whether You Realize It or Not

A decade ago, buyers were grateful if a NJ home had laundry in the unit at all. Today, the laundry room is a real consideration — especially above the $500K mark across Union, Essex, and Morris counties. Buyers want first-floor or second-floor laundry, front-loaders that look and smell clean, and a space that signals the home has been cared for.

That last part matters more than people think. A laundry room that smells musty, has a washing machine drum with a visible mildew ring, or shows moisture staining at the base of the washer tells a story buyers don't want to hear. I've watched those signals send otherwise interested buyers looking for reasons to walk away.

The irony is that most of the damage comes from habits homeowners think are correct. More detergent means cleaner clothes, right? Running a packed machine saves time, right? Wrong on both counts. Here's what's actually happening — and why it matters for your home's value when you decide to sell.

The 6 Outdated Habits to Quit

1 Using Too Much Detergent

Modern high-efficiency washers — which is what most NJ homes have had for the past decade or more — are engineered for small amounts of HE-rated detergent. We're talking one to two tablespoons per load, not the full cap. Manufacturers know you'll fill the cap. The fill line on the cap is not the correct dose. It's the manufacturer's dose, which generates more purchases.

What excess detergent actually does: it leaves a sticky residue inside the drum, around the door gasket, and in the drain pump. That residue traps moisture, body soil, and lint — and becomes the foundation for mold growth. It also coats your clothing, which is why heavily over-dosed laundry often develops a sour smell even right out of the wash. The drum is clean. The clothes are coated in a soap film that holds odors.

The Fix:

Cut your detergent dose in half and see what happens. Your clothes will be just as clean. The drum will stay cleaner. If you've been over-dosing for years, run two or three drum-cleaning cycles with hot water and white vinegar to strip the accumulated residue.

2 Overloading the Washer

Packing the drum full saves trips to the laundry room. It also shortens your machine's lifespan significantly and leaves clothes incompletely rinsed. When the drum can't rotate freely, clothing stays in clumps. The center of that clump gets little water and almost no spin — so it comes out still soapy, still holding soil, and takes far longer to dry. Which leads to the next problem.

On the mechanical side, overloading puts abnormal stress on the drum bearings and the motor. Bearings in a modern front-loader last 10 to 15 years with normal use. Consistent overloading cuts that to 5 to 7 years. A washer bearing replacement on a front-loader typically runs $300 to $600 in parts and labor. A new washer runs $600 to $1,200. Either way, it's an avoidable cost.

The Fix:

Fill the drum no more than three-quarters full. Heavier items — denim, towels, blankets — should be their own load. The machine will work faster, the clothes will be cleaner, and the machine will last longer.

3 Never Cleaning the Washer Drum

This is the one that catches NJ sellers off guard at the worst possible time. The washer is a sealed, humid environment that runs through cycles of hot and cold, wet and damp. Over months and years without cleaning, mold colonizes the door gasket on front-loaders, the drum walls, and the detergent dispenser. By the time it smells, the mold is established.

Buyers notice. A musty laundry room is one of those sensory signals that creates generalized doubt about how a home has been maintained, even if everything else is in order. I've had buyers walk out of a showing and say they didn't want to inherit "whatever's going on in the laundry room." That's a lost sale for a problem that costs nothing to fix.

The Fix:

Run a hot water drum clean cycle monthly — most modern machines have a dedicated drum clean setting. Add two cups of white vinegar, or use a commercial washer cleaner tablet. After every load, leave the door ajar so the drum can dry out. Wipe down the door gasket weekly with a damp cloth. If mold is already established, a diluted bleach wipe-down of the gasket followed by several cleaning cycles will address it in most cases.

4 Using Fabric Softener on Towels

Fabric softener works by coating fabric fibers with a waxy chemical layer that makes them feel softer and reduces static. That works fine for clothing. For towels, that same coating progressively reduces absorbency with every wash. Heavily fabric-softened towels feel plush but barely dry anything. Buyers often don't connect this to the product — they just notice that the towels in the house look worn or feel thin.

The bigger concern for sellers: fabric softener residue in older front-loaders builds up in the dispenser drawer and the pump. That residue turns rancid in a warm, humid laundry room and contributes to the musty smell that kills buyer impressions.

The Fix:

Skip fabric softener entirely on towels and bedding. Use half a cup of white vinegar in the rinse cycle instead — it softens naturally without coating the fibers, and it has a mild deodorizing effect. For clothing where you want softness and reduced static, dryer balls are a non-residue alternative.

5 Leaving Wet Clothes in the Washer

It happens to everyone. You start a load, get busy, and the clothes sit in the drum for four, six, eight hours. The problem is that a sealed, damp drum with wet clothes sitting in it is an optimal mold environment. Within 8 to 12 hours in summer conditions — and NJ summers are humid — that load can start developing that mildewy smell that's very difficult to fully remove once it's in the fabric.

Over time, repeated wet-load sitting accelerates mold growth on the drum walls and gasket, compounding the problem described above. For NJ sellers specifically, the smell can transfer to the room even when the machine is empty — particularly if the laundry room has limited ventilation, which is common in basement utility areas.

The Fix:

Don't start a load you can't be around to move. If you forget and a load sits too long, rewash it before drying — dried mildew smell in fabric is next to impossible to remove with normal laundering. Set a phone timer when you start the machine. Most modern washers also have a delay-start feature you can use to time completion for when you'll actually be home.

6 Ignoring the Dryer Duct Beyond the Screen

Most NJ homeowners clean the lint screen after every load. That's correct. What they don't do is clean the exhaust duct that runs from the dryer to the exterior vent — and that duct accumulates lint every single cycle. In many NJ split-levels and older Colonials, the laundry is in a first-floor or basement utility room with a long exhaust run that makes several turns. Those bends are where lint packs most heavily.

A lint-packed dryer duct is the number one residential dryer fire cause in the US. It's also the number one reason dryers take two cycles to dry a single load — which most homeowners attribute to a failing dryer rather than a blocked exhaust. And it's one of the items NJ home inspectors look at. An inspector who finds a visibly restricted dryer exhaust vent (visible from the exterior) will flag it, and buyers will ask for a credit.

The Fix:

Have the dryer exhaust duct professionally cleaned once a year if you do multiple loads per week. It costs $100 to $175 and takes less than an hour. In the meantime, check the exterior duct flap when the dryer is running — it should swing open fully and close when the cycle stops. If it barely moves, the duct needs cleaning.

The Real Estate Angle: What Buyers Notice in the Laundry Room

When I walk a buyer through a home, the laundry room is one of the rooms that creates the clearest signal about how the previous owners maintained the place. Not because it's complex — it isn't. Because how people maintain the unglamorous spaces is exactly how they maintain everything else.

A clean laundry room with a well-maintained washer and dryer tells buyers: this home has been cared for. A musty laundry room with a stained drum, detergent buildup on the dispenser, and a duct that clearly hasn't been cleaned in years tells buyers: what else didn't they take care of?

In NJ's competitive market, that doubt translates directly into more aggressive credit requests after inspection. I've seen buyers come back with $4,000 to $8,000 in combined requests that were triggered, in part, by the story the laundry room told. The actual repair cost for most laundry room issues is under $300. The credit the sellers ended up giving was ten times that, because they negotiated from a position of acknowledged deferred maintenance.

If you're thinking about listing your NJ home in the next 6 to 12 months, the laundry room is worth an afternoon of focused attention. Clean the drum. Service the dryer duct. Replace the washer hoses if they're rubber and older than five years (braided stainless steel hoses are $20 and last significantly longer). Leave the door open so the room smells clean. It is not a significant investment. The return, in terms of buyer confidence and cleaner negotiating conditions, is real.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does laundry room condition affect NJ home value?

Yes, directly. A musty laundry room, visible drum mold, or a laundry area that shows deferred maintenance creates buyer doubt that extends to the whole house. In NJ's market above $500K, buyers expect a functional, clean laundry room — especially if the washer and dryer are being left with the home. Issues here regularly generate inspection credit requests disproportionate to the actual repair cost.

What laundry room issues do NJ home inspectors flag?

Inspectors most commonly flag: lint-packed dryer exhaust ducts, rubber washer supply hoses older than five years, no drip pan under the washer on upper floors, lack of proper exhaust venting to the exterior, and moisture staining around the washer base indicating past leaks. All of these are fixable for under $200 combined if addressed before the inspection.

How do I get rid of musty washer smell before selling my NJ home?

Run a hot drum-clean cycle with two cups of white vinegar, then follow with a cycle using half a cup of baking soda. Leave the door open between uses. On front-loaders, wipe the door gasket thoroughly with a diluted bleach solution — that's where mold usually establishes first. If the smell persists after three or four cleaning cycles, the gasket may need replacement. Cost: $50 to $150 for the part plus installation.

How often should I clean my washing machine in NJ?

Monthly if you run multiple loads per week. In NJ's humid summer months, mold establishes faster — so monthly cleaning is especially important between June and September. NJ homeowners on hard water (common in Union, Morris, and Middlesex counties) should also run a descaling cycle every one to two months to prevent mineral buildup in the drum and pump.

Does overusing detergent cause real damage over time?

Yes. Excess detergent residue builds up inside the drum, around the door gasket, and in the drain pump. Over time, that residue traps moisture and becomes the foundation for mold. It also coats clothing fibers, which causes them to hold odors. Modern HE washers need a fraction of the detergent cap dosage — typically one to two tablespoons. Start there and adjust based on load size and soil level, not the fill line on the cap.
