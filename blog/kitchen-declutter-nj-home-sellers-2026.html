15 Things to Clear From Your NJ Kitchen Before You List Your Home

Published  ·  August 2026

Short answer

Clear everything off your counters except two or three deliberate items. Remove the toaster, knife block, paper towel holder, spice rack, small appliances, and everything else. Do it before your photographer arrives — not after. A kitchen with clear counters photographs as 30–40% larger than the same kitchen with normal counter clutter. In NJ's competitive market, that difference matters.

The kitchen drives NJ home sales more than any other room. Before your listing photos are taken — and before your first showing — here are the 15 things that need to come out of your kitchen.

Why the Kitchen Is the Room That Makes or Breaks Your NJ Sale

I've been selling homes in Union County, Essex County, Morris County, and across New Jersey for 15 years. If I had to name the single room that most consistently determines whether a home generates multiple offers or sits on the market, it's the kitchen. Not the primary bathroom. Not the living room. The kitchen.

Here's why this matters right now in August 2026. The NJ buyer in the $700K–$1.3M range — which covers most of Westfield, Summit, Chatham, Maplewood, Cranford, and Madison — has been scrolling through listings on Zillow and Realtor.com since February. They've seen dozens of kitchens. They know what granite versus laminate looks like from a thumbnail. They know what "recently renovated" means when there are 47 photos and none of them show the counter near the sink. And they know what a kitchen that hasn't been touched since 2008 looks like — because the countertop clutter is telling the story the seller doesn't want told.

You have two options before you list: renovate the kitchen (expensive, takes time, 60-80% return on investment at best), or stage it aggressively through decluttering (costs almost nothing, can add $10,000–$20,000 to your final number when done properly). The list below is about doing the second thing right.

The 15 Things to Remove From Your Kitchen Before Listing

1

Everything on the Countertops — All of It

Start here. Remove every single item from every counter surface. The toaster, the knife block, the paper towel holder, the fruit bowl, the small appliance collection, the mail pile, the kids' artwork, the charger cords — all of it. Start from zero. Then make deliberate decisions about what earns its spot back (two items maximum per counter section, and only if they're visually clean and recent). This is the highest-return single action you can take before listing photos.

2

The Countertop Appliance Collection

The coffee maker, toaster oven, air fryer, instant pot, stand mixer, blender — each one takes up counter space and signals to buyers that there isn't enough storage space for these items. Pack them. Store them in a cabinet, the garage, or a temporary storage unit. During showings, a single current-model coffee maker on a clear counter is fine. Nothing else.

3

Expired Pantry Items and Spice Jars Over 2 Years Old

Buyers open pantry doors and cabinet doors at showings. A pantry stuffed with expired canned goods, stale grains, and spice jars with faded labels reads as disorganized and neglected. Pull everything out, check dates, and remove what's expired. Organize what remains with labels facing forward on shelves with actual space between items. In NJ homes above $800K, buyers expect organized storage — a chaotic pantry becomes a negotiating point about whether the kitchen "works."

4

The Takeout Packet Drawer

The drawer full of soy sauce packets, plastic forks, paper napkins, extra chopsticks, menus from restaurants that closed in 2021, and the rubber bands that arrived on someone's broccoli in 2019. Empty it. Buyers open junk drawers. Every NJ home has one. The goal is not to eliminate it — it's to make it look like you have a handle on the house. One organized drawer communicates control. A chaotic drawer raises questions about what else hasn't been maintained.

5

Chipped, Mismatched, or Stained Dishes

Open the cabinet and look at the dishes and glasses honestly. Chipped mugs, cloudy glasses, mismatched plates — these aren't things buyers normally see in a listing, but at showings in NJ homes above $700K, buyers open cabinet doors. A cabinet with a matching set of clean dishes and glasses communicates care. A cabinet that looks like the aftermath of a moving sale does the opposite. You don't need to buy new dishes — just remove the damaged and mismatched pieces.

6

Everything on the Refrigerator

The magnets, the school schedules, the birthday party invitations, the coupons, the kids' artwork, the pizza delivery menus. All of it. A refrigerator covered in paper and magnets reads as clutter in listing photos regardless of how clean everything else is — and in wide-angle kitchen shots, it draws the eye immediately. Clear the refrigerator door completely. Also: clear the top of the refrigerator, which is often the most neglected kitchen surface in every home I walk through.

7

Specialty Gadgets That Only Work Once a Year

The bread maker. The popcorn machine. The pasta roller. The fondue set. The waffle iron used three times since purchase. These items are taking up prime real estate in your cabinets and your counters, and they communicate "not enough storage" to buyers. Pack them for the move. You're moving anyway. Start early.

8

Worn Kitchen Towels and Oven Mitts

This is a small detail but buyers notice it at showings. A faded, stained, or fraying kitchen towel hanging from the oven handle is a five-dollar problem that signals years of accumulated neglect to a buyer who has just talked themselves into spending $900,000 on your house. Replace them with clean, neutral-colored replacements before your first showing. It costs $15 and removes a nagging visual.

9

Baby and Kids' Items That Have Been Outgrown

Sippy cups for a six-year-old. Baby bottle drying racks. The kid's plate with the cartoon character that's been there since 2019. These items are not staging assets. They're clutter markers. And in NJ homes where buyers are comparing you to new construction in the area, they read as the house being lived-in in a way that works against you. Pack them with the rest of the things you're moving.

10

The Under-Sink Cabinet Contents

Buyers open the cabinet under the kitchen sink. What they're looking for: evidence of water damage, mold, or plumbing issues. What they find in most NJ homes: a chaotic mix of cleaning products, plastic bags, expired dish soap, and whatever else couldn't find a home. Clear it completely except for two or three current cleaning products. Make sure the cabinet floor shows no staining, moisture rings, or evidence of past leaks. If there is staining, address it or disclose it — don't let a buyer find it on their own.

11

Duplicate Utensils and Broken Gadgets

The utensil drawer that has six spatulas, four wooden spoons, and a can opener from 1994. You do not need all of this for the six to eight weeks your home is on the market. Reduce to the essentials — one of each, organized and visible. A tidy utensil drawer communicates a well-organized home. It's a small thing that NJ buyers register subconsciously during showings.

12

Plastic Bag and Container Collections

The cabinet with the overflow of plastic grocery bags that never gets used. The container drawer with lids that don't match anything. The takeout containers you've been "saving." Pack them or dispose of them. Cabinet space in NJ kitchens above $700K is something buyers expect to have enough of — a cabinet that can barely close because of mismatched containers gives the wrong impression about whether the kitchen is functional.

13

Old Cookbooks, Recipe Cards, and Food Delivery Menus

The stack of cookbooks on the shelf that haven't been opened since smartphones made them redundant. The recipe cards in the drawer. The printed menus from local restaurants. These are not staging assets. Pack them with the books you're moving. A clean shelf in the kitchen — or a maximum of two visually appealing cookbooks standing upright — photographs far better than a crowded shelf of mismatched spines.

14

Cleaning Products on the Counter or Visible Near the Sink

The dish soap, the hand soap pump (unless it's new and attractive), the sponge, the dish scrubber, the bottle of cleaning spray sitting next to the faucet. Store these under the sink or in a cabinet during the showing period. A clear area around the sink makes it look larger. This is a specific detail that professional staging consistently addresses — the area around the kitchen sink is one of the first things buyers and photographers notice.

15

Burned-Out Bulbs and Dead Under-Cabinet Lighting

This last item is not technically clutter — but it affects your kitchen's presentation as much as any physical object. Walk through your kitchen at night and turn on every light. Burned-out recessed cans, flickering under-cabinet fixtures, and dim overhead bulbs make a kitchen look smaller and older in listing photos. Replace every bulb with a matching daylight LED (5000K color temperature) before your photographer arrives. The difference in photos is significant and the cost is under $30.

The photographer's rule: Before your listing photographer arrives, walk through your kitchen and ask yourself: if a stranger saw only this one room and had to decide whether to schedule a showing, what would they think? If the answer is anything other than "spacious, clean, and well-maintained," you have more work to do. I tell every NJ seller I work with: the photographer's visit is your rehearsal for the closing table. The kitchen is your opening act.

What the NJ Market Pays For in Kitchens Right Now

In 2026, NJ buyers in the primary commuter markets are paying a significant premium for kitchens that feel move-in ready. In Westfield, Chatham, Cranford, Maplewood, and Madison, a kitchen that presents as clean and spacious versus one that reads as cluttered and dated can represent $20,000–$40,000 in final offer differences at the $800K–$1.3M price point.

This is not an opinion. It's what I've watched happen in dozens of negotiations over the past three years. The same house — same square footage, same neighborhood, same school district — sells for meaningfully more when the kitchen photographs well than when it doesn't. The decluttering cost: zero to $500. The return: often ten times that.

You don't need a new kitchen. You need a clean one. These 15 items are where you start.

Frequently Asked Questions

How important is kitchen staging when selling an NJ home?

The kitchen is the single most important room in most NJ home sales. In the $600K–$1.5M range that covers most of Union, Essex, and Morris counties, the kitchen is the room buyers compare most directly against new construction. A clean, uncluttered kitchen photographs dramatically better than a cluttered one — and 90% of NJ buyers have made a preliminary decision about your home based on online photos before they ever schedule a showing. Kitchen staging costs almost nothing if you approach it as strategic removal rather than renovation.

Should I renovate the kitchen before selling in NJ?

Full kitchen renovations rarely return their full cost at sale in NJ — typically 60–80% of the renovation cost depending on the market and scope. What does return well: countertop replacement if yours are laminate in a market expecting granite or quartz (typically $3,000–$6,000 with high ROI), repainting cabinets in a neutral color if they're dated (typically $1,500–$3,000), and new hardware on existing cabinets ($200–$500). Decluttering and deep cleaning return far more per dollar than any renovation — do that first before deciding what else needs to happen.

What kitchen updates return the most when selling an NJ home?

In New Jersey's $700K–$1.3M market range, the kitchen updates that return the most at sale are: countertop replacement (granite or quartz if you have laminate), cabinet repainting or refacing, new stainless steel appliances (especially the dishwasher and range, which buyers notice), and fresh backsplash tile. Hardware replacement and a professional deep clean are also high-return investments. Minor updates in the $2,000–$8,000 range often add $10,000–$20,000 to a NJ home's final number.

Does countertop clutter affect NJ home appraisals?

Appraisers are professionals who look past staging and clutter in theory — but in practice, an appraiser walking through a kitchen with clear, spacious counters has a different experience than one walking through a kitchen that reads as cramped and chaotic. More importantly, the photos used to justify comparable sales and condition adjustments reflect your home's presentation. A kitchen that photographs well tends to generate more competitive offers, which gives the appraiser more support for the agreed sales price.

How do I make a small NJ kitchen look bigger for listing photos?

The single most effective technique: clear every countertop completely except two or three deliberate items (a bowl of fruit, a coffee maker if it's new, a single plant). Nothing else. Clear counters are the fastest way to make a small kitchen photograph as larger than it is. Also: clean all appliance surfaces until they reflect light, declutter the top of the refrigerator completely, remove everything from the refrigerator door, and replace any burned-out under-cabinet lights before photos.
