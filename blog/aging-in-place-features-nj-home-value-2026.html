Planning an Accessible NJ Home: Features to Check Before You Buy

Assess entrances, bathrooms, daily routes and future adaptations in an NJ home without assuming an accessibility feature guarantees resale value.

Discuss Your Home PlansSelling in New Jersey
Written by Jorge Ramirez, licensed NJ real estate agent since 2017 · NJ license #1754604 · Keller Williams Premier Properties
Published: 2026-09-12 · Updated: 2026-09-15

Start with the person’s daily routine

A useful home assessment starts with how someone enters, cooks, bathes, sleeps and leaves in an emergency. Write down the tasks that are difficult today and the changes that might help. An occupational therapist or accessibility specialist can translate an individual’s needs into a design brief. Age alone does not tell you which features a household needs.

Use this guide to compare a current home with a prospective purchase. It is a planning worksheet, not a certification that a property is accessible.

Walk one continuous route

  1. Arrival: Record steps, thresholds, door operation, lighting and the distance from parking to the entrance.
  2. Daily living: Trace the route from the bedroom to a full bathroom, kitchen and laundry. Note any floor changes and tight turns.
  3. Bathroom: Record the actual shower entry, available maneuvering area and existing support fittings. A towel rail is not evidence of a correctly installed grab bar.
  4. Kitchen: Identify frequently used storage and controls. List anything that currently requires climbing, stretching or carrying items on stairs.

Photograph and measure with the occupant’s permission. A doorway dimension by itself does not establish that a mobility device can turn through the adjoining space.

Separate movable obstacles from construction

The CDC recommends reducing trip hazards, improving lighting, adding appropriate bathroom supports and providing handrails on stairs. CDC: preventing falls.

Make two lists: furniture and storage arrangements you can change, and building changes needing a qualified assessment. For a ramp, bathroom alteration or doorway change, request a scoped design and ask the municipal construction office which approvals apply. NJ DCA: construction permits and homeowner resources.

Compare the whole plan, not a resale promise

For each property, record the task, present obstacle, proposed change, installer, written quote and any unresolved permission. Include association restrictions where relevant. Do not assume a proposed first-floor bathroom is feasible merely because there is an unused room.

For a listing, describe observable features accurately: entry steps, bathroom location and installed equipment. Avoid labels such as “fully accessible” without a defined basis. No particular adaptation guarantees a higher sale price; its usefulness depends on the person and property.

Questions about this checklist

Does a first-floor bedroom make a home accessible?

No. Check the entrance, bathroom, circulation and daily tasks together. A bedroom location alone does not establish that the home meets someone’s needs.

Should I use a universal doorway width as my purchase test?

No. Record actual clear openings and turning space, then have the individual and an accessibility professional assess the complete route.
