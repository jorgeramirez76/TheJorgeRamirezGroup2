10 Lifesaving Home Safety Tips NJ Homeowners Probably Don't Know

Published  ·  July 2026

Most homeowners know the basics — smoke detectors, locking the doors. These 10 tips go deeper: the lesser-known hazards hiding in plain sight in NJ homes that most people don't think about until something goes wrong.

After 15 years selling homes across Union, Essex, Morris, and Middlesex counties, I've walked through a lot of houses. I've also read a lot of inspection reports. Some of the most dangerous conditions in NJ homes aren't the obvious ones — they're the things homeowners live with for years because nobody ever told them to look.

Here are 10 home safety tips that most NJ homeowners don't know. Some are fire-related. Some are chemical. Some are about your water. All of them are worth five minutes of your time today.

Tip 01

Water on a Grease Fire Will Make It Worse — Use This Instead

Water and burning oil don't mix — they react. Throwing water on a kitchen grease fire causes a rapid steam explosion that sends burning oil in every direction. The correct response: slide a metal lid over the pan to cut off oxygen and smother the fire, turn off the burner, and don't touch it until it cools completely. A Class K fire extinguisher works too. Grease fires in the oven: close the door, turn the oven off. Never pull the pan off the burner and carry it anywhere while it's burning.

Tip 02

A Yellow Pilot Light Is a Carbon Monoxide Warning

On any gas appliance — furnace, water heater, range — the pilot light should burn blue. A yellow, orange, or flickering pilot light indicates incomplete combustion, which means the appliance is likely producing carbon monoxide instead of burning it off cleanly. Open the windows, leave the house, call your gas utility, and get a licensed HVAC technician in before you restart the appliance. In NJ, natural gas utilities (PSE&G, NJ Natural Gas, Elizabethtown Gas) all have emergency lines for this exact situation.

Tip 03

If Your Tap Water Smells Like Rotten Eggs, Stop Drinking It

A sulfur or rotten egg smell in household water typically signals hydrogen sulfide gas or sulfur bacteria — both of which are concerning at elevated levels. In NJ, this is most common in homes with private wells, particularly in Morris and Middlesex counties where certain aquifer compositions can produce it. Don't drink the water until it's tested. NJ DEP has a private well testing program, and most county health departments can direct you to certified labs. Municipal water showing this smell is unusual and should be reported to your water utility immediately.

Tip 04

Some Cleaning Products Create Toxic Gas When Mixed

Bleach is in a lot of NJ homes. So is ammonia-based glass cleaner. So is vinegar. Mixing bleach with ammonia produces chloramine gas — a respiratory irritant that can cause serious lung damage. Mixing bleach with vinegar produces chlorine gas. Mixing bleach with rubbing alcohol produces chloroform. These combinations happen when people use two products in the same space without thinking about what's in each one. Read labels. Store them separately. Never use them simultaneously in a closed bathroom or kitchen without opening a window.

Tip 05

Close Your Bedroom Door Before You Sleep

FEMA data shows 51% of fatal house fires occur between 10pm and 6am. The Fire Safety Research Institute found that a closed door creates nearly a 900°F temperature difference between a burning room and the space behind the door. In live fire testing, open-door rooms reached approximately 1,000°F; closed-door rooms stayed around 100°F. Two seconds, no cost, potentially life-saving. Full breakdown here.

Tip 06

Your GFCI Outlets Need to Be Tested — They Fail Over Time

GFCI (Ground Fault Circuit Interrupter) outlets are the ones with the Test and Reset buttons, found near sinks, bathrooms, garages, and outdoor areas. They're designed to cut power in milliseconds if they detect a ground fault — which is what prevents electrocution near water. The problem: GFCI outlets fail silently. They stop tripping the circuit, but continue to pass power — giving you the false impression that protection is active. NJ code requires them in wet areas of homes. Test each one monthly by pressing the Test button (power should cut) and then Reset (power should restore). Replace any that don't respond correctly.

Tip 07

Dryer Lint Is a Fire Hazard — and Your Vent Duct Is Worse

Dryer fires are among the most common appliance fires in NJ homes. Most people know to clean the lint trap — but the bigger risk is the duct that vents hot air to the outside of the house. Lint accumulates in that duct over years and becomes a fire-waiting-to-happen, especially in longer or kinked duct runs common in older NJ ranch and split-level homes. Have the duct professionally cleaned every 1 to 2 years. Signs it's overdue: clothes taking longer to dry, the dryer running hotter than usual, or the exterior vent flap not opening fully when the dryer is running.

Tip 08

Test Your Garage Door Auto-Reverse

Every NJ home with an automatic garage door opener built after 1993 is required to have auto-reverse — the mechanism that stops and reverses the door if it contacts an obstruction while closing. This mechanism saves lives, particularly for children and pets. The problem: it can fail without any visible indication. Test it monthly by placing a 2x4 flat on the floor in the door's path. When the door contacts it, it should immediately reverse. If it doesn't, the auto-reverse mechanism is not functioning and needs service before the door is used again.

Tip 09

Annual Exterior Caulk Walk — Wood Rot Prevention for NJ Homes

NJ's freeze-thaw cycles destroy exterior caulk. When it fails — and it does, every year — water gets into wall assemblies, causes wood rot, and eventually mold and structural damage. Walk around your home once a year, check every joint where two materials meet, and re-caulk any failed sections. A tube of caulk costs $10. Ignoring the caulk costs $2,000 to $10,000 in repairs — and it shows up on buyer inspection reports as a negotiating point when you sell. Full caulk audit checklist here.

Tip 10

Know Where Your Gas Shutoff and Water Main Are — Before an Emergency

In an emergency, every second counts. Know where your gas shutoff is before you need it — typically near the gas meter on the exterior of your NJ home. Know where your main water shutoff is — typically in the basement near the front of the house or in a utility closet. If a pipe bursts or you smell gas, you need to act immediately, not search. Locate both now, make sure you can access them, and show every adult in your household where they are. Keep a gas shutoff wrench on a hook near the meter.

NJ-specific note: If you smell gas in your NJ home, do not operate any electrical switches, do not use your phone inside the house, and do not try to find the leak yourself. Leave immediately, move away from the building, and call PSE&G at 1-800-436-7734, NJ Natural Gas at 1-800-221-0051, or 911. These utilities have 24-hour emergency response.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should you do if you have a grease fire in your NJ kitchen?

Never use water — it causes the burning oil to explode. Slide a metal lid over the pan to smother the fire, turn off the burner, and leave the lid in place until fully cooled. Use a Class K extinguisher if available. If the fire is in the oven, close the oven door and turn it off. If it spreads beyond the pan, get out and call 911.

What does a yellow pilot light mean on a gas appliance in my NJ home?

A normal pilot light burns blue. Yellow or orange indicates incomplete combustion and possible carbon monoxide production. Ventilate the area, turn off the appliance, leave the house, and call your gas utility and a licensed HVAC technician. Do not restart the appliance until it's been serviced.

Which household cleaners should never be mixed?

Bleach and ammonia produce chloramine gas. Bleach and vinegar produce chlorine gas. Bleach and rubbing alcohol produce chloroform. Store these separately and never use them simultaneously in enclosed spaces without ventilation.

What does a rotten egg smell in my NJ home's water mean?

Typically hydrogen sulfide gas or sulfur bacteria — both concerning. Stop drinking the water until tested. In NJ, contact your water utility for municipal water or the NJ DEP's private well testing program for well water. Most county health departments can direct you to certified testing labs.

Do these home safety issues affect NJ home values?

Yes. Water quality issues, GFCI failures, CO detector placement, and dryer vent conditions all appear on buyer inspection reports and generate credit requests. Addressing safety issues proactively — before listing — prevents them from becoming negotiating leverage for buyers. It also protects your family while you own the home.
