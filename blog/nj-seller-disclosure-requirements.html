Is a Seller’s Disclosure Required in New Jersey? What You Actually Have to Tell Buyers

Updated July 2026 · By Jorge Ramirez, Keller Williams Premier Properties

Yes — New Jersey sellers have a duty to disclose known material latent defects. That means problems you are aware of, that a buyer could not reasonably discover for themselves, and that affect the value or safety of the property. The form most people mean when they say “seller’s disclosure” is the Seller’s Property Condition Disclosure Statement, which is standard practice here and called for by the customary New Jersey contract. The duty and the form are two different things, and the duty is the part that survives regardless.

The Distinction That Trips People Up

Sellers ask whether the form is mandatory, hoping the answer means they can skip a conversation. It does not work that way.

The obligation to disclose known material latent defects comes from New Jersey law and does not depend on whether anyone hands you a form. The disclosure statement is simply the instrument the industry uses to satisfy it in an organised way. Declining to complete one does not remove the duty — it removes the record that you met it, which is the opposite of what a seller wants if a dispute arises later.

In practice, the standard New Jersey contract of sale calls for the statement, so nearly every listed transaction includes one. A seller who refuses tends to create suspicion rather than avoid exposure.

“Known” and “Latent” Are Doing the Work

Both words limit the obligation, and understanding them is what keeps sellers out of trouble without over-disclosing into a discount.

  • Known means what you are actually aware of. You are not required to investigate your own house or hire experts to find problems you do not know about. You are required not to conceal what you do know.
  • Latent means hidden — something a buyer could not reasonably discover through an ordinary inspection. Peeling paint on a visible wall is not latent. A basement that floods every spring, currently dry in October, is.
  • Material means it matters to value or safety. You do not owe a buyer an inventory of every cosmetic imperfection.

The classic problem case is the recurring condition. A finished basement that has taken water three times in twelve years looks perfectly sound on a dry inspection day. That is precisely the kind of thing the duty exists to cover.

Underground Oil Tanks: The Highest-Stakes Item in This Market

Of everything on a New Jersey disclosure, an underground heating oil storage tank carries the most consequence — because if it has leaked, you are no longer discussing a repair. You are discussing environmental remediation, and buyers, their attorneys and their lenders all know it.

You can absolutely sell a house in New Jersey with an underground oil tank. What you cannot do is let it surface late. Handled openly, it is a documented condition with a known cost. Discovered at inspection after going unmentioned, it becomes both a renegotiation and a credibility problem, and the second one costs more.

What keeps these deals together is documentation: a clean tank certification, or evidence that a tank was removed or abandoned properly with the paperwork to prove it. A clean certification matters particularly in Union and Essex County homes with older heating systems, where buyers and their attorneys have seen enough tank issues to ask about it first.

If you do not know whether the property has one — and plenty of owners of older homes genuinely do not — find out before you list rather than during attorney review.

What Happens If You Leave Something Off

Concealing a known material defect can expose a seller to liability after closing. The exposure does not end when the deed is recorded, which is the part sellers tend to assume incorrectly.

The practical logic runs opposite to instinct. Sellers hide problems because they fear losing money on them, but a disclosed problem gets priced once, at a number you can negotiate. An undisclosed problem discovered at inspection gets priced as leverage — and a buyer who has just learned you knew and did not say tends to be a great deal less generous about everything else in the deal.

What You Generally Do Not Have to Disclose

  • Non-physical matters. The duty concerns the physical condition of the property. Events that occurred in a home, such as a death, are typically outside it.
  • Things you genuinely do not know. The standard is knowledge, not omniscience. You are not obliged to investigate.
  • Obvious, visible condition. A buyer inspecting the property is expected to see what is there to be seen.

Where sellers get into difficulty is rarely the exotic case. It is the ordinary one they decided was not worth mentioning.

Fix It, or Disclose It?

Usually both, in that order. Disclosing an unrepaired defect invites the buyer to price it, and buyers price problems higher than contractors do — often several times higher, because they are pricing uncertainty rather than the actual repair.

Repairing the item first and disclosing the repair with receipts converts an open-ended risk into a closed one. That is generally worth more to your net than the money you saved by not fixing it. Common Questions About NJ Seller Disclosure

Is a seller's disclosure required in NJ?

New Jersey sellers have a legal duty to disclose known material latent defects — problems they are aware of that a buyer could not reasonably discover on inspection and that affect the value or safety of the property. Separately, the Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Statement is standard practice in New Jersey and is called for by the customary contract of sale, so in practice nearly every listed sale involves one. Ask your attorney how the obligation applies to your specific sale.

Is the NJ seller's disclosure form mandatory?

The distinction that matters is between the form and the duty. The underlying duty to disclose known material latent defects comes from New Jersey law and does not depend on any form. The disclosure statement itself is the customary instrument used to satisfy it, and the standard New Jersey contract calls for it — so declining to complete one is unusual and tends to raise questions rather than avoid them.

Can you sell a house in New Jersey with an underground oil tank?

Yes, but it needs to be handled openly and early. An underground heating oil tank is the highest-stakes disclosure item in this market because a leak becomes an environmental remediation question rather than a repair. Buyers, their attorneys and their lenders will all want documentation. A clean tank certification, or evidence of a properly documented removal or abandonment, is what keeps the deal on track — particularly in Union and Essex County homes with older systems.

What happens if a NJ seller does not disclose a known defect?

Concealing a known material defect can expose a seller to liability after closing, and the exposure does not end at the closing table. That is why the practical advice runs opposite to instinct: disclosing a problem early usually costs less than having it discovered late, when it becomes leverage rather than a line item.

Do you have to disclose a death in the house in NJ?

Generally no. The disclosure duty concerns physical conditions of the property, not events that occurred in it. Non-physical matters such as a death in the home are typically outside it. If you have any doubt about a specific circumstance, that is a question for your closing attorney rather than something to guess at.

Should I fix a defect before listing or just disclose it?

Often both, and in that order. Disclosing an unrepaired defect invites the buyer to price it, and buyers almost always price a problem higher than it costs to fix. Repairing it first and disclosing the repair with documentation usually protects more of your proceeds than a credit negotiated after an inspection.

Not Sure Whether Something Needs Disclosing?

Most sellers who worry about this are worrying about one specific thing. Naming it out loud early usually turns out to be far less expensive than it feels, and there is generally a way to handle it that does not cost you the sale.
