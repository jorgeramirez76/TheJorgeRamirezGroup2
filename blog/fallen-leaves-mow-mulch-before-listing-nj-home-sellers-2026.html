Mow, Don’t Bag: What to Do With Fallen Leaves Before Listing a NJ Home This Fall

House Outlook’s advice this week is to put the leaf blower away and get the mower out: run over the leaves until the pieces are about the size of a dime and let them feed the lawn. I agree, and I would add that a seller has a different calendar than a gardener. A listing in Westfield or Chatham in late October drops leaves every day for six weeks, and every showing and every photo happens somewhere inside that window. Here is the routine I give sellers.

The House Outlook method

The argument is straightforward. Whole leaves left on grass form a mat that blocks light and air, and the lawn under it comes back thin in spring. Shredded leaves do the opposite: mow over them until the pieces are roughly dime-sized, and the grass grows up through them while they break down into compost over the winter. For a small pile, the article suggests a string trimmer inside a trash can; for a yard under big oaks and maples, a standalone shredder. The shredded leaves also work as mulch on perennial beds and around fall plantings.

What leaves do to a listing

Buyers decide a lot from the curb, and a lawn under a solid blanket of leaves reads as a house that has stopped being cared for, whatever the inside looks like. Leaves also hide the things buyers trip on and inspectors look for: the lifted flagstone on the walk, the wet front steps, the downspout that discharges against the foundation. Piles against the siding hold moisture and give mice a place to live until the house gets cold enough to move indoors. And wet leaves in the gutters of a Scotch Plains split or a Maplewood colonial are the start of the ice-dam story the buyer’s inspector tells in January.

The seller’s leaf routine

  1. Mow weekly through the drop, mulching blade on, bag off. Two passes at right angles if the pieces are still big. The lawn looks cut and the leaves disappear into it.
  2. Clear the walk, steps, porch and driveway before every showing. A broom, not a blower, ten minutes before the appointment. Wet leaves on steps are a liability and a bad first impression in the same moment.
  3. Keep leaves off the foundation and out of window wells. Rake a clean band around the house and check the wells, where leaves hold water against the basement windows.
  4. Clean the gutters and downspouts after the main drop, then once more before the first freeze. If you are considering guards, read the gutter guard guide first.
  5. Use shredded leaves in the beds, not whole leaves against the siding. Two to three inches over the perennials and around shrubs, pulled back from stems and from the house.
  6. Follow your town’s collection rules exactly. Some towns want leaves bagged, some take loose piles at the curb on a schedule, and most will fine you for leaves in the street outside the pickup window. Never put them near a storm drain; New Jersey DEP has asked that of homeowners for years.
  7. Schedule the exterior photos the day after a cleanup, and ask the photographer to reshoot the front if the first set was taken under a carpet of leaves. The front photo is the one every buyer sees first.

Jorge’s tip: A clean lawn with a few scattered leaves photographs better in October than a scalped, blown-bare yard, because it looks like fall instead of a cleanup. Mow, clear the hard surfaces, and stop. Buyers in Summit and Millburn are not grading your yard; they are deciding whether the house looks loved.

Trees, leaves and the questions buyers ask

A yard that drops this many leaves has big trees, and big trees get questions. Buyers in Montclair and Morristown ask how close the oak is to the foundation, whether the roof has been patched where a limb came down, and when the gutters were last cleaned. Have an arborist’s report or an invoice if there is one, and if a tree is dead or leaning, deal with it before listing rather than negotiating it in attorney review. A stump in the front lawn is its own conversation, covered in the stump removal guide.

For buyers: read the yard under the leaves

Visit after a rain and look at where the water goes. Leaves collect in the low spots, so a soggy pile against the house in Cranford or Edison is telling you about grading, not about raking. Ask the seller how the leaves are usually handled, which tells you whether the gutters were cleaned, and check the basement corners nearest the biggest trees for stains.

Questions about leaves, lawns and fall listings

Is it really better to mow leaves than bag them?

For the lawn, yes. House Outlook’s point is that whole leaves form a mat that smothers grass, while dime-sized shredded pieces break down into compost over winter. For a listing it also saves the weekly bagging and keeps the lawn looking cut between showings.

Can I just leave the leaves until the house sells?

No. A lawn under a solid layer of leaves reads as neglect in listing photos and at showings, hides trip hazards on the walk, and holds moisture against the foundation. Mow weekly and clear the hard surfaces before every appointment.

What are the rules for leaves at the curb in New Jersey?

They vary by town. Some require bags, some collect loose piles on a published schedule, and most prohibit leaves in the street outside the pickup window or near storm drains. Check your municipality’s current notice and follow it; a fine or a warning letter during a listing is an avoidable distraction.

Should I clean the gutters before listing?

Yes, after the main leaf drop and again before the first freeze. Buyers’ inspectors look at gutters and downspouts closely, and clogged gutters in fall are the start of ice dams and foundation water problems a buyer will ask about.

Does a yard full of big trees hurt my sale?

Mature trees are usually a plus in New Jersey towns, but buyers will ask about limbs over the roof, roots near the foundation and the condition of any tree that looks sick. Have records of tree work ready and deal with a dead or leaning tree before listing rather than during negotiations.
