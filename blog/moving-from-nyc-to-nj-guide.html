Moving From NYC to NJ: Logistics, Deadlines, and Towns

Moving from NYC to New Jersey is two jobs at once. First the logistics: reserve your building's service elevator, get your mover's certificate of insurance approved, and plan the truck around NYC curb rules. Then, once you land, New Jersey gives you 60 days to transfer your license and registration.

What does my NYC building need before I can move out?

Two things, and both run through the managing agent rather than the front desk: a reserved service-elevator window on an approved move day, and a certificate of insurance from your moving company that names exactly the entities the building specifies. Neither is optional in most New York City co-ops, condos, and larger rental buildings, and a rejected certificate is the single most common reason a move date slips.

The certificate of insurance — everyone calls it the COI — is a document your mover's insurer issues on request. It lists the coverage the mover carries and names the building's ownership entity, the managing agent, and often the board as additional insureds. Buildings reject COIs constantly, almost always for a boring reason: the legal entity name is one word off, or the liability limits are below what the building's rules require. That is why the order of operations matters.

Do it in this sequence:

  1. Email the managing agent first. Ask, in one message, for the building's move-out rules in writing: which days and hours moves are permitted, how far in advance the elevator has to be reserved, the exact entity names and coverage limits the COI must show, whether a refundable move-out deposit or fee applies, and whether the building requires floor and wall protection.
  2. Then book the mover, and send them the building's requirements verbatim rather than paraphrasing them.
  3. Have the COI issued and submitted for approval with enough runway that a rejection can be corrected. Get written confirmation that the building accepted it.
  4. Only then lock the elevator slot. In buildings with one service elevator, the popular dates go early — end of month, and the last weekday before a lease turns over.

One more check worth two minutes: because a move from New York City to New Jersey crosses a state line, it is an interstate move, and the carrier has to be registered with the federal government and carry a USDOT number. The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration runs a public mover search where you can confirm a company is registered, is insured, and is actually authorized to transport household goods — registered and authorized are not the same thing. Do that before you pay a deposit.

Can I reserve street parking for the moving truck in NYC?

No. New York City DOT does not issue permits that hold curb space for a moving truck. In its Truck Driver FAQ, NYC DOT states plainly: "Currently, there are no permits for reserving curb space for temporary on-street parking of commercial vehicles for general pick-up and delivery purposes through NYC DOT."

That single fact reshapes move day, because two other city rules apply at the same time. On parking duration, NYC DOT states: "When parking is not otherwise restricted, no person shall park a commercial vehicle in any area, including a residential area, in excess of three hours." And on staging the truck the night before, it states: "Overnight residential street parking for trucks is prohibited in NYC," specifically that no commercial vehicle may park on a residential street between 9 pm and 5 am. (Source: NYC DOT Truck Driver FAQ.)

What that means in practice:

  • Move day is a single daylight operation. The truck cannot sit outside your building overnight waiting for a morning start, and it cannot idle at the curb all afternoon while you finish packing. Be packed before the truck arrives, not while it is there.
  • Ask the building whether there is a loading dock or a service entrance before you assume the truck will be at the front door. In many buildings the dock is the whole answer to the curb problem.
  • Walk the block for your actual move date. Check alternate-side street cleaning, No Standing zones, bus stops, hydrants, and construction sheds. A block that is clear on a Tuesday afternoon can be unusable at 8 a.m.
  • If the street is genuinely impossible, that is a conversation to have with the mover during the estimate, not on the morning of. Shuttle vans and longer carries change the price, and you want that priced in advance rather than added as a surprise on the day.

How long do I have to get a New Jersey license and registration?

Sixty days — or before your current license and registration expire, whichever comes first. The New Jersey Motor Vehicle Commission states: "you must transfer your out-of-state license and vehicle title/registration within 60 days of your move or before your current license and vehicle registration expires, whichever occurs first."

The vehicle side carries the same clock. NJ MVC states: "Under New Jersey law, you must transfer your vehicle title and registration (with or without a lien) within 60 days." Note that this is title and registration — if there is a lienholder on the car, the title transfer involves your lender, and that adds mail time you should not discover in week eight. (Source: NJ MVC, Moving to New Jersey.)

Two things surprise people coming from New York City. The first is that many NYC transplants have never held a car registration at all, so the 60-day clock is the first time they deal with titles, liens, and insurance in one appointment. The second is that New Jersey uses a points-based identity standard — the "6 Points of ID" system — so you cannot walk in with a single document and expect to leave with a license.

Before your appointment, pull the current document checklist and fee schedule straight from nj.gov/mvc and confirm what New Jersey requires for insurance on the vehicle you are registering. Requirements and fees change, appointments are scheduled in advance, and this is exactly the kind of thing you should verify with the agency rather than take from any website — including this one.

What changes on my taxes when I move from NY to NJ?

Where you file changes, and one layer of tax genuinely goes away — but if you keep working in New York, New York generally still taxes those wages. The mechanism matters more than any number, so here it is without rates, because rates and thresholds change and you should get yours from your CPA.

There is no NY–NJ reciprocity

New Jersey's reciprocal personal income tax agreement is with Pennsylvania. New York is not part of it. New Jersey's Division of Taxation notes that New Jersey residents employed in Pennsylvania cannot claim a credit for taxes paid to Pennsylvania on those earnings precisely because of that reciprocal agreement — which tells you what reciprocity does, and that New York does not get it. So the "I moved, so I only pay one state now" assumption is the wrong model for a New York commuter.

You file in both states, and New Jersey credits you

The standard pattern for a New Jersey resident earning New York-source wages is two returns: a New York nonresident return on the New York-source income, and a New Jersey resident return on everything. New Jersey then reduces the double-count with its credit for taxes paid to other jurisdictions. The Division of Taxation puts it this way: "You may qualify for a credit if you paid income or wage tax on the same income in the same year to both New Jersey and to another jurisdiction outside New Jersey." The credit is claimed on Schedule NJ-COJ, and it is capped — "Your credit cannot be more than the amount you would have paid if you earned the income in New Jersey." (Source: NJ Division of Taxation, Credit for Taxes Paid to Other Jurisdictions.)

The New York City resident tax stops applying

This is the layer that actually disappears, and it is the one most people are thinking of when they say the tax math works. The New York State Department of Taxation and Finance states: "Nonresidents of New York City are not liable for New York City personal income tax." Once you are no longer a New York City resident, that city-level personal income tax is not yours. (Source: NY Department of Taxation and Finance, Nonresident FAQs.)

Two things to get right in the move year

First, the year you move you will most likely file part-year in both states, which is its own set of forms. Second — and this is the one that costs people money — residency for tax purposes is a separate legal test from the MVC's 60-day clock. It turns on domicile and day counts, not on when you swapped your license. Tell your employer's payroll team the date your residency changes so withholding follows you, and have a CPA who handles NY/NJ commuters look at your first year. Confirm anything specific with the NJ Division of Taxation and the NY Department of Taxation and Finance directly.

In what order do I switch utilities, mail, and voter registration?

Utilities first, because they gate your closing and your first night in the house. Mail second. Voter registration third, on the same trip as your MVC appointment. Running them in that order avoids the two classic failures: a dark house on move-in day, and a registration you miss by a week.

1. Utilities — start before closing, not after

Schedule the New Jersey accounts to open on the day you take possession, and schedule the New York City accounts to close the day after you hand back the keys. An overlap day costs very little; a gap means no lights while movers are carrying boxes up the stairs. Two New Jersey specifics catch NYC people off guard: in many towns water and sewer are municipal, billed by the town or a local authority rather than bundled with electric, so that is a separate account to open. And your closing may depend on municipal inspections that get scheduled weeks out, not days — ask your real estate attorney early which inspections and certificates your specific town requires before the closing date, because that answer varies town to town.

2. Mail — file the change of address, then fix the leaks

File the USPS change of address. Standard forwarding covers First-Class Mail for 12 months; periodicals forward for only 60 days, so magazines and newspapers need their addresses updated at the source (USPS, Standard Forward Mail). Forwarding is a safety net, not a system. Work through the list that actually matters: employer payroll, bank and brokerage, auto and health insurance, the IRS, your mortgage servicer, and anything with a recurring charge.

3. Voter registration — and why it is not just civic housekeeping

The New Jersey Division of Elections states that "The registration deadline to vote in the next election is 21 days prior to the election day," and that to be eligible you must be "a resident of the county for 30 days before the election" (NJ Division of Elections). Two deadlines, not one — the 30-day county residency is what makes you eligible, and the 21-day cutoff is when the paperwork has to be in.

Do it the same week as your MVC appointment. A driver license and a voter registration are among the ordinary records used to show where a person actually lives, so leaving one of them pointing at a New York address for a year is the kind of loose end that is easy to close now and annoying to explain later.

Why People Leave NYC for New Jersey

The short answer: space, schools, and total cost. A monthly budget that rents an apartment in Manhattan buys a multi-bedroom house in a New Jersey commuter town, New Jersey residents are not liable for New York City personal income tax, and strong public school districts replace private-school tuition. Hybrid work then turned the train commute into a two-or-three-day problem instead of a five-day one.

The reasons have not changed much over the decades, but they have gotten more urgent since the pandemic and the rise of hybrid work. Here are the most common motivations Jorge hears from clients making the move.

Space — Real, Actual Space

For a comparable monthly cost, an NYC apartment becomes a house with a yard, a basement, an attic, and a garage. When you start working from home two or three days a week, space goes from "nice to have" to "non-negotiable" — the spare bedroom stops being a guest room and starts being an office.

Schools

New Jersey consistently ranks as one of the top three states in the nation for public education. Towns like Summit, Millburn, Chatham, and Westfield have nationally recognized school districts that rival the best private schools in New York City — and they are included in your property taxes. Many families find that even with high NJ property taxes, the total cost is less than private school tuition in Manhattan.

Taxes Can Actually Be Better

This surprises people. Yes, NJ property taxes are high. But New York City residents pay a city personal income tax on top of state tax, and New York State is explicit that "Nonresidents of New York City are not liable for New York City personal income tax." No New Jersey municipality levies an equivalent. When you add up state income tax, that city layer, property taxes, and total housing cost, many families find the New Jersey math works in their favor — but the answer is genuinely personal, so run your own numbers with an accountant rather than assuming New Jersey is more expensive because of the property tax headlines. The filing mechanics are covered in the NY-to-NJ tax section above, and every town's average bill is in Jorge's NJ property tax guide.

Quality of Life

This is harder to quantify, but it matters. A backyard for the kids. A driveway where you park your car without circling for 30 minutes. Weekend farmers' markets, youth sports leagues, community events, and neighbors you actually know by name. The pace of suburban NJ is slower than NYC, and for many people that is exactly the point.

Hybrid Work Changed the Equation

Before 2020, the daily commute was the deal-breaker for many people considering NJ. If you commute to Midtown five days a week, a 45-minute train ride adds up. But if you only go in two or three days, the math changes dramatically. Suddenly a town like Madison or Morristown — a little further out on the train line, with lower prices and more land — becomes a real option.

What Surprises NYC People About NJ

Three things surprise almost everyone: the property tax bill, the cars, and how much the town line matters. New Jersey's average residential property tax bill was $10,597 in 2025 per the state DCA figures, and popular commuter towns run far higher — Maplewood averages $19,378 and Summit $19,697. Most transplant families also end up owning two cars within a year.

Jorge has helped enough NYC transplants to know the common "surprises" that catch people off guard. Here is what you should expect.

Property Taxes Are Real

We already mentioned this, but it bears repeating: the property tax bill is an annual, permanent line item, and it varies enormously by town — not by a little. Two houses at the same price in two neighboring municipalities can carry very different bills, because each town sets its own rate and does its own assessment.

Do not work from ranges. Look up the actual average residential bill for each town on your shortlist in the NJ property tax guide, which uses the New Jersey Department of Community Affairs figures, and look up the bill on the specific property before you write an offer. These taxes fund the schools, roads, parks, and municipal services — think of them as prepaid private school tuition and doorman fees rolled into one — but they belong in your monthly math from the first showing, not at the closing table.

You Need a Car — Probably Two

If you move beyond Hoboken or Jersey City, you need a car. Groceries, kids' activities, restaurants on a Friday night — all require driving. Most NJ commuter families end up with two vehicles, and the full cost is not just the payment: insurance, fuel, maintenance, and parking at the train station all recur. Get quotes for New Jersey auto insurance before you finalize a budget, because the number can differ meaningfully from what you were paying — or not paying — in the city. The offset is that the daily car-service and rideshare spending largely disappears.

Town Identity Matters

In NYC, neighborhoods blend into each other. In NJ, each town is its own municipality with its own school district, its own taxes, its own police department, and its own personality. Summit feels different from Westfield, which feels different from Maplewood, which feels different from Montclair. You are not just buying a house — you are joining a community. Take the time to understand what each town is like before you commit.

The Pace Is Different

Stores close earlier. Restaurants are quieter on Tuesday nights. Your neighbors will wave at you. There is a learning curve, and some people take a few months to adjust. But most NYC transplants tell Jorge that within six months, they wonder why they did not make the move sooner.

The NJ Home Buying Process — How It Differs From NYC

The headline differences: in NJ both sides hire real estate attorneys, there is no board approval, and inspections carry real negotiating weight. Closings typically run about 45 to 60 days from accepted offer — faster than most NYC co-op deals. You will also sign an attorney-review clause and put down an earnest money deposit held in escrow.

If you have only ever bought a co-op or condo in New York City, the NJ process will feel different. Here are the key differences.

You Need a Real Estate Attorney

In NJ, both the buyer and the seller hire their own real estate attorney. This is standard practice and different from NYC, where title companies do more of the heavy lifting. Your attorney reviews the contract, handles due diligence, and represents your interests through closing. Most NJ real estate attorneys quote a flat fee for a residential purchase — ask for it in writing up front so it lands in your closing-cost estimate rather than as a surprise.

No Board Approval

Unless you are buying a condo with an HOA, there is no board to approve your purchase. No board package, no financial disclosure to strangers, no months-long approval process. You make an offer, it gets accepted, and you move forward.

Inspections Are Critical

In NJ, the home inspection is a major part of the process. You will hire a licensed home inspector who examines the roof, foundation, electrical, plumbing, HVAC, and everything in between. The inspection results give you leverage to negotiate repairs or credits. For NYC buyers who have never owned a detached house, this is where a local agent earns their keep — knowing which findings are deal-breakers, which are ordinary for a house of that age, and which are worth a credit rather than a repair.

Closing Timeline

From accepted offer to closing, expect 45 to 60 days. This includes attorney review (about 5 business days), inspections (within two weeks), mortgage commitment, and title search. It is faster than many NYC co-op transactions, which can take 3 to 4 months with board approval.

Earnest Money Deposit

You will put down an earnest money deposit when the contract is signed. The amount is negotiated as part of the offer, it is held in escrow rather than paid to the seller, and it is applied toward your purchase at closing. Your attorney will confirm the figure and the escrow terms before you sign.

Timeline: How Long Does the Move Actually Take?

Three to six months from first conversation to moving day. Months one to two are research, weekend town visits, and mortgage pre-approval; months two to three are the active search; once an offer is accepted, attorney review, inspections, and the mortgage process take another 45 to 60 days. Selling a NYC co-op or condo first adds two to three months.

Common Mistakes NYC-to-NJ Movers Make

The five mistakes that cost real money: chasing the lowest sticker price while ignoring taxes and commute costs, only visiting on weekends, underestimating door-to-desk time, touring without a pre-approval, and going unrepresented. In NJ the seller typically pays both agents' commissions, so skipping buyer representation saves you nothing and costs you local knowledge.

Jorge has seen these patterns repeatedly over the years. Avoid them and you will have a smoother transition.

Mistake 1: Choosing a Town Based Only on Price

A lower home price does not always mean lower total cost. Factor in property taxes, commute costs, and car expenses. A $700K home with $18,000 in annual taxes and a $400/month train pass may cost more than an $850K home with $14,000 in taxes and a shorter commute.

Mistake 2: Not Visiting on a Weekday

Towns look different on Saturday afternoon versus Tuesday at 6 PM. Visit your top choices during the work week. See the commute for yourself. Walk the downtown after work hours. That is when you will see what daily life actually looks like.

Mistake 3: Underestimating the Commute

Train schedules are not subways. If you miss the 7:42 express, the next one might be 25 minutes later and take 15 minutes longer. Factor in the drive to the station, parking, and the walk on the other end. The "38-minute express" is really a 60-to-70-minute door-to-desk experience.

Mistake 4: Not Getting Pre-Approved Before Looking

The NJ market moves fast in desirable towns. If you see a home you love on Saturday and need two days to get pre-approved, it may be under contract by Monday. Get your mortgage pre-approval before you start touring homes.

Mistake 5: Skipping the Real Estate Agent

In NJ, the seller typically pays both agents' commissions. There is no cost to you for buyer representation, and a good agent who knows the towns will save you from making expensive mistakes. Frequently Asked Questions: Moving From NYC to NJ

How long does it take to move from NYC to NJ?

The entire process typically takes 3 to 6 months from the time you start looking to closing day. If you are renting in NYC, you can often align your lease end date with your NJ closing. Buyers who need to sell a co-op or condo first should budget 4 to 8 months for the full transition. Jorge can help coordinate the timing to minimize overlap and stress.

Are NJ property taxes really that high?

Yes, NJ property taxes are among the highest in the country. Annual taxes range from $10,000 to $35,000+ depending on the town and home value. However, when you factor in the elimination of NYC city income tax, free public schools that rival private school quality, and lower overall housing costs per square foot, many families come out ahead financially. An accountant can run the specific numbers for your situation.

Do I need a car if I move from NYC to NJ?

In most NJ suburbs, yes. Hoboken and Jersey City are exceptions — they are walkable and transit-friendly. Once you move into suburban communities like Summit, Westfield, or Chatham, a car is essential. Most families own two vehicles. Budget accordingly.

What are the best NJ towns for NYC commuters?

It depends on where you work. For Midtown, the Midtown Direct NJ Transit line offers express service from Summit (38 min), Maplewood (35 min), Chatham (42 min), and Madison (48 min). In NJ, both buyers and sellers hire real estate attorneys — this is standard. There are no co-op board approvals. Should I rent first or buy immediately when moving from NYC to NJ?

Renting for 6 to 12 months lets you test a town before committing, but in a competitive market, desirable homes move fast and prices can rise. Many of Jorge's clients do thorough research — visiting towns on weekends, talking to residents, and walking the streets — then buy directly without renting first.
