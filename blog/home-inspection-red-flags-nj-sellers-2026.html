Why Inspections Kill Deals — and How to Stop It

In my experience selling homes across NJ, the most common reason deals fall apart after going under contract is not financing. It is not the appraisal. It is the home inspection. Specifically, it is inspection issues the seller did not know about, did not disclose, and did not address before listing — and which the buyer's inspector then found in detail, in writing, in a 50-page report with photos.

When that report lands, you have a problem. A buyer who was emotionally committed to the house reads a laundry list of issues and starts doing math they were not doing a week ago. Their attorney advises them to negotiate. Their agent tells them to request credits. Some of them walk. All of them recalibrate the deal in ways that favor the buyer, not the seller.

The solution is straightforward: know what is in your home before you list it, and deal with the serious issues on your terms — not under contract pressure with a closing date looming.

NJ context: Most NJ homes were built between 1940 and 1990. That means the housing stock in Union, Essex, Morris, and Middlesex counties has age-related issues that show up in virtually every inspection. Some are cosmetic. Some are serious. Knowing the difference before you list is worth more than any amount of staging.

The Red Flags That Routinely Blow Up NJ Deals

1. Basement Water Intrusion

Deal impact: Buyer walk or $5,000–$30,000+ credit request

Water in a basement is the single most common deal-killer I see in NJ. Every buyer in the $500K–$1.5M range has heard about basement flooding in NJ — because it is common. Our clay-heavy soils, older drainage systems, and aggressive storm events mean a huge percentage of NJ homes have had water in the basement at some point.

The problem is not always visible. Inspectors use moisture meters and look for staining patterns, efflorescence (white mineral deposits), and past water marks. If they find evidence of water intrusion — even old, dried-up evidence — they flag it. And buyers read those flags as "ongoing problem" regardless of when it actually happened.

What to do: If you have had water in your basement, address it before listing. A professional waterproofing assessment ($0–$200 for the eval) will tell you whether you need interior drainage, exterior grading correction, window well covers, or a sump pump. Fixing an active water problem before listing removes the buyer's most powerful negotiating lever.

2. Federal Pacific and Zinsco Electrical Panels

Deal impact: Lender refusal or $3,000–$6,000 replacement request

NJ has a high concentration of Federal Pacific Electric (Stab-Lok) and Zinsco electrical panels because of the age of our housing stock. These panels were installed from the 1950s through the early 1980s and are documented fire hazards due to breakers that fail to trip under overload conditions. Every licensed home inspector flags them. Many lenders — particularly FHA and VA loan programs — will not issue a mortgage on a home with these panels without a replacement.

If your home was built before 1985 and still has the original electrical panel, have an electrician look at it before you list. If it is a Federal Pacific or Zinsco, budget for replacement and get a quote. Panel replacements in NJ run $3,000–$6,000 depending on amperage and permitting complexity. That is a lot less than a buyer walking after inspections.

3. Roof Condition and Age

Deal impact: $5,000–$20,000 credit request or buyer exit

A roof near end of life — typically 20–25 years for asphalt shingles common in NJ — will get flagged in every inspection. Inspectors note missing shingles, granule loss, flashing issues, and approximate age. When they estimate "5–7 years of remaining life," buyers do math on when they will have to replace the roof and how much it will cost ($15,000–$25,000 for a standard NJ home). That math goes directly into their offer revision or credit request.

What to do: Know how old your roof is. If it is over 18 years, get a roofing company to evaluate it before you list. You may be able to repair specific issues (flashing, a few shingles) for a few hundred dollars rather than doing a full replacement. Or you may decide to price the replacement into the sale and disclose it upfront. What you should not do is hope the inspector does not notice.

4. HVAC Systems That Have Not Been Serviced

Deal impact: $2,000–$15,000 credit request, buyer concern

A furnace or central air unit that is 15+ years old and has no service record is a red flag in every inspection. Inspectors note age, run a functional test, and flag anything that is not operating properly. An aging, un-serviced HVAC system raises two questions in a buyer's mind: when is it going to fail, and how much is it going to cost to replace? Both questions end in price reductions or credits.

What to do: Have your HVAC serviced before the listing goes live. A $150 tune-up gets you a service record that demonstrates you maintain the home. If the system is over 20 years old, consider getting a replacement quote proactively so you can price accordingly or offer a credit with a defined dollar amount — controlled negotiation is better than surprised negotiation.

5. Underground Oil Tanks (NJ-Specific)

Deal impact: Deal killer if undisclosed, $5,000–$100,000+ remediation if contaminated

This is a New Jersey-specific issue. A significant number of NJ homes built before 1980 were heated by oil, and many have underground storage tanks (USTs) that were decommissioned in place when the home converted to gas. Buyers of pre-1980 NJ homes almost universally request an oil tank sweep as part of due diligence. If an undisclosed tank is found, it is a major problem — both legally and practically.

If you know a tank exists or suspect one, have it swept before listing. If it tests clean and is properly decommissioned, you can disclose that upfront and move forward. If it shows contamination, you are better off knowing and addressing it on your timeline than having a buyer find it mid-transaction.

6. Bathroom and Kitchen Moisture Issues

Deal impact: Buyer concern, $1,000–$8,000 credit request

Inspectors spend a lot of time in bathrooms and under kitchen sinks. Failed caulk around tubs and showers, missing grout, soft floors near toilets, water staining under the sink — all of these indicate moisture problems, whether they are active or historical. Buyers have been trained to worry about mold and water damage, and even minor bathroom issues can send them to a worst-case mental scenario.

What to do: Walk every bathroom and the kitchen with a critical eye. Re-caulk any tub or shower surround where caulk is cracked, discolored, or separating. Replace failed grout. Check under every sink for staining or soft cabinet floors. These are $50–$300 fixes that remove potentially costly inspection flags.

7. Improper Bathroom Ventilation and Mold

Deal impact: $2,000–$15,000 remediation request

Bathrooms without functioning exhaust fans — or with fans that vent into the attic rather than to the exterior — create moisture accumulation that leads to mold. In NJ homes with older construction, this is one of the most common inspection findings. Inspectors check fan function and, if they see staining or moisture damage, they flag it clearly. "Evidence of mold" in an inspection report sends buyers to mold remediators and attorneys faster than almost anything else.

If your bathrooms do not have exterior-vented exhaust fans, add them before listing. A licensed electrician can install a proper fan in a few hours. That is a much better outcome than a mold investigation mid-transaction.

What Sellers Should Do Before Listing: The Pre-Inspection Advantage

The single best thing an NJ seller can do before listing is hire their own inspector. Not to hide anything — to know what is in the home before a buyer's inspector finds it and uses it as leverage.

A pre-listing inspection runs $400–$600. It tells you exactly what a buyer's inspector will find. You then have three choices with each issue: fix it proactively, price it into the sale and disclose it upfront, or decide it is cosmetic enough that you will address it at the buyer's request. All three of those are controlled decisions you make on your timeline. The alternative — finding out during a buyer's inspection under contract — puts you in a reactive position where the buyer has all the leverage.

In my experience, sellers who do pre-listing inspections sell faster and with fewer post-inspection renegotiations. They also spend less on credits because they controlled the narrative around existing issues rather than letting a 50-page inspection report frame the conversation.

Frequently Asked Questions: Home Inspections for NJ Sellers

What are the most common home inspection failures in New Jersey?

In NJ homes — especially older stock in Union, Essex, and Morris counties — the most common inspection issues are: water intrusion in basements or crawl spaces, aging electrical panels (especially Federal Pacific Stab-Lok and Zinsco panels), roof condition issues, HVAC systems over 15 years old that have not been serviced, and improper bathroom ventilation causing mold or moisture. Underground oil tanks are a NJ-specific issue that requires a separate sweep if the home was built before 1990.

Should I do a pre-listing inspection before selling my NJ home?

In most cases, yes. A pre-listing inspection costs $400–$600 and tells you exactly what a buyer's inspector will find. You can then choose to fix issues proactively, price them into the sale, or disclose them upfront. The advantage is control: you decide how to handle issues on your timeline rather than under contract pressure. Sellers who go in blind often end up negotiating price reductions or repair credits larger than the inspection cost would have been.

What is a Federal Pacific electrical panel and why does it matter for NJ sellers?

Federal Pacific Electric (FPE) Stab-Lok panels were installed in millions of US homes between the 1950s and 1980s, and NJ has a high concentration of them. These panels have documented failure rates and are flagged by virtually every home inspector. Buyers and their lenders often require replacement before closing. A panel replacement runs $3,000–$5,000 in NJ. Sellers with FPE panels should either replace it before listing or price accordingly and be prepared to negotiate.

Do I have to disclose a buried oil tank in NJ?

Yes. New Jersey requires sellers to disclose known underground storage tanks. Buyers routinely request oil tank sweeps on pre-1990 NJ homes. If an undisclosed tank is found, it creates significant liability for the seller and can blow up a deal entirely. If you know or suspect a tank exists on your property, have it swept and addressed before listing.

What repairs give NJ sellers the best return before listing?

The highest-ROI pre-listing repairs for NJ sellers: waterproofing or addressing basement moisture (prevents buyers from walking), HVAC service (inexpensive but removes a common inspection flag), fixing any active leaks in roof or plumbing, and replacing failed caulk and grout in bathrooms and kitchen. These items come up in nearly every inspection report and buyers use them as leverage. Fixing them proactively removes that leverage entirely.
