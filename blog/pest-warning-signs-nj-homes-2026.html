6 Pest Warning Signs in NJ Homes That Sellers Miss — And Buyers Find at Inspection

Termites, rodents, and carpenter ants don't announce themselves. They leave clues. Here's what to look for before your buyer's inspector does.

Why Pest Problems Are a Real Estate Problem

In 15 years of selling homes across Union, Essex, Morris, and Middlesex Counties, I've watched pest problems derail deals that should have closed cleanly. Not because the pest issue was catastrophic — often it wasn't — but because the sellers had no idea it was there, the buyers' inspector found it, and suddenly we're renegotiating the deal under contract pressure.

That's the worst position to negotiate from. The buyer has leverage. The seller is backed into a corner. And the number that comes out of that conversation is almost always more than the seller would have spent fixing it proactively.

NJ's older housing stock — and I'm talking about the Colonials, Tudors, and Cape Cods built between 1920 and 1980 that make up most of the inventory in Maplewood, Chatham, Montclair, Summit, and Westfield — is genuinely susceptible to pest pressure. Mature tree cover. Older framing. Basements and crawl spaces that weren't designed with modern moisture standards. The conditions are perfect for termites and carpenter ants.

Here are the six warning signs I tell every seller to check before they call me to list their house.

1 Hollow-Sounding Wood Near the Foundation

Take a screwdriver and tap along your basement sill plates, wood window frames, and any exposed structural beams. Wood that sounds hollow when tapped — instead of solid — is a classic early termite indicator. Eastern subterranean termites eat wood from the inside out. By the time you see visible surface damage, the interior is already gone.

If you find hollow-sounding wood, don't panic and don't ignore it. Get a licensed termite inspector (look for a Wood-Destroying Insect inspector licensed by the NJ Department of Environmental Protection) before you list. A clean WDI report is an asset. A buyer discovering hollow sill plates is a liability.

What it costs to fix: $1,200–$3,500 for treatment, plus structural repair if needed.

2 Scratching or Scurrying Inside Walls at Night

If you hear repetitive scratching, scurrying, or what sounds like tiny footsteps inside your walls or ceiling — especially after 10 PM when the house is quiet — that's almost certainly rodents. Mice and rats are most active at night. They nest in wall cavities, attic spaces, and insulation.

In NJ's suburban communities bordering wooded areas — I'm thinking of towns like Chatham, Basking Ridge, Denville, and Morristown — mice entry is extremely common in fall and winter. They come in through gaps as small as a dime at the foundation, utility penetrations, and garage door weatherstripping.

Buyers' inspectors look for rodent evidence: droppings, chewed wiring, entry points, nesting material. Any of those findings triggers a credit request. Fix it now.

What it costs to fix: $300–$800 for exclusion and baiting by a licensed pest control company.

3 Ammonia Smell in the Basement, Pantry, or Under the Sink

Mice and rats navigate by urine trails. They mark paths they use repeatedly, and over time those trails create a sharp, ammonia-like smell — particularly in enclosed spaces where air doesn't circulate well. Basement storage areas, under kitchen and bathroom sinks, behind appliances, and in pantry corners are the most common spots.

The tricky part: people who live in the house often stop noticing it. It becomes background. Buyers — and buyers' agents — walking in fresh notice it immediately. I've had buyers leave a showing within five minutes because of rodent odor. They don't always say that's why. They just don't offer.

If you smell it, assume you have an active or recent rodent issue and deal with it before anything else. Clean affected areas with an enzyme-based cleaner (not bleach, which masks but doesn't neutralize). Then seal entry points.

What it costs to fix: $200–$600 for exclusion plus cleaning supplies.

4 Sawdust-Like Frass Near Windows, Baseboards, or Doors

Carpenter ants don't eat wood the way termites do — they excavate it to build galleries. What they leave behind is frass: a fine, sawdust-like material mixed with insect parts and debris, often found in small piles near window frames, doorframes, or at the base of exterior walls.

In NJ, carpenter ants are especially active in spring and early summer — which is also peak listing season. The timing is not in sellers' favor. If you're listing between April and July and you have mature trees or landscaping touching your exterior, do a walkthrough and check every exterior windowsill and doorframe for frass deposits.

Carpenter ants indicate moisture-damaged wood nearby. The ants didn't damage the wood — but they're proof it exists. An inspector will find both the ants and the underlying moisture issue.

What it costs to fix: $150–$400 for treatment, plus identifying and repairing the moisture source.

5 Unexplained Small Holes in Stored Clothing or Fabrics

Pull out the wool sweaters, cashmere throws, and any natural-fiber garments you store in guest bedroom closets. Small, irregular holes that weren't there before? Clothes moths. Their larvae feed on natural fibers — wool, silk, fur, cashmere — and are most commonly found in closets that stay dark and undisturbed for long stretches.

Clothes moths aren't a deal-killer in a home sale the way termites are, but they indicate that a closet has been closed off and unmaintained — which buyers notice. And if the closet also has a humidity issue (common in older NJ homes without climate-controlled storage), that's a different conversation.

The fix is straightforward: cedar blocks or sachets, cedar lining in closets, and laundering or dry-cleaning stored items. The bigger takeaway is to open every closet, inspect it, and make sure buyers aren't discovering neglect in storage spaces.

What it costs to fix: $50–$200 for cedar deterrents and cleaning.

6 Small Dark Droppings Near Food Storage or Cabinet Backs

Mouse droppings are small, dark, and tapered at both ends — about the size of a grain of rice. You'll find them in the backs of kitchen cabinets, in pantry drawers, behind the refrigerator, under the stove, and along baseboards where mice travel. Rat droppings are larger, about the size of an olive pit.

This is one of the first things buyers do at a showing: open cabinets. Kitchen cabinets especially. A buyer who opens a drawer and finds rodent droppings is not making an offer at list price. They may not make an offer at all. And if they do, expect a significant credit request and a pest inspection contingency.

Check every cabinet, every drawer, every closet floor before the first showing. If you find droppings, call a pest control company before you list — not during the inspection period.

What it costs to fix: $300–$700 for professional rodent exclusion and baiting program.

The WDI Inspection: What NJ Sellers Need to Know

A Wood-Destroying Insect (WDI) inspection — also called a termite inspection — is standard in virtually every NJ home sale. Buyers request it. VA loan buyers are federally required to have it. And increasingly, buyers are ordering it alongside the general home inspection rather than as a separate contingency.

The WDI inspector checks for evidence of termites, carpenter ants, carpenter bees, powder post beetles, and other wood-destroying organisms. They check the basement, attic, garage, and any accessible crawl spaces. The report documents active infestations, prior damage, and conducive conditions — things like wood-to-soil contact, inadequate ventilation, and moisture-damaged framing.

My recommendation to every seller: order a pre-listing WDI inspection before you put the house on the market. If it comes back clean, you have a document to share with buyers that builds confidence. If it finds something, you can address it on your timeline instead of under contract pressure at someone else's negotiating pace.

The cost of a WDI inspection is $75–$150. The cost of a buyer-found pest problem under contract is typically 10–30 times that in credits or concessions.

Frequently Asked Questions: Pest Issues and NJ Home Sales

Do I need a pest inspection to sell my home in NJ?

NJ does not legally mandate a Wood-Destroying Insect inspection for sellers, but buyers almost always request one. VA loan buyers must have it — federal requirement. In Morris, Essex, and Union Counties, where older homes and mature landscaping are common, buyers and their agents routinely include pest inspection contingencies in offers. My advice: order it yourself before you list. A clean report is a selling tool. An unfound problem is a liability.

How much does termite damage remediation cost in NJ?

Treatment alone runs $1,200–$3,500 for an average single-family home. If there's structural damage — compromised sill plates, floor joists, or framing — add $3,000–$15,000 for carpentry repair depending on scope. The earlier you catch it, the cheaper it is. A small hollow section of sill plate is a $500 repair. A compromised floor joist run is several thousand dollars and a much harder conversation with a buyer.

Should I disclose a past pest problem when selling my NJ home?

Yes. NJ's Seller's Disclosure form asks about pest history. Deliberately concealing a known past infestation is a fraud exposure. What you want to do is disclose honestly, provide documentation of any past treatment, and present a current clean WDI report. That combination — transparent history plus current resolution — is far better for your sale than hoping it doesn't surface during the buyer's inspection.

What pests are most common in NJ homes?

Eastern subterranean termites are the most structurally damaging and most commonly found in NJ inspections. Carpenter ants are second — especially in homes with moisture-damaged wood or landscaping that contacts the exterior. Mice are widespread in NJ's suburban areas, particularly in fall and winter. All three are routinely flagged in NJ home inspections and generate buyer credit requests when found under contract.
