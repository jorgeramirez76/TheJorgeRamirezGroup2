7 Things NJ Home Sellers Regret Tossing When They Declutter

Most sellers in Union, Morris, and Essex County go into decluttering mode and don't know when to stop. They toss things they'll wish they'd kept — and strip their home of the warmth that makes buyers fall in love with it.

The decluttering advice sellers get is usually too aggressive

Every real estate article on the internet tells you to declutter before listing. And they're right — a home drowning in stuff is harder to sell. But nobody talks about the other problem: the sellers who go too far. I've walked through NJ homes that look like they've been staged by someone who's never actually lived in a house. Bare walls. Empty shelves. Furniture reduced to one chair per room. No art, no books, no color.

These homes feel cold. Buyers walk through and can't picture themselves in them because there's nothing human there to connect with. And here's the kicker — sellers in Chatham, Westfield, Maplewood, Montclair, and every other town I work in often regret the overzealous purge after the fact. They tossed things they actually needed, things they loved, things that made the home feel like a home.

The goal of pre-listing decluttering is to reveal the home underneath the stuff — not to create a hotel lobby. There's a meaningful difference between editing your belongings and erasing yourself entirely from the property. Here are the seven categories sellers most often toss and then regret.

Jorge's rule: Declutter to reveal the home — not to hide the life lived in it. A buyer buying in Summit or Cranford or Florham Park is buying a life as much as a structure. Give them something to imagine themselves into.

7 things sellers toss and later regret

  1. Books — especially shelved ones A room with a few shelves of well-organized books tells buyers something important: someone lived here with intention. Books are one of the most powerful visual staging tools in a home. They add color, texture, and warmth without trying. Sellers who clear their bookshelves entirely leave flat, featureless walls that buyers remember as "the one without any character." If you have hundreds of books, reduce by half. But don't clear the shelves.
    Keep: A curated selection. Toss: Paperbacks you read once in 2011.
  2. Art and wall decor with personal meaning You've heard "depersonalize your home before listing." That advice gets taken too far. A couple of framed prints, a piece of art you've had for years, a meaningful photograph printed large — these are fine. They add life to walls that would otherwise look blank. What buyers don't want is a gallery wall of every family vacation, a collection of religious imagery across six rooms, or portraits of you and your spouse in every hallway. Edit the personal. Don't erase the aesthetic.
    Keep: Art that enhances the room. Toss: Photos that make it feel like someone else's home.
  3. Extra furniture that defined rooms Sellers often pull furniture out aggressively to make rooms look bigger. The result is a living room with a couch and nothing else, a bedroom with just a bed frame, a dining room with a table but no chairs. These choices make buyers anxious about whether their furniture will fit. Proper furniture helps buyers understand scale. It tells them a king fits there, that the dining area seats eight, that the family room works with a sectional. Removing too much furniture creates doubt, not confidence.
    Keep: Pieces that define function and scale. Remove: Pieces that crowd or block natural traffic flow.
  4. Tools and hardware from around the house That junk drawer full of Allen wrenches and picture hooks. The spare screws from the bed frame you assembled in 2019. The box of assorted fasteners in the garage. Sellers toss these in the decluttering frenzy and then spend $80 at Home Depot right after moving in somewhere new trying to reassemble things and hang artwork. Tools and hardware are expensive and annoying to replace. In the garage specifically, a well-organized tool setup communicates to buyers that maintenance happens here — which is actually a selling point.
    Keep: Organized tools and hardware. Toss: Broken tools, duplicates with no purpose.
  5. Spare household items guests and emergencies depend on Extra blankets. A spare set of towels. The guest mugs you only use four times a year. These feel like clutter right up until the first cold night in your new place or the first time someone stays over. They're also useful for staging. A neatly folded throw blanket on a reading chair adds warmth. Extra towels folded and displayed in the bathroom signal to buyers that storage exists and works. Hold onto these until you're actually unpacked in the new home.
    Keep: Anything with a plausible use in the next 12 months. Toss: The random tchotchkes that haven't moved in three years.
  6. Formalwear and occasion clothing Nothing triggers a clean-out like listing a home. Sellers donate entire wardrobes of formalwear — cocktail dresses, suit jackets, event pieces — in the goal of clearing closets for buyers. Then six months later there's a wedding, a work event, a dinner that requires something other than business casual. Formalwear is expensive and size-specific. Unless you're certain it no longer fits or will never be needed, box it neatly and store it. A buyer opening a clean, organized closet doesn't count your suits — they're measuring shelf space.
    Keep: Quality formalwear in your size. Toss: Anything visibly dated, damaged, or two sizes wrong.
  7. Chargers and cables for devices you still own Old camera. Wireless speaker you've had for eight years. The tablet that still works fine. Sellers toss the cable pile in the decluttering rush and then can't power the devices they intended to keep. The tangled cable drawer is legitimately something to address — but address it, don't delete it. Sort the cables. Label what you know. Keep anything that powers a device currently in the house. Toss duplicates and obvious orphans.
    Keep: Cables for working devices you own. Toss: Unknown cords for hardware long gone.

What you should actually get rid of before listing your NJ home

Decluttering has a real target: items that add visual noise without adding value, items that signal poor maintenance, and items that make it harder for buyers to see the home itself. Here's where the focus should actually go.

Clear out closets to 50–60% capacity — not 100%. Buyers open closets and judge storage. A closet that's packed wall-to-wall reads as too small. A closet with some breathing room reads as adequate. The goal isn't empty; it's organized and spacious-looking.

Remove furniture in genuinely poor condition — the scuffed coffee table, the dining chair with a broken rung, the desk that warps in the corner. These items age a home visually. They don't add warmth; they broadcast neglect.

Personal photos on every surface — reduce significantly. Buyers in a home with 40 family photos feel like they're walking through someone else's memory. One gallery wall, a few framed pieces — fine. Photo clusters on every shelf and counter — too much.

Pet evidence. Beds, food dishes, litter boxes during showings — these need to be moved, cleaned, or staged out of sight. NJ buyers are often allergic to pets, and even those who aren't associate pet smells with maintenance issues that show up on inspection.

The result you're going for is a home that reads: organized, maintained, spacious, and lived-in by someone who takes care of things. That is a very specific target, and hitting it means editing — not erasing.

Frequently asked questions

What do NJ home sellers most often regret tossing when decluttering?

The most common decluttering regrets from NJ sellers include books and art that gave rooms warmth, extra furniture that helped buyers understand scale, tools and hardware that were expensive to replace, sentimental items discarded in the urgency of the move, formalwear tossed to clear closet space, spare household items, and electronic cables for devices still in use. The goal is to show off space, not strip the home of character.

How much should I declutter before listing my NJ home?

The goal is strategic, not total. Remove items that crowd rooms or distract buyers. But a completely empty home looks cold and often sells slower than one that feels warm and curated. Leave enough furniture to define each room's purpose, enough art to give walls a focal point, and enough personal touches that the home feels human — just edited.

Should I get rid of furniture before listing my NJ home?

Remove pieces that block traffic flow, overcrowd rooms, or are in poor condition. But don't strip furniture down to nothing. Buyers need furniture to understand scale — whether a king fits, whether the dining area seats six, whether the family room works with a sectional. The sweet spot is furniture that defines the room without dominating it.

Is it OK to keep books and art when selling my NJ home?

Yes. A few well-curated shelves of books signal to buyers that this is a home where people lived well. Art gives walls a focal point. The key is editing, not eliminating. Reduce volume if you're an aggressive collector, but keep what makes the space feel like a home rather than a showroom.

What should I definitely get rid of before listing my NJ home?

Clear out broken items, multiples you don't use, expired pantry goods, toys kids have outgrown, and anything in storage untouched for two years. Reduce personal photos significantly. Store pet supplies out of sight during showings. Address the junk drawer. Focus on items that broadcast clutter without adding visual value — those are your real targets.
