Why New Jersey Property Tax Rates Cannot Be Compared Town to Town

Updated July 2026 · By Jorge Ramirez, Keller Williams Premier Properties · NJ licensed agent since 2017, License #1754604

Scotch Plains taxes at 11.768 per $100 of assessed value. Clark taxes at 2.214. A buyer comparing those two numbers would conclude Scotch Plains is more than five times as expensive. It is not — the two towns’ actual burdens are far closer than the rates suggest. New Jersey rates are applied to assessed value, and assessed values sit at very different fractions of market value from town to town, so a published rate on its own tells you almost nothing about what you will pay.

Why the Rate Is Misleading

A New Jersey tax rate is applied to your home’s assessed value, which is a number the town assigned, not the number you paid. Towns revalue on their own schedules, and some have not done it in a long time. A town whose assessments have drifted far below market value has to charge a much higher rate to raise the same revenue from the same houses.

So the rate tells you almost nothing on its own. To use it you also need the assessment ratio — how close assessed values sit to real sale prices — and that ratio varies enormously across towns that are ten minutes apart.

Six Union County Towns, Same County, Wildly Different Rates

These are the 2024 general tax rates as published by the State of New Jersey — the number a town advertises and the number buyers try to compare:

TownPublished general tax rate (per $100 of assessed value)
Clark2.214
Westfield2.252
Berkeley Heights4.287
Summit4.356
Cranford6.779
Scotch Plains11.768

On its face that looks like Scotch Plains homeowners pay more than five times what Clark homeowners pay. They do not. These six towns sit in one county, share a housing market, and their actual tax burdens are far closer together than the rates suggest — in some cases nearly identical.

The reason is the denominator. The rate is multiplied by your assessed value, and assessed values across New Jersey sit at very different fractions of what homes actually sell for, because towns revalue on their own schedules. A town whose assessments have drifted well below market has to charge a much higher rate to raise the same money from the same houses. A town that revalued recently charges a low-looking rate against assessments close to full value.

New Jersey formally acknowledges this: the state publishes an equalization ratio (often called the Director’s ratio) for every municipality precisely because assessed value and market value diverge. That ratio is the missing piece in every rate comparison you have seen.

The Number You Actually Want, and Where to Get It

The comparable figure is the effective tax rate — the annual bill as a percentage of what the home is genuinely worth. It is the only version that compares fairly across towns, and it is the one to ask for.

Get it from the source rather than from a listing portal or a blog table, including this one. Two places give you an authoritative answer:

  • The New Jersey Division of Taxation publishes general tax rates, equalization ratios and effective tax rates by municipality each year. That is the definitive comparison set.
  • The municipal tax assessor for the specific property. They can tell you the current assessment, the current bill, and when the town last revalued — the three facts that actually determine what you will pay.
Why this page does not print its own effective-rate table. I built one from Garden State MLS closed sales and then checked it against the state’s published figures. Mine came out roughly 30% low across all six towns — a uniform bias across rates spanning 2.2 to 11.8, which is the signature of a data artifact rather than a market finding. The likely cause is that an MLS record’s tax figure reflects an earlier assessment year than its sale price, so dividing one by the other understates the rate in an appreciating market. The argument on this page holds up; my arithmetic did not, so it is not printed here. Use the state’s figures.

The Two Numbers Sellers and Buyers Confuse

Dollars and percentages point in opposite directions here, and conflating them causes real mistakes.

  • In dollars, Summit is expensive — a median bill around $15,300 — because the houses are worth a great deal. Clark’s median bill is roughly $10,400.
  • As a share of value, Summit is the cheapest of the six and Clark is near the top.

If you are choosing between towns at the same budget, the effective rate is what you want. If you are budgeting a monthly payment on a specific house, the dollar bill is what your lender will use. Both are legitimate; they are simply not interchangeable, and a buyer told only the published rate has been given the least useful of the three.

What This Means Before You Make an Offer

Three questions worth asking about any New Jersey town you are seriously considering:

  • When did this town last revalue? A town due for one is a town where assessments are about to move toward market value. That does not automatically raise everyone’s bill — if the town needs the same revenue, the rate drops as assessments rise — but homes that were under-assessed relative to their neighbours tend to see the largest increases.
  • How does this specific home’s assessment compare to its neighbours’? That comparison, not the sale price, is what drives whether a bill looks high or low after a revaluation.
  • What is the actual current bill? Not the rate, not an estimate from a listing portal — the recorded number. It is public, and it is the figure your lender will escrow against.

Buying above the assessed value does not, on its own, trigger a reassessment in New Jersey. That is one of the most common worries I hear and it is largely misplaced.

Common Questions

Why is one NJ town’s rate five times another’s?

Because the rate applies to assessed value and towns revalue in different years. A town whose assessments have drifted well below market value needs a far higher rate than one whose assessments sit close to full value, to collect the same amount from the same houses.

What is an effective property tax rate?

The annual bill divided by what the home is actually worth. It is the only figure that compares fairly between New Jersey towns, because it strips out the assessment-ratio distortion. Get it from the Division of Taxation or your municipal assessor.

Which of these towns has the lowest property taxes?

In raw dollars the answer tracks home values, so the most expensive towns carry the largest bills. As a share of what a home is worth the ranking can look quite different. Both are legitimate questions — get the figures from the state rather than a third-party estimate before you rely on either.

Does a higher assessment mean a higher bill?

Not by itself — only if your assessment rises faster than your neighbours’. A townwide revaluation that lifts every assessment while revenue stays flat lowers the rate and moves most bills very little.

Why is one NJ town's property tax rate five times another's?

Which Union County town has the lowest property taxes?

That depends on which question you are asking, and it is worth getting the figure from the state rather than a third party. In raw dollars the answer tracks home values, so the expensive towns have the largest bills. As a share of what a home is worth the ranking can differ substantially, which is why the published rate alone will mislead you.

Does a higher assessment mean higher taxes?

Not by itself. What matters is your assessment relative to other homes in the same town. If a townwide revaluation raises every assessment and the town needs the same revenue, the rate falls and most bills move very little. Your bill rises meaningfully only if your assessment rises faster than your neighbours'.

Should I expect my NJ property taxes to change after I buy?

Buying at a price above the assessed value does not automatically trigger a reassessment in New Jersey. But if the town is due for a revaluation, assessments across the town will be brought toward market value, and a home that had been under-assessed relative to its neighbours can see a larger increase. Asking when a town last revalued is a fair question before you make an offer.

Want the Real Tax Number on a Specific House?

Before you make an offer, it is worth knowing the recorded bill, how the assessment compares to the street, and whether the town is due to revalue. That is a fifteen-minute conversation, not a research project.
