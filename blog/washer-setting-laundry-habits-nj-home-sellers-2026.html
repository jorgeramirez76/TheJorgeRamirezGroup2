The Only Washer Setting You Need: Laundry Habits That Protect an NJ Home Before You Sell

A laundry expert says one washer setting handles almost everything. Here is why I agree, and why the laundry area deserves attention before your house goes on the market in New Jersey.

Why a laundry article belongs on a real estate blog

Buyers open every door. In a colonial in Westfield or Cranford the laundry room is usually off the kitchen or in the basement, and in the newer townhouses in Edison and Woodbridge it is a closet on the second floor. Either way, buyers look inside, and so does the home inspector. A washer that smells, a lint-packed dryer vent or a puddle under a hose tells them the house has been run casually. That impression follows them into the offer.

So when House Outlook ran a piece this week arguing that most people only need one washer setting, I read it as a seller's article. Fewer cycles, less water heating and less wear on the machine all add up to a laundry room that is easier to keep ready for showings.

The one setting: normal cycle, cold water

Washing machines now come with buttons for activewear, bulky items, sanitize, quick wash and more. The expert's point is that the normal cycle in cold water covers everyday clothes, sheets and most towels. Modern detergents are designed to work in cold water, and heating water is the largest share of the energy a washer uses. The U.S. Department of Energy says as much in its laundry energy guidance.

My version for a house that is listed: run normal and cold by default, use warm or hot only when a care label or an illness in the house calls for it, and stop overloading the drum. A machine that runs fewer hot cycles is quieter, drier inside between loads and less likely to develop the sour smell that buyers notice before they notice the tile.

The dryer side: lint, balls and the vent

A second House Outlook item this week asked whether wool dryer balls go bad. They compress and pick up lint over time, and a hot wash and full dry refreshes them. I like them for sellers because they shorten drying time without the scented sheets that leave residue on the lint screen and a smell in the room.

The bigger issue is the vent. Clean the lint screen every load, and have the duct cleaned to the outside at least once a year. The U.S. Fire Administration lists failure to clean as the leading factor in home dryer fires. Inspectors in Summit and Morristown walk outside and look at the vent hood. If it is stuck open, packed with lint or vents into the garage, it ends up in the report.

What buyers and inspectors check in the laundry area

  • Hoses and valves. Rubber supply hoses with bulges or cracks get replaced with braided stainless ones. The shutoff valves should actually turn.
  • The drain pan. Second-floor laundry closets in Hoboken, Jersey City and the Middlesex townhouse communities should have a pan with a drain. A rusted pan or a stain on the ceiling below is a disclosure item, not a cleaning item.
  • Vent material. A plastic or foil accordion hose behind the dryer is an easy replacement with rigid or semi-rigid metal duct.
  • Outlets and lighting. Missing GFCI protection near a utility sink and a dead overhead bulb are cheap fixes that keep the room off the inspector's list. See my electrical warning signs guide.
  • Odor and moisture. Leave the front-loader door ajar between loads and run the drum-clean cycle once. Do not mask a musty smell; find the source.

Do the washer and dryer stay?

In New Jersey, appliances are personal property unless the listing and the contract include them. Decide before the photographer arrives. If they stay, note the model and age in your appliance inventory and make sure they work on showing day. If they go with you, say so in the listing remarks so a buyer in Montclair does not write an offer around a set that is leaving. Your agent and attorney confirm the inclusions during attorney review, and any known defect belongs on the seller disclosure statement.

A showing-day laundry reset

Run one normal cold load the night before, empty both machines, put the baskets away and wipe the tops. Nothing wet, nothing hanging, no detergent bottles on the floor. Pair it with the household laundry checklist for linens and towels, and the room reads as what it is: a working space that has been taken care of.

Questions about laundry, appliances and an NJ sale

Is the normal cycle in cold water really enough for most laundry?

For everyday clothes, sheets and most towels, yes. Modern detergents are formulated for cold water, and heating water is the largest share of a washer's energy use. Follow the care label, and use warm or hot when a label or a health situation calls for it.

Do the washer and dryer stay with the house in New Jersey?

Only if the listing and the contract include them. Appliances are personal property unless they are written in. Decide before you list, state it in the remarks, and confirm the inclusions with your agent and attorney during attorney review.

Will a home inspector look at the laundry room?

Yes. Inspectors in my experience check the supply hoses, shutoff valves, the drain pan under an upper-floor washer, the dryer vent material and the exterior vent hood. Lint, plastic accordion ducting and a stained ceiling below the laundry are common report items.

How often should a dryer vent be cleaned?

Clean the lint screen every load and have the full duct cleaned to the outside at least once a year, more often for a long run or a household that does a lot of laundry. The U.S. Fire Administration lists failure to clean as the leading factor in home dryer fires.

Do wool dryer balls wear out?

They compress and pick up lint over time rather than going bad. A hot wash and a full dry refreshes them. They shorten drying time without the scented residue that dryer sheets leave on the lint screen.
