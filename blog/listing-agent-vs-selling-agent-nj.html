Listing Agent vs Selling Agent in New Jersey

Updated July 30, 2026 · by Jorge Ramirez, NJ License #1754604

The listing agent represents the seller. The “selling agent” is usually the agent who brought the buyer. That sounds backwards, which is exactly why this question gets asked so often. The term comes from older paperwork that split a deal into the listing side and the selling side — the side that produced the buyer who bought. So the selling agent and the buyer’s agent are normally the same person.

Why the term is genuinely confusing

You are not misreading it. “Selling” sounds like it should describe the person helping you sell, and plenty of people — including some agents — use “selling agent” to mean exactly that. Both usages are in circulation, which makes the phrase unreliable in a conversation where money is at stake.

The fix is to stop using it. Say listing agent for the seller’s side and buyer’s agent for the buyer’s side, and the ambiguity disappears entirely. When you see “selling agent” on a document or a portal listing, assume it means the buyer’s side and confirm.

“Showing agent” is a third thing entirely

A showing agent opens houses and runs tours on behalf of another agent’s client, usually because the primary agent is booked. They may have no part in writing your offer or negotiating your contract.

This matters more than it sounds. If the person walking you through four houses on a Saturday is not the person who will advise you on price and terms, you want to know that before you fall in love with one. It is a fair and unawkward question: who will actually be advising me when I write an offer?

Who pays whom, after August 2024

This is the part that genuinely changed. Following the August 2024 NAR settlement, buyer-agent compensation is negotiated separately rather than advertised through the MLS, and buyers sign a written buyer agency agreement before touring homes. There is more detail on the buyer side in the NJ buyer agency agreement explained.

For a seller, the practical position is this: your total commission is negotiated in your listing agreement, and in most New Jersey markets that lands somewhere around 4% to 6% of the sale price covering both sides. You can still offer to cover part or all of the buyer agent’s fee, and many New Jersey sellers do because it widens the buyer pool — but it is now a negotiated term rather than an automatic feature of listing.

The number matters less than what it buys. Ask what the marketing actually consists of, whether any portion is being offered toward the buyer’s side, and what happens if the house does not sell.

What your listing agreement commits you to

Four terms are worth reading before you sign. None are unusual and all are negotiable:

  • The term. How long the agent holds the exclusive right to sell.
  • The commission and how it is allocated. Total figure, and what if anything is offered toward the buyer’s agent.
  • Cancellation. What happens, and what it costs, if you want out early.
  • The protection period. This one surprises people: after the agreement expires you can still owe commission if the eventual buyer was introduced during the term.

On dual agency. One agent representing both sides is permitted in New Jersey only with informed written consent from both. Be clear-eyed about what it means: nobody can argue for the highest price and the lowest price at the same time, so what you get is a facilitator rather than an advocate. Worth asking your listing agent up front what happens if the buyer they bring turns out to be their own client.

You need an attorney too, not instead

New Jersey is an attorney-review state, so the contract passes through a review period in which either side’s lawyer can change or cancel it. Your agent handles pricing, marketing and negotiation. Your attorney handles the contract. Those are different jobs and a seller wants both — it is not a redundancy and it is not optional in practice.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a listing agent and a selling agent?

The listing agent represents the seller and markets the home. The selling agent, in the way the industry normally uses the term, is the agent who brought the buyer — the agent on the selling side of the completed transaction, which is the same person most people call the buyer's agent. The confusion is real and it is not your fault: plenty of people use "selling agent" to mean the seller's agent, because "selling" sounds like the seller. If clarity matters in the moment, say "listing agent" and "buyer's agent" instead and the ambiguity disappears.

Is a selling agent the same as a buyer's agent?

In normal industry usage, yes. "Selling agent" and "buyer's agent" typically describe the same person: the agent representing the purchaser. The term survives from older paperwork where the two sides of a deal were the listing side and the selling side, meaning the side that produced the buyer who bought.

What is a showing agent?

A third and different thing. A showing agent opens homes and runs tours on behalf of another agent's client, usually because the primary agent is unavailable. They are not necessarily the person representing you in the negotiation, and they may have no involvement in your contract at all. Worth asking directly who will actually be advising you when it comes time to write an offer.

Who pays the buyer's agent in New Jersey now?

It is negotiated rather than assumed. Since the August 2024 NAR settlement, buyer-agent compensation is negotiated separately instead of being advertised through the MLS, and buyers sign a written buyer agency agreement before touring. A seller can still offer to cover some or all of the buyer agent's fee, and in many New Jersey transactions they do, but it is now a negotiated term of the deal rather than an automatic feature of the listing.

What commission does a listing agent charge in NJ?

It is fully negotiated in the listing agreement, and in most New Jersey markets a total commission of roughly 4% to 6% of the sale price covers both sides. What matters more than the headline number is what the agreement says it buys, whether any portion is offered toward the buyer's agent, and how long you are committed for.

Can one agent represent both the buyer and the seller?

That is dual agency, and it is permitted in New Jersey only with informed written consent from both sides. Be clear-eyed about the trade-off: a single agent cannot advocate for the highest price and the lowest price simultaneously, so the practical effect is that you get a facilitator rather than an advocate. Ask what happens if the buyer your listing agent brings turns out to be their own client.

What does a NJ listing agreement actually commit me to?

Read for four things before signing: the term, meaning how long the agent has the exclusive right to sell; the total commission and how it is split or offered; what happens if you cancel early; and the protection period, which can obligate you to pay commission after expiry if the eventual buyer was introduced during the term. None of these are unusual, but all of them are negotiable and all of them are easier to discuss before you sign.

Do I need a lawyer as well as a listing agent in NJ?

In practice yes. New Jersey is an attorney-review state, so real estate contracts pass through a review period in which either side's lawyer can modify or cancel the agreement. Your agent handles pricing, marketing and negotiation; your attorney handles the contract. They are separate jobs and you want both.
