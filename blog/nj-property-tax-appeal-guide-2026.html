Is Your NJ Home Over-Assessed?

I've worked full time in New Jersey real estate since 2017. The question I hear most often from homeowners isn't about the market — it's about property taxes. And underneath that question is usually the same thing: "Am I paying too much?"

The answer, for a meaningful number of NJ homeowners, is yes. Assessments don't update every year in most municipalities. When the market moves fast — and NJ's has — assessments can lag. Some homeowners end up paying taxes on an implied value that's $50,000, $100,000, or more above what comparable homes actually sell for. That difference costs them thousands every year.

The appeal process exists precisely for this situation. It is not complicated. Most people don't use it because they don't know where to start. This guide walks you through it.

How NJ Assesses Property Values

NJ municipalities assess property at a percentage of market value called the "average ratio" or "equalization rate." Some towns assess at or near 100% of value after a recent revaluation. Others are running at 60%, 70%, or 80% if the revaluation hasn't been done in years.

To know if you're over-assessed, the math works like this:

  • Find your assessment on your tax bill or the municipality's property records portal.
  • Divide your assessment by the average ratio for your town. (The NJ Division of Taxation publishes these annually.)
  • That gives you your implied market value according to the assessor.
  • Compare that implied value to what comparable homes in your neighborhood actually sold for in the past 12 months.

If your implied value is significantly higher than what comps support — typically 15% or more — you have a viable appeal. Below that threshold, the assessor has a defensible margin, and the county board is less likely to reduce your assessment.

Example: Your assessment is $420,000. Your town's average ratio is 75%. Your implied market value is $560,000. But three comparable homes in your neighborhood sold in the past year for $490,000-$510,000. That gap is roughly $60,000 — a legitimate case for an appeal.

The NJ Property Tax Appeal Process, Step by Step

  1. Get your assessment notice. It arrives each year, usually in January or February. It shows your assessed value and the tax levy.
  2. Calculate your implied market value using your town's equalization ratio. Compare it to recent comparable sales.
  3. Gather comparable sales (comps). You need homes similar to yours — same general size, age, condition, neighborhood — that sold between October 1 of the pretax year and the present. Three to five solid comps is the target.
  4. File your appeal with the County Board of Taxation before April 1. The form is straightforward. Include your comps as exhibits.
  5. Attend your hearing. County board hearings are informal. You present your comps, the assessor presents theirs, and the board decides. Most residential appeals are settled before the hearing ever happens.
  6. Accept or escalate. If the county board rules against you, you have 45 days to appeal to NJ Tax Court. That's where an attorney earns their fee.

What the County Actually Looks For

County boards want to see credible comparable sales. The strongest comps are:

  • Sales within your municipality, not just your county
  • Similar square footage, lot size, bedroom and bathroom count
  • Sales within the last 12 months before October 1 of the pretax year
  • Arms-length transactions — no foreclosures, estate sales, or related-party deals

A formal appraisal from a licensed NJ appraiser is the strongest evidence you can bring. But many homeowners win straightforward cases at the county board level with well-organized MLS comparable sales and a clear presentation. If your case goes to Tax Court, hire an attorney. At the county level, a prepared homeowner with good comps wins more often than people expect.

What This Means for Your Property Value When You Sell

Here's what a lot of homeowners don't think about: a high assessment doesn't just cost you money every year. It affects how buyers perceive your home's value, and it affects what buyers budget for ongoing costs after they move in. When I'm listing a home, I look at the assessed value the same way a buyer's agent will. If your taxes are significantly out of line with comparable properties, smart buyers will factor that into their offers.

If you're planning to sell in the next one to three years, appealing a high assessment now accomplishes two things: it reduces your carrying costs in the meantime, and it makes your property more competitive when you list. A buyer comparing two similar homes will notice a $4,000 annual tax difference. It shows up in their mortgage calculation every month.

NJ Property Tax Appeal: Frequently Asked Questions

What is the deadline to file a property tax appeal in New Jersey?

The standard NJ property tax appeal deadline is April 1 of the tax year you're appealing. If your property was recently reassessed through a municipality-wide revaluation, the deadline may extend to May 1. Missing the deadline means you lose the right to appeal for that year. Mark it on your calendar in January when your assessment notice arrives.

How does New Jersey assess property values?

NJ municipalities assess property at a percentage of market value called the "average ratio." Some towns assess at 100% of value after a recent revaluation; others assess at a lower historical percentage. To know if you're over-assessed, divide your assessment by the ratio to get your implied market value, then compare it to what comparable homes actually sold for. A significant gap is the basis of a viable appeal.

What evidence do I need for an NJ property tax appeal?

The most persuasive evidence is comparable sales — homes similar to yours in size, age, condition, and location that sold in the 12 months before October 1 of the pretax year. You typically need three to five solid comps. A formal appraisal carries the most weight, but a well-organized set of MLS comps with a clear argument often works at the County Board level without one.

Should I hire a property tax attorney to appeal in NJ?

For County Board of Taxation hearings on a typical residential property, most homeowners can represent themselves with solid comparable sales. An attorney earns their fee when the case goes to NJ Tax Court, involves a commercial property, or when the assessment discrepancy is large enough that the math really matters. Attorney fees for tax appeals are typically contingency-based — a percentage of the first year's savings.

Can appealing my property tax assessment hurt me when I sell?

Not directly. A successful appeal lowers your assessed value, which is public record. Buyers sometimes look at assessment vs. sale price as a rough value check, but a well-priced home sells based on comparable sales, not the tax assessment. The bigger concern for sellers is usually the opposite — a high assessment that pushes annual taxes above comparable properties.

How much money can I save with a successful NJ property tax appeal?

Savings depend on how far off your assessment is and your local tax rate. NJ's effective property tax rates average around 2.2-2.5% depending on municipality. If your home is over-assessed by $100,000, you might save $2,200-$2,500 per year. Over five years without a reassessment, that's $11,000-$12,500. The math makes it worth the effort for most homeowners with a legitimate case.

What happens if the municipality appeals MY appeal?

Municipalities can cross-appeal if they believe your property is actually under-assessed. This is more common on high-value or commercial properties. On typical residential homes in NJ, municipalities rarely cross-appeal. If you're concerned — especially if you recently purchased at a price significantly above your assessment — consult a tax attorney before filing.
