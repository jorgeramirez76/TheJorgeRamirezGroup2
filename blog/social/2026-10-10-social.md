### Facebook Post (100-150 words)

House Outlook's newsletter this week pointed to a Real Simple piece where professional organizers shared the decluttering rules they actually live by. Not a weekend purge. Small habits that keep mess from creeping back. That is exactly what a listed house needs, because most sellers declutter once before photos and the house slides back by the second open house.

My seller's edition of those rules: one in, two out from the day you decide to list. Every object gets a home, and the home is never a counter or dresser top. Decide once, no "maybe" pile. Reset the flat surfaces every night. And declutter in the order a buyer walks, not the order that bothers you.

Buyers in Westfield, Summit and Montclair open every closet. Give them two-thirds full with floor showing.

## Article 2: 2026 Houseplant Trends: Which Ones Help a NJ Listing and Which to Skip

**Live URL:** https://thejorgeramirezgroup.com/blog/houseplant-trends-nj-home-listing-staging-2026
**Image:** https://thejorgeramirezgroup.com/images/properties/03-shingle-porch.webp (crop to 1200×630)
**Instagram image:** https://thejorgeramirezgroup.com/images/properties/03-shingle-porch.webp (crop to 1080×1080)

### Facebook Post (100-150 words)

House Outlook's newsletter this week linked Livingetc's roundup of 2026 houseplant trends, from low-maintenance plants treated as design pieces to full "verdant maximalism." I read it as a listing agent, because I have watched one good plant make a room in Montclair and twenty mediocre ones hide a room in Maplewood.

For a seller only two questions matter. Does the plant make the room read larger and better kept? And is it hiding or causing damage?

One large healthy plant in an empty corner helps photos. Plant shelves across windows, propagation jars on the sill, grow lights and indoor hanging planters all cost you, either in light, in photos, or in water rings an inspector will find.

Lift every pot before photos. What is under it is a repair item, not a staging choice.

### Facebook Post (100-150 words)

House Outlook's newsletter this week linked a Daily Passport ranking of the U.S. cities with the highest cost of living, built on Numbeo data. Every week a buyer from Manhattan, Brooklyn or Queens sends me a list like this and asks whether New Jersey is "cheaper."

A cost-of-living index measures groceries, restaurants, transit and rent for a whole city. It cannot measure the property tax bill on a specific house in Cranford, the NJ Transit pass from Summit, whether you will buy a car, or how your income taxes change when you cross the river. Those are the whole decision.

I build a one-page side-by-side with every relocating buyer: current rent, transit and city taxes on the left, the full monthly on two or three real NJ listings on the right. The answer becomes a number for a specific house.
