3 Design Fixes for Your NJ Home's Biggest Eyesores Before You List

Good design does two things: it highlights the beauty and quiets the necessities. Every NJ home has radiators, cords, thermostats, and pipes that can't go away. The trick is making sure buyers never look at them.

Buyers look where the eye is pulled — not where you want them to

I've stood in a lot of beautiful living rooms in Montclair, Maplewood, Westfield, and Summit and watched buyers stare at the wrong thing. Original 1920s molding, oak floors, a working fireplace — and their eyes go straight to the rusty radiator under the window, or the tangle of cords hanging off the TV, or the beige thermostat and alarm keypad sitting in the middle of an otherwise perfect wall.

None of those things are defects. They're necessities. Every home has them. But in listing photos and in the first two minutes of a showing, they compete with the features buyers should be falling in love with. And when the eye lands on a necessity instead of a feature, buyers start mentally discounting before they've reached the stairs.

Designers solve this with three moves: camouflage it, contain it, or redirect the eye away from it. Here's how I apply each one to the eyesores I see most in NJ homes before they go on the market.

Jorge's observation: This isn't about hiding problems. The radiator, the panel, and the pipes are all still there and all still disclosed. It's about presentation. A well-staged home controls where the buyer's eye goes in the first sixty seconds, and that first sixty seconds sets the price they're willing to pay.

The three fixes, applied to NJ's most common eyesores

  1. Camouflage: paint radiators, vents, and panels to match the wall Cast-iron radiators, baseboard heaters, cold-air returns, and electrical panel doors in older Essex, Union, and Morris County homes are usually chipped, rusty, or a different color than everything around them. A coat of high-heat radiator paint or regular trim paint in the wall color makes them recede. The radiator is still there. Buyers just stop looking at it — and a clean, painted radiator says "maintained" instead of "neglected."
    Cost: $15–$40 per radiator · Time: An afternoon
  2. Camouflage: give thermostats and keypads a quiet background A thermostat, alarm keypad, doorbell chime, and intercom stacked on one wall is the classic NJ hallway. You can't move them cheaply, but you can stop them from being the focal point. Hang a piece of art or a mirror nearby so the wall has an intended feature. Replace a yellowed plastic cover with a clean white one. Never hang a frame over the thermostat — buyers open everything.
    Cost: $0–$30 · Impact: the hallway reads intentional in photos
  3. Contain: get every cord out of sight Visible cords are the single most common thing I edit out of a room before photos. For a wall-mounted TV, an in-wall cord kit hides power and HDMI behind the drywall for under $50. If you don't want to open the wall, a paintable raceway painted to match disappears. Behind the console, bundle everything with velcro ties into a power strip hidden inside the cabinet. Lamps get their cords tucked behind furniture legs.
    Cost: $20–$50 · Time: One hour per room
  4. Contain: pet gear, trash, and recycling go into furniture or out the door Litter boxes, crates, food bowls, and the open kitchen trash can are eyesores that also carry smell. Before every showing, pet items leave the main living areas — garage, closed closet, or the car. If the litter box has to stay, it goes into a furniture-style enclosure in the least-used room and stays spotless. Trash and recycling move into a pull-out cabinet or a lidded bin in the mudroom. Many NJ buyers are allergic or uneasy about pets, and one visible litter box makes them sniff the air for the rest of the tour.
    Cost: $0–$120 for an enclosure · Impact: removes a smell trigger, not just a visual one
  5. Redirect: give every room one clear focal point If the eye has somewhere good to land, it stops hunting. In a living room, that's the fireplace or the window with the best light — arrange furniture toward it, put a lamp beside it, and keep the wall around it clean. In a bedroom, it's the bed and the headboard wall. A room with a strong focal point can carry a window AC unit or a baseboard heater without anyone noticing. A room without one turns every necessity into a distraction.
    Cost: $0 · Time: 30 minutes of rearranging
  6. Redirect: in the basement, light and paint beat concealment Exposed pipes, ductwork, and the mechanical corner are unavoidable in NJ basements from Cranford to Edison to Woodbridge. Don't box them in — that raises questions at inspection. Instead, paint the ceiling and pipes one uniform color (dark or light, but one color), swap in bright LED bulbs, and clear the floor. A clean, evenly lit basement reads as usable space. A dim one with pipes in three colors reads as "problem area."
    Cost: Paint plus bulbs · Impact: basement goes from liability to bonus square footage

Photo via Unsplash — Furniture aimed at a focal point, cords out of sight, and warm lighting: the room's necessities are still there, but nobody is looking at them.

What quieting the necessities does for your sale

I can't put a dollar figure on a painted radiator. What I can tell you is what changes in the room. When the eye lands on the fireplace instead of the cords, buyers talk about where their couch would go. When it lands on a rusty radiator, they ask how old the boiler is. Same house, same boiler, completely different conversation — and the second conversation is the one that ends in a lower offer and a longer inspection list.

This matters more in NJ's older towns than almost anywhere else. The homes in Glen Ridge, South Orange, Madison, and Scotch Plains that command premium prices are full of radiators, plaster walls, and 1920s mechanical quirks. Buyers accept all of that when the presentation says the home has been loved. They punish it when the presentation says it's been tolerated.

The three moves — camouflage, contain, redirect — cost very little and take a weekend. Before you spend real money on upgrades, spend that weekend first.

Frequently asked questions

What counts as an "eyesore" when selling a NJ home?

Anything necessary that pulls the eye away from the room's best features: radiators and baseboard heaters, thermostats and alarm keypads, TV cords and power strips, window air conditioners, exposed basement pipes and ductwork, litter boxes and pet gear, trash and recycling bins, and the laundry area. None of these are defects, but in listing photos and showings they compete with the things buyers should be noticing — the light, the layout, the molding, the kitchen.

Should I paint my radiators before listing my NJ home?

Yes, if they're chipped, rusty, or a different color than the wall. A coat of high-heat radiator paint in the wall color (or the trim color) makes a cast-iron radiator recede visually and reads as well-maintained. Buyers in Maplewood, Montclair, South Orange, and Westfield expect radiators in older homes — what they don't want is a radiator that looks neglected. Radiator covers are a second option, but a clean, painted radiator usually looks better than a cheap cover.

How do I hide TV cords and cables for showings and listing photos?

For a wall-mounted TV, an in-wall cord kit runs power and HDMI behind the drywall for a clean look and costs under $50 at a hardware store. If you don't want to open the wall, a paintable cord raceway painted to match the wall is nearly invisible. Bundle everything behind the console with velcro ties, and plug into a power strip hidden inside the cabinet. Visible cords are the single most common thing I edit out of a room before photos.

What should I do with pet items during showings in NJ?

Take the litter box, crate, food bowls, and toys out of the main living areas and bathrooms before every showing — into the garage, a closed closet, or the car. If the litter box can't leave the house, put it in a furniture-style enclosure in the least-used room and keep it spotless. Many NJ buyers are allergic or simply uneasy about pets, and one visible litter box makes them sniff the air for the rest of the walkthrough.

Is it worth hiding eyesores if buyers will see them at inspection anyway?

Absolutely. Nothing here is about concealing a defect — the radiator, the panel, and the pipes are all still there and disclosed. This is about presentation. Buyers form their opinion of a home's value in the first few minutes based on what their eye lands on. If it lands on the light and the layout, they see a home worth the asking price. If it lands on a tangle of cords and a rusty radiator, they start mentally discounting before they've seen the second floor.
