The Price You Feel Like You Should Get vs. The Price That Gets You The Most Money

There are two numbers every NJ seller has in their head when they sit down with an agent. One is what they feel like their home should be worth — based on what they paid, what they've put into it, what their neighbor got three years ago, and what their gut says. The other is what buyers in today's market will actually pay.

The gap between those two numbers is where sellers lose money. Not because they are greedy — they are not. Because the market does not care what you paid for the house in 2018 or what the basement renovation cost. It only cares about what comparable homes are selling for right now, in your neighborhood, in current condition, to buyers with today's financing costs.

My job is to close that gap — to show you the actual data and help you arrive at the number that will get you the most money in the shortest time. That is not always the highest number. Almost never, in fact.

Why Overpricing Costs You More Than Underpricing

Every experienced NJ agent knows this. Most sellers do not believe it until they live it. So let me walk through the math.

Scenario A: Overpriced by 8%

Market value: $850,000. List price: $919,000 ("to have room to negotiate").

What actually happens: The home gets traffic in weeks one and two because it's new. Buyers see it, compare it to other active listings, and move on. No offers. By week four, the listing has accumulated 28 days on market. Buyers start asking what is wrong with it. Showings drop. The seller reduces to $885,000. Still no offers. Reduces again to $855,000 in week eight. Gets one offer at $830,000 from a buyer who knows they have leverage now — the home has been on market for 60 days. Seller accepts at $830,000.

Result: $20,000 below market value, 60+ days of showings, two price reductions, and a stigmatized listing.

Scenario B: Priced at Market

Market value: $850,000. List price: $849,900.

What actually happens: The listing goes live Thursday evening. Zillow, Redfin, and every buyer's saved search pings with the new listing. Showings fill up over the weekend. Four buyers submit offers by Sunday evening. Competitive situation drives the final sale to $872,000 — above asking price, waived inspection contingency, 30-day close.

Result: $22,000 above market value, 10-day process, no price reductions, clean close.

The difference between those two outcomes is not the house. It is the price. The first seller got $830,000. The second got $872,000. A $42,000 swing on an identical home. This is not theory. This is a pattern I watch play out across NJ every single year.

How to Actually Determine What Your NJ Home Is Worth

Step 1: Ignore Zillow as Your Primary Source

Zillow's Zestimate is a useful starting point and a terrible ending point. In NJ markets — especially in high-turnover commuter towns like Summit, Chatham, and Maplewood — Zestimate accuracy ranges from pretty close to wildly off. I have seen Zestimates that were $150,000 below market because Zillow did not pick up a recent renovation. I have seen them $80,000 above market because a neighboring sale had unusual circumstances Zillow could not contextualize. Use it to get a rough range. Stop there.

Step 2: Look at Actual Closed Sales — Not Active Listings

Active listings are competition, not comparables. What matters is what homes actually sold for in the last 60–90 days. In NJ, "list price" and "sale price" can diverge significantly in both directions. The only number that matters is the price recorded at closing. Your agent should be pulling closed sales data from MLS — not Zillow, not estimates, actual recorded transactions.

Step 3: Make the Right Comparisons

Good comparables match your home on the things that actually drive price in NJ: square footage, bedroom and bathroom count, lot size, school district, and condition. A four-bedroom Colonial in Westfield does not compare to a four-bedroom Colonial in Cranford. Same bedroom count, completely different market. Block-level factors matter too — proximity to train, school quality on that specific street, noise, lot configuration.

The comparables that matter: Same town, same general neighborhood, same approximate size, same bedroom count, sold within 90 days, similar condition and improvements. If you can't find three strong comps that tick all those boxes, you need someone who knows the market well enough to bridge the gaps — not someone who guesses.

Step 4: Account for Current Market Conditions

A comp from 12 months ago is not the same as a comp from 60 days ago, especially in a market that has been appreciating at 6–10% annually. Your agent should be applying a market adjustment to older comps and weighting the most recent sales most heavily. In a declining or softening market, the adjustment goes the other way. The NJ commuter corridor has generally held up well in 2026, but individual neighborhoods vary.

The First Two Weeks Are Everything

In New Jersey's commuter markets, the first two weeks a home is on market are the highest-traffic, highest-interest period in the entire listing. Every buyer in your price range who has been waiting for inventory gets notified when your home goes live. That initial burst of interest is the most valuable thing you have.

If you price correctly, that burst converts to showings, showings convert to offers, and competing offers drive the price up. If you price too high, the burst produces showings but no offers, and the buyers move on — often to other homes that come on market the following week. You cannot recreate that initial burst. Once it is spent, it is gone.

📊
The 14-day rule: If you have had 10+ showings and no offers in the first two weeks, the price is wrong. Not wrong by a little — wrong enough that buyers are walking after seeing the home in person. A price reduction at this point should be meaningful (3–5%), not cosmetic ($5,000 on a $900,000 listing).
📊
The zero-showings signal: If you have had fewer than five showings in the first two weeks, the price is likely so far above market that buyers are filtering it out online before ever requesting a showing. This requires an immediate and significant correction, not a wait-and-see approach.

NJ-Specific Pricing Factors Most Sellers Underestimate

Property Taxes Are Part of the Price Conversation

NJ property taxes are the highest in the country. Every buyer is doing a monthly cost calculation that includes PITI: principal, interest, taxes, and insurance. A home with $20,000 in annual property taxes adds roughly $1,667/month to a buyer's fixed costs, on top of the mortgage. When you price your home, you are effectively pricing the all-in cost of homeownership. That matters more in NJ than in any other state.

School District Carries Pricing Power

In the NJ commuter corridor, a school district boundary can mean $50,000–$100,000 in price difference between two otherwise comparable homes. Summit school district vs. a neighboring town. Chatham schools vs. Florham Park schools on the border. Buyers with children know exactly which district they are targeting, and they will not cross a school boundary to get a lower price. Price accordingly.

Train Access Is a Multiplier in NJ Commuter Markets

Walkability to a Midtown Direct station is worth real money to NJ buyers. The closer to the station, the higher the premium — up to a point. A home 3 blocks from the Summit train is priced differently than a home 15 minutes away by car. If you have walkable train access, it belongs in the pricing conversation and in your marketing.

Frequently Asked Questions: NJ Home Pricing

What is the biggest pricing mistake NJ home sellers make?

Overpricing. Every year I watch sellers list above market because they want to "have room to negotiate." Overpriced homes in NJ sit. Buyers in 2026 are sophisticated — they see every comparable in real time and they know when a price is off. A home that sits for 30+ days starts to carry a stigma: buyers assume something is wrong. By the time the seller reduces to market, the urgency that drives multiple offers has evaporated.

How do I find out what my NJ home is actually worth in 2026?

The most accurate method is a Comparative Market Analysis (CMA) run by a licensed NJ real estate agent who knows your specific market. A CMA looks at homes that actually sold in your area in the last 60–90 days — similar size, condition, location, and features. Zillow estimates can be $50,000–$150,000 off in both directions in NJ markets because they do not account for specific block-level factors, condition, or recent improvements.

Should I price my NJ home high and leave room to negotiate?

No. This strategy consistently produces worse outcomes than pricing at market. In active NJ markets — Summit, Chatham, Westfield, Maplewood — homes priced correctly generate multiple offers within the first two weeks, which creates competitive pressure that drives prices up. Homes priced above market do not generate that competition. They sit, collect days-on-market stigma, and then sell for less than they would have at a correct initial price.

What does 'days on market' mean for NJ home pricing?

Days on market (DOM) is one of the most powerful pricing signals in NJ real estate. In the current market, well-priced homes in commuter towns go under contract in 7–21 days. Once a home has been on market for 30+ days, buyers start asking what is wrong with it. After 45+ days, even if you reduce to the correct price, you have burned through the initial burst of buyer interest that drives competitive offers. Getting the price right at launch is worth far more than any later reduction strategy.

Can I underprice my NJ home to create a bidding war?

Strategic underpricing — listing slightly below market to generate competition — can work in high-demand NJ markets like Summit, Chatham, and Maplewood where buyer demand is strong and inventory is tight. It requires real confidence in the market and typically works best in spring and early fall when buyer traffic is highest. In slower markets or slower seasons, underpricing just means you sell for less. I use this strategy selectively based on market conditions, competition, and the specific home.
