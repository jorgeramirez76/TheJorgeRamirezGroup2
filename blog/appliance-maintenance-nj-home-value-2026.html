Appliance Maintenance NJ Homeowners Skip — And Why It Costs Thousands at Closing

The dryer vent you haven't cleaned since last spring. The HVAC filter that's been in since early 2025. The dishwasher door seal that's started to smell. These items show up on every NJ home inspection report — and buyers use them to negotiate your price down.

Why appliance maintenance is a real estate issue, not just a home issue

I've walked through hundreds of pre-listing inspections across Westfield, Chatham, Montclair, Florham Park, Summit, Cranford, and every other market I serve in NJ. There are things that show up on almost every report. The failed HVAC filter. The dryer vent packed with lint. The water heater with a corroded anode rod that's one season from failing. The dishwasher that leaves a film because the filter hasn't been cleaned in two years.

Here's what makes these items different from cosmetic issues: buyers and their agents know exactly what they mean. A dirty HVAC filter tells them the system hasn't been maintained. A clogged dryer vent gets flagged as a fire hazard — the one category that buyers don't negotiate around, they require resolution. A water heater at the end of its service life goes straight onto a request-for-credit list.

Individual items seem small. Together, they build a narrative about how the home has been maintained — and that narrative follows the property through every offer, every counter, and every concession conversation. Sellers who go into inspection with a clean appliance maintenance record lose that leverage point entirely. That's worth more than the cost of a few service calls.

Jorge's rule: I tell every seller the same thing: the inspection is not a pass/fail test, it's a negotiation document. Every item on that report is a bullet point in the buyer's request-for-credit letter. Your job before listing is to eliminate as many bullet points as possible — and appliance maintenance is some of the lowest-cost elimination you can do.

The appliance maintenance tasks NJ sellers most often skip

  1. Replace the HVAC filter — and have the system serviced A visibly dirty filter tells an inspector the system has been neglected. Replace the filter before listing with a new one (cost: $15–$40 depending on size and MERV rating) and schedule a professional HVAC tune-up ($80–$150). Get a service receipt. When buyers ask about the HVAC — and they always do — you can hand them documentation of recent professional maintenance. That's a negotiating position, not a question mark.
    Cost: $100–$200 total · Inspection protection: high
  2. Clean the dryer vent — all the way to the exterior This is the one that catches sellers most off guard. Lint builds up inside the dryer vent duct over time, and inspectors flag a blocked vent as a fire hazard. Not as a maintenance note — as a safety issue. Professional dryer vent cleaning runs $80–$120 and takes about an hour. Do it before listing. Have the technician check the exterior termination point as well — a clogged cap is just as much of an issue as a clogged duct.
    Cost: $80–$120 · Inspection protection: critical
  3. Run a dishwasher cleaning cycle and check the filter Most NJ homeowners don't know their dishwasher has a filter that needs to be cleaned monthly. Pull the bottom rack, unscrew the filter assembly at the bottom of the tub, and rinse it under running water. Then run a cleaning cycle with a dishwasher cleaner tablet. A dishwasher that smells or leaves a film on dishes is a showing problem before it's an inspection problem — buyers notice during walkthroughs.
    Cost: Under $10 · Impact: Showing and inspection
  4. Check the water heater age and condition Every home inspector notes the water heater's age and visible condition. In NJ, a water heater older than 10–12 years is considered approaching end of service life, and inspectors say so in writing. If yours is in that range, buyers will ask for a credit. If the anode rod is corroded or the sacrificial rod has never been replaced, the unit may be showing external signs of that wear. Know the age, and be prepared for the conversation.
    Awareness: Check the label on the tank for manufacture date
  5. Clean refrigerator coils and check door seals Condenser coils clogged with dust make the refrigerator run harder and louder, and inspectors notice both. Pull the refrigerator away from the wall and vacuum the coils on the back or bottom (depending on the model). Then check the door gaskets — they should seal firmly with no cracks, tears, or gaps. A failing door seal makes the unit cycle constantly, which inspectors can sometimes hear. A simple dollar-bill test: close the door on a dollar bill; if it pulls out without resistance, the seal needs attention.
    Cost: Free (DIY) · Time: 20 minutes
  6. Test every appliance that conveys with the sale The inspector will. Before they do, you should. Run every burner on the stove. Run the oven through a bake cycle and check that it holds temperature. Run the dishwasher through a full cycle and verify it drains completely. Test the garbage disposal — run it with water and listen for unusual grinding or vibration. A disposal that sounds rough is a $90 replacement that buyers will try to leverage for $500 in credit.
    Time: One afternoon · Benefit: No surprises on inspection day

The compounding cost of deferred appliance maintenance

Here's the math that sellers rarely think about until they're sitting across from a buyer's agent with an inspection report in hand. Each appliance item on that report represents a buyer's request. Individually, a dryer vent cleaning is $100. On an inspection report, it becomes a $300–$500 credit request because buyers don't want to deal with it themselves and factor in their time and uncertainty. A water heater that's 11 years old might cost $900–$1,200 to replace. On an inspection report, buyers ask for $1,500–$2,000 because they're worried about timing and inconvenience.

The sellers who come out ahead are the ones who made the $100 dryer vent call six months before listing. They eliminated the line item entirely. The cost of prevention is almost always a fraction of the cost of concession — and in the NJ markets I work, where buyers are sophisticated and inspection reports are thorough, every item you hand them is leverage you're giving away.

This isn't about perfection. A 20-year-old NJ home will have a 20-year-old NJ home inspection. The goal isn't a blank report — it's showing buyers that the major systems and appliances have been actively maintained. That story matters as much as the inspection findings themselves.

Frequently asked questions

What appliance issues do NJ home inspectors flag most often?

The most common appliance findings on NJ inspection reports are dirty or failed HVAC filters, blocked dryer vents (flagged as fire hazards), dishwashers with deteriorated door seals or standing water, refrigerators with damaged gaskets or ice maker leaks, and water heaters approaching end of service life. These are almost always preventable with routine maintenance.

Does a failed appliance affect a NJ home sale?

Yes — and the impact multiplies. A failed appliance on an inspection report gives buyers grounds to request a repair credit or price reduction. Sellers who go into inspection with no appliance issues lose that leverage point entirely, which protects both the sale price and the transaction timeline.

How often should I replace HVAC filters in a NJ home?

Every 1–3 months depending on filter type, whether you have pets, and whether anyone has allergies. Many NJ homeowners on a 90-day replacement cycle discover at listing time that it's been eight months since the last change. A visibly dirty filter signals to buyers that system maintenance has been neglected.

Should I service the HVAC before listing my NJ home?

Yes, always. An HVAC service call ($80–$150) before listing gives you documentation that the system has been professionally maintained. Inspectors check whether the system runs properly, and buyers always ask about age and service history. Sellers who can produce service records are in a far stronger negotiating position.

Is dryer vent cleaning worth doing before listing a NJ home?

Absolutely. A clogged dryer vent is one of the most commonly flagged items in NJ home inspections, noted as a fire hazard — the one category buyers don't negotiate around, they require remediation. A professional dryer vent cleaning runs $80–$120 and removes the item from the report entirely.
