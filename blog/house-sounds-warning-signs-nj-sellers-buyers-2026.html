6 House Sounds NJ Sellers and Buyers Should Not Ignore

A house talks. Before you list, and before you write an offer, it pays to know which sounds are ordinary and which ones mean pick up the phone today.

Why sounds matter in a sale

Most homeowners tune out the noises their house makes. Buyers do not. On a showing in Cranford or Montclair a buyer is quiet, listening, and a clank from the basement or a scratch from the attic lands harder than any scuff on a wall. Home inspectors listen too, and a sound is often the first sign of a condition that becomes a repair request or a renegotiation later.

I am not a plumber, electrician or pest professional, and this article is not a substitute for one. What it does is sort the common sounds so you know who to call and how urgent the call is. If you are preparing a home, pair it with the maintenance triage plan. If you are buying, keep it with your inspection and offer guide.

The six sounds and what they usually mean

  • Banging or knocking when a faucet or washer shuts off. Often called water hammer, this is a pressure surge in the supply lines, sometimes made worse by loose pipe straps or high water pressure. It is usually a plumber visit, not an emergency, but it stresses joints and fittings over time and buyers hear it every time they test a tap.
  • Hissing near a gas appliance, meter or line. Treat this as urgent. Leave the house, do not flip switches, and call your gas utility's emergency number from outside. A hiss from a radiator valve or a refrigerator is a different matter, but you do not diagnose that by guessing.
  • Scratching, skittering or gnawing in walls, ceilings or the attic, mostly at night. That is almost always mice, squirrels or something larger finding a way in through an old soffit, a gap at the roofline or a missing vent screen. Pre-war homes in Maplewood, Westfield and Summit have plenty of those entry points. A licensed pest professional finds the opening; sealing it matters as much as trapping.
  • Buzzing, crackling or sizzling from an outlet, switch or the panel. Stop using that circuit and call a licensed electrician. Loose connections and overloaded circuits are fire risks, and this is exactly the kind of finding I cover in the electrical warning signs guide.
  • Gurgling from a drain or a toilet that bubbles when another fixture runs. This points to a venting problem or a partial blockage in the drain or main sewer line. Older clay or cast-iron sewer laterals in towns like Cranford, Scotch Plains and Bloomfield are a common source. A camera inspection of the line tells you whether it is roots, a belly or a break.
  • A boom, rumble or delayed whoosh when the furnace or boiler starts. Delayed ignition means gas or oil builds up before it lights. That needs a heating technician before the season starts, and it is a reminder to test every carbon monoxide alarm. The CPSC carbon monoxide information center explains placement and replacement schedules.

Two sounds that are usually fine: the tick of baseboard heat expanding, and a hot-water pipe pinging as it warms. Mention them to a buyer anyway. A known, ordinary noise stops being a question mark once it has a name.

What sellers should do before photos and showings

Walk the house at night with the television off. Run each faucet and shut it hard. Flush every toilet while a sink runs. Stand near the furnace or boiler at a cold start. Write down what you hear and where, then book the right trade for each item on the list. Keep the invoices and service reports in the folder you hand to your agent and attorney.

Do not mask a sound for a showing. A sewer line that gurgles will do it during the inspection, and New Jersey's seller disclosure statement asks about known conditions in plumbing, heating, electrical and pests. Fixing the item and disclosing the repair is the stronger position. An oil-fired boiler with a rough start also raises the question of the tank, which is why I point sellers to the oil tank sweep guide early.

What buyers should do on a showing and after

Ask to run water and flush a toilet. Stand in the basement for a minute. If you hear any of the six sounds, note it for your inspector and ask for a sewer camera scope and a heating system evaluation as part of the inspection contingency. In Hoboken and Jersey City condos, listen for the same knocking and hissing in the shared risers and ask the association what has been serviced.

A sound is information, not a reason to walk away. Priced repairs are negotiable. Surprises after closing are not.

Questions about house sounds and a sale

Which house sound needs immediate attention?

A hissing sound near a gas appliance, meter or line. Leave the house, avoid switches and flames, and call the gas utility's emergency line from outside. Buzzing or crackling from an outlet or panel is next: stop using the circuit and call a licensed electrician.

Do I have to disclose noisy pipes when selling in NJ?

New Jersey's seller disclosure statement asks about known conditions in plumbing, heating, electrical and pest systems. Discuss what you know with your agent and attorney, and keep the receipts for any repair so the disclosure is accurate and complete.

Is water hammer a serious problem?

It is usually a plumbing service call rather than an emergency, but repeated pressure surges stress fittings and joints. A plumber can add arrestors, secure loose pipes or adjust water pressure. Buyers notice it every time they test a faucet.

Should a buyer ask for a sewer camera inspection?

If drains gurgle, toilets bubble or the home is older with a clay or cast-iron lateral, yes. A camera scope is a common add-on to a New Jersey home inspection and shows roots, bellies or breaks before you commit to the repair cost.

Will fixing these sounds raise my sale price?

This article does not claim a price effect. Servicing the systems removes inspection findings and questions, and a documented repair is easier to explain than a noise a buyer hears on the first visit.
