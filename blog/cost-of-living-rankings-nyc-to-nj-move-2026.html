Cost-of-Living Rankings and the NYC-to-NJ Move: What the Lists Miss

House Outlook’s newsletter this week linked a Daily Passport roundup of the ten U.S. cities with the highest cost of living, drawn from Numbeo’s database of more than sixty major North American cities. I am not going to argue with where any city landed. Every week a buyer from Manhattan, Brooklyn or Queens sends me a list like this and asks whether New Jersey is “cheaper.” Here is how to read the list, what it cannot tell you, and the five numbers that actually answer the question.

The list House Outlook linked this week

This week’s House Outlook newsletter pointed to a Daily Passport piece ranking the ten U.S. cities with the highest cost of living. The source was Numbeo, a crowd-sourced cost-of-living database, which compared more than sixty major North American cities. Lists like this tell you the most expensive cities are expensive. What the list cannot do is tell a family in a two-bedroom rental in Brooklyn whether a house in Cranford or a condo in Jersey City costs them less per month.

What a cost-of-living index measures, and what it leaves out

Numbeo-style indexes combine user-reported prices for groceries, restaurant meals, transit, utilities and rent, and they describe a whole city as one number. They do not measure the property tax bill on a specific house, the cost and length of a particular commute, whether you will need to buy a car, how your income taxes change when you cross the river, homeowners insurance, or the maintenance a house needs and an apartment does not. For a buyer, those are the whole decision.

The five numbers that actually decide the move

  1. The full monthly housing payment, not the price. Mortgage principal and interest, property taxes, insurance and any HOA fee. New Jersey property taxes vary from town to town and house to house, so use the actual bill on the listing, never a town average. The tax rate versus what you pay guide explains why those two numbers differ.
  2. The commute, in dollars and hours. A monthly NJ Transit or PATH pass, station parking. Midtown Direct towns like Summit, Chatham, Millburn, Maplewood, South Orange and Montclair, Northeast Corridor towns like Metuchen, Edison, Woodbridge and Rahway, and PATH towns like Hoboken and Jersey City all price differently. The commute cost guide breaks it down.
  3. Income taxes. New York City residents pay a city income tax that ends when they move. New Jersey residents who work in New York generally file in both states with a credit. The net result depends on your income and situation, so have an accountant run it before you sign anything.
  4. The car. Most households leaving the city for a suburb add at least one car, with insurance, fuel and maintenance attached. In Hoboken, Jersey City or a walkable downtown like Morristown you may still skip it.
  5. Space per dollar, and what the space replaces. Compare bedrooms, square feet and yard against what the same money buys in the city, and add what changes with them, from childcare to a home office.

Jorge’s tip: Build a one-page side-by-side. Left column: your current rent or carrying cost, transit, parking and city taxes. Right column: the full monthly on two or three specific New Jersey listings, with the commute and a car added. I do this with every relocating buyer. It also ends the “is New Jersey cheaper” argument, because the answer becomes a number for a specific house.

How different NJ towns land on that worksheet

Hoboken and Jersey City sit closest to city pricing, with the shortest commute and the best odds of staying car-free. Maplewood, South Orange and Montclair trade higher property taxes for a Midtown Direct train and a full-size house with a yard. Westfield, Cranford, Summit and Scotch Plains fall along the Raritan Valley and Morris and Essex lines. Edison, Woodbridge and Metuchen on the Northeast Corridor tend to deliver more house per dollar with a longer ride. Which column wins depends on the house, not the town’s reputation, and the commuter towns guide and affordable towns guide are the place to start narrowing.

What the rankings get right

The cities at the top of these lists are expensive for everyone, including the buyers leaving them. New York households arrive in New Jersey with savings and equity scaled to Manhattan prices, and they compete hard. If you are one of them, the bidding war guide explains how offers are actually won here. If you are a seller in Union, Essex, Morris or Hudson County, that buyer pool is a real part of your market this fall.

Questions about cost of living and moving from New York to New Jersey

Is it cheaper to live in New Jersey than in New York City?

For most households moving from a Manhattan or Brooklyn rental to a New Jersey suburb, the housing cost per square foot drops, and property taxes, commuting and often a car come in. Whether the total is lower depends on the specific house and commute, so run the numbers on real listings rather than a city ranking.

Which NJ towns are the least expensive for NYC commuters?

Generally the towns farther along the rail lines and away from Midtown Direct service, such as Rahway, Linden, Woodbridge and Edison on the Northeast Corridor, or Bloomfield and Nutley in Essex County. Verify the actual tax bill and the real commute for each listing before deciding.

How do New Jersey property taxes compare with New York City?

New Jersey property taxes are high by national standards and vary widely by town and by assessment. New York City owners pay property taxes too, plus a city income tax. Compare the actual bill on a specific listing against your full New York tax picture with an accountant.

Do I still pay New York taxes if I live in New Jersey and work in Manhattan?

Generally you file a New York non-resident return on New York-source wages and New Jersey gives you a credit, while the New York City resident income tax stops. The specifics depend on your situation and change over time, so confirm with a tax professional before you decide.

Should I rent in New Jersey first before buying?

It is reasonable if you do not know the towns. A one-year lease in Hoboken, Jersey City or Montclair lets you test the commute and the lifestyle. The trade-off is buying a year later in a market that has moved and moving twice, which is why many of my relocating buyers tour towns on weekends instead.
