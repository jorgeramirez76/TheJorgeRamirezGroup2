8 Things NJ Home Buyers Miss at Showings (That Cost Them Later)

Published  ·  July 2026

Most NJ buyers walk a house and focus on what they like — the kitchen layout, the yard, whether their furniture fits. Here's what experienced buyers check instead: the eight details that reveal expensive problems before you're under contract and locked into the deal.

The Problem With Most Home Tours

I've worked full time in New Jersey real estate since 2017. The pattern is almost always the same. First-time buyers walk in and fall in love with the kitchen countertops. They mentally place their furniture in the living room. They start talking about which bedroom is for which kid. They make an emotional decision before they've looked at a single thing that actually matters for the long-term cost of owning the house.

Then the inspection report comes back. And suddenly they're staring at a 22-year-old furnace, evidence of past basement flooding, and a Federal Pacific electrical panel — and trying to figure out how to negotiate $30,000 off a deal they're emotionally committed to at a price they already stretched to reach.

The kitchen countertops are nice. But they're also replaceable. A failing HVAC system, a wet basement, or a problematic electrical panel are not cheap fixes and they are not cosmetic. They affect the actual cost of ownership, and they come up at every inspection in NJ's aging housing stock.

Here's what to look for the next time you walk through a house — before you decide you love it.

8 Things to Check at Every NJ House Showing

1 HVAC Age and Condition Replacement: $8,000–$15,000

Ask the listing agent how old the furnace and central AC unit are. Most NJ homes have the HVAC equipment in the basement or utility room — look at the manufacturer labels yourself. A furnace with more than 20 years on it is at end of life. Central AC over 15 years is in the same position. These aren't deal-breakers, but they're negotiating points. And if a seller claims "it runs great" about a 24-year-old furnace, that's not a selling feature — it's deferred maintenance that landed in your lap.

Why It Matters:

A full HVAC system replacement in NJ — furnace plus central AC — typically runs $8,000–$15,000 depending on system size and whether ductwork needs attention. That number belongs in your financial model before you make an offer, not after you're already in contract.

2 Basement Moisture and Water History Waterproofing: $5,000–$25,000

NJ's housing stock skews old — many homes in Union, Essex, and Morris County were built between 1920 and 1970, before modern waterproofing standards. Walk the basement and look at the walls. White chalky deposits (efflorescence) mean water has been moving through the foundation. Rust stains at the base of the walls mean the same. Water lines at different heights on the walls mean it's happened more than once. A sump pump with a backup battery in a corner is usually a sign someone is managing a water problem rather than having solved it.

Why It Matters:

Interior basement waterproofing in NJ ranges from $5,000 for a simple drain tile installation to $20,000–$25,000 for full perimeter drainage systems. If the seller discloses no water issues but you see clear evidence of moisture history, that discrepancy is worth a direct question and a clause in the contract.

3 The Electrical Panel Replacement: $2,500–$4,500

Find the electrical panel. It's usually in the basement, utility closet, or garage. Look at the brand. Federal Pacific (Stab-Lok) and Zinsco panels are both safety concerns that show up routinely in NJ's older homes and are flagged by virtually every home inspector. They are not banned — you can buy a home with one — but they are fire hazards according to electrical safety research, and most buyers eventually replace them. Aluminum wiring throughout the home (common in NJ homes from the 1960s–70s) is a related concern worth discussing with an electrician.

Why It Matters:

Panel replacement runs $2,500–$4,500 in New Jersey. More important than the cost is the insurance implication — some NJ insurance carriers won't write a policy on a home with a Federal Pacific panel, or will require replacement as a condition of coverage. Find out before you're in contract.

4 Water Pressure at Multiple Fixtures

Turn on a faucet in the kitchen. Then go turn on the shower in the master bath. If the shower pressure drops significantly when the kitchen is running, you may have a supply line issue or a pressure problem. Also flush a toilet while the shower is running. Extreme temperature or pressure swings indicate plumbing issues that are worth investigating. In older NJ homes, galvanized steel supply pipes are common — they corrode from the inside over decades, reducing water pressure and eventually failing. Replacement to copper or PEX is a significant project.

Why It Matters:

Plumbing supply line replacement in an older NJ home can run $5,000–$15,000 depending on the size of the home and accessibility. Low pressure that you notice at the showing will still be there after closing.

5 The Attic and Roof from the Exterior

Walk the perimeter of the home and look up at the roofline. Sagging ridgeline, visible missing shingles, and granules collecting in the gutters are all signs of roof age and wear. If you can get into the attic — or ask that the inspector make it a priority — look for daylight coming through, signs of animal intrusion, and the condition of the insulation. Ask how old the roof is and get the name of any roofing contractor the seller has used. An NJ home inspection will assess the roof, but roof condition visible from the ground is worth noting before you fall in love with the house.

Why It Matters:

A full roof replacement in NJ typically runs $12,000–$25,000 for a standard colonial depending on square footage and materials. Buyers who miss this step are often negotiating credits after the inspection for costs they could have anticipated at the showing.

6 Foundation Cracks — Horizontal vs. Vertical

Walk around the outside of the home and look at the foundation. Vertical hairline cracks are common in NJ's clay soils and often cosmetic — they result from normal concrete curing and minor settlement. Horizontal cracks or step cracks in block foundations are more serious. Horizontal cracking in a poured concrete or block foundation can indicate lateral pressure from soil — a structural issue that gets expensive to address. If you see horizontal cracks at the showing, flag it for your inspector and consider getting a structural engineer's opinion before you finalize your offer price.

Why It Matters:

Structural foundation repair in NJ can range from $5,000 for wall anchor installation to $30,000+ for major underpinning work. Cosmetic crack sealing is $500–$1,500. Knowing the difference before the inspection saves you from a post-inspection shock.

7 Window Condition and Age

Check the windows — not just for drafts (hard to feel in summer), but for fogging between the panes. Fogged or cloudy insulated glass means the seal has failed and the insulating argon gas has escaped. The window still functions, but it no longer provides its rated insulation value. Check multiple windows on different floors and exposures. In NJ's older homes, single-pane windows or original wood-frame windows are common and expensive to replace — budget $600–$1,200 per window for quality replacements with installation.

Why It Matters:

A full window replacement on a 4-bedroom NJ colonial — 20–30 windows — can easily run $15,000–$25,000. This is a predictable cost that's often visible at a showing if you know what to look for. It's also a serious energy efficiency consideration for NJ's cold winters.

8 The Neighborhood at Different Times

This one isn't about the house. If you see a property you're serious about at a Sunday afternoon open house, come back on a Tuesday morning and a Friday evening. NJ neighborhoods behave differently depending on the day and time. A quiet Sunday afternoon can hide a commuter rat-run that comes alive at 7:30 AM. A tree-lined street in summer can be a noise corridor from a nearby school when September arrives. Proximity to NJ Transit lines and rail crossings — which you might not hear on a Sunday — is worth a weekday check. I always recommend buyers drive the neighborhood at different times before making a final decision.

Why It Matters:

Neighborhood dynamics are not fixable after you close. A house can be renovated. A loud intersection or a difficult neighbor situation cannot. Spend the time before you're in contract — it's much cheaper than regret at 7:45 AM on your first Monday as a homeowner.

How This Translates to Your Offer Strategy

Everything on this list is observable at the showing — before you make an offer, before you're under contract, before you're emotionally committed to closing. That's the whole point. A buyer who walks out of a showing knowing that the furnace is 23 years old, the basement has past moisture, and the electrical panel is Federal Pacific is a buyer who can make an informed offer. They can price in the deferred maintenance, ask for a credit before negotiations get heated, or make a completely different decision about whether the home makes sense at the seller's number.

This doesn't mean you're looking for reasons to walk away from every house. It means you're gathering information that belongs in your financial picture before you make a six-figure decision. The inspection will find everything — but by the time the inspection happens, you're already emotionally invested and financially committed to the deal. Walking in informed is always better.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I look for when touring a home in NJ?

Beyond the layout and finishes, check HVAC age, basement for moisture evidence (efflorescence, rust stains, sump pump), electrical panel brand (Federal Pacific and Zinsco are safety flags), water pressure at multiple simultaneous fixtures, foundation crack direction (horizontal is more serious than vertical), roof condition from the exterior, window seal integrity, and neighborhood conditions at multiple times of day and week.

How old should an HVAC be before I ask for a credit in NJ?

A furnace over 20 years and central AC over 15 years are approaching end of life and are appropriate negotiating points in an NJ home purchase. Replacement costs run $4,000–$8,000 for a furnace and $4,000–$7,000 for AC in New Jersey. Request a credit, a price reduction, or a seller-paid replacement as part of your inspection response — your agent can advise on what's customary in your specific market.

Are Federal Pacific electrical panels dangerous in NJ homes?

Federal Pacific Stab-Lok panels, common in NJ homes built between 1950 and 1990, are considered a fire safety concern by electrical safety experts and most home inspectors. They are not to code for new installation. Some NJ insurance carriers won't write policies on homes with these panels. Replacement typically costs $2,500–$4,500. If you see one during a showing, note it for your inspector and check with your insurance broker before finalizing your offer.

What does efflorescence in a NJ basement mean?

Efflorescence — white chalky deposits on basement walls — means water has been moving through the foundation walls. It's not necessarily active flooding, but it is moisture history worth investigating. In NJ's older housing stock, this is common and warrants a follow-up with a waterproofing specialist. Basement waterproofing in NJ ranges from $5,000 for a basic drain tile system to $25,000+ for full interior drainage.

How do I negotiate repairs after an NJ home inspection?

NJ buyers typically have an inspection contingency that allows negotiation of repairs, credits, or price reductions. Be specific: request a credit based on an actual contractor estimate, not a general discount. Major structural, electrical, plumbing, and HVAC issues are fair game even in competitive markets. Cosmetic issues generally are not. Your agent should help you prioritize the items worth fighting for versus the ones to release — aggressive demands on minor items can blow up deals unnecessarily.
