Keep a Record of Your Home’s Paint Colors: The Small Habit That Pays Off When You Sell in NJ

House Outlook published a practical trick this week: record each room’s paint brand, color name and sheen somewhere you will actually find it, such as the back of a switch plate or inside a cabinet door. It is the kind of small habit that nobody thinks about until they need it, and sellers need it twice. Once when the nail holes get patched before the photographer comes, and again when a buyer in Chatham or Millburn stands in the living room at the final walkthrough and asks what color this is.

The House Outlook trick

The idea is to write the paint details where they live. Pull a switch or outlet cover plate, write the brand, color name, color code, sheen and the date on the back in permanent marker, and screw it back on. Some people use the inside of a closet door or the underside of a cabinet shelf instead. The point is that the record stays with the room, not in a notebook that gets lost in the move.

I would add one step for sellers: copy the same information into one page of the house handbook you leave for the buyer. The plate is for you. The page is for them.

Why it matters before you list

Touch-ups are the first thing that happens when a house gets ready for photos. Nail holes from the gallery wall, scuffs along the stair wall, the spot behind the door. With the color and sheen written down, a touch-up takes an afternoon. Without it, you are at the paint counter with a chip of drywall guessing between four whites, and the patch flashes in the photos because the sheen is wrong. Sellers in Westfield or Summit who painted three years ago rarely remember whether the hallway was eggshell or satin. The plate remembers.

It also tells you which rooms need a full repaint rather than a touch-up. If the record says the family room was painted in 2014, no touch-up will match, and that room goes on the repaint list instead.

  1. Walk every room with a marker and a screwdriver. Record what you know. For rooms you cannot identify, note that too; it is useful information when you decide what to repaint.
  2. Match the sheen, not just the color. Most touch-up failures are sheen failures. Eggshell over satin shows as a dull patch in daylight.
  3. Keep one labeled quart per color, not a basement of gallons. Buyers read a wall of rusted cans as deferred maintenance. Most New Jersey counties run household hazardous waste collection days for the rest; check your county’s schedule.
  4. Photograph the record. One phone picture per plate, filed by room, backs up the plate if a buyer replaces it.
  5. Add the exterior. Siding, trim, shutters and the front door, with the year. The front door color is the one buyers ask about most.

Jorge’s tip: If you are about to repaint for the sale, choose the color with the record in mind. A warm white from a major brand with a standard code is easy for the next owner to match and easy for you to touch up before closing. Buyers also respond to it. What to hand over at closing, and what you do not have to

New Jersey’s seller disclosure covers condition and known defects, not paint colors, so there is no requirement here. This is goodwill, and goodwill has value in a transaction. A buyer in Morristown or Montclair who gets a one-page paint list with the keys remembers the seller well, which matters if a small repair question comes up after closing. A buyer who spends a Saturday at the paint store with a chip of drywall remembers that too.

Keep it simple: room, brand, color name and code, sheen, year painted, and whether a labeled can is in the basement. Add the exterior colors and any stain used on the deck or fence. If the house has a homeowner association or sits in a historic district, note any approved exterior colors, because the buyer will need that approval record more than any other.

For buyers: ask for it

If you are buying, add paint colors to the list of questions for the seller alongside the appliance manuals and the alarm code. Ask during attorney review or at the walkthrough, when the seller is still in the house and can read the back of a plate. Most sellers do not have a record, and that is useful information too: it tells you a repaint is in your first-year budget. The guide to what stays with the house covers the rest of the handover.

Questions about paint records and selling a NJ home

Do I have to tell the buyer what paint colors are in the house?

No. New Jersey’s seller disclosure covers the property’s condition and known defects, not paint colors. Leaving a one-page list of colors, sheens and dates is a goodwill gesture that buyers appreciate, and it costs you an hour. Confirm anything disclosure-related with your attorney.

Where should I write down my home’s paint colors?

On the back of a switch or outlet cover plate in each room, inside a closet door or under a cabinet shelf, in permanent marker: brand, color name and code, sheen and date. Then copy the same details into one page for the buyer and photograph each plate as a backup.

Should I leave leftover paint cans for the buyer?

Leave one clean, labeled can per current color if it is still usable. Dispose of the rest; a basement full of old cans reads as deferred maintenance to buyers. Most New Jersey counties run household hazardous waste collection days for paint and solvents, so check your county’s schedule.

Can I touch up paint before listing photos or should I repaint?

Touch up if you have the exact color and sheen and the paint is only a few years old. If you are guessing at either, or the wall has faded, a touch-up will show as a patch in daylight and in photos. Repaint that room in a neutral instead and record the new color.

What should a house handbook for the buyer include besides paint?

Appliance manuals and model numbers, service records you actually have, the names of contractors who have worked on the house, utility and alarm details, exterior colors and stains, and any homeowner association or historic district approvals. Keep it factual; do not claim service or repairs you cannot document.
