Documents You Need to Sell an Inherited Property in New Jersey

To sell an inherited New Jersey property you need certified death certificates, the will, Letters Testamentary or Letters of Administration with a Surrogate's short certificate, the existing deed, a New Jersey inheritance tax waiver, and municipal items like the smoke and carbon monoxide certificate. The waiver is what releases the State's lien so title can transfer.

Last updated  ·  August 2026

This is the full checklist, in the order the documents actually get used, with the ones that stall closings flagged.

By Jorge Ramirez | Keller Williams Premier Properties | Updated August 2026 · NJ licensed agent since 2017, License #1754604

Why the paperwork sets the timeline, not the market

An inherited house in Union, Essex, Morris, Middlesex or Hudson County generally sells for what a comparable house sells for. What makes estate sales feel slow is not buyer demand. It is that three separate offices each have to hand you a document before a deed can be recorded: the registrar that issues the death certificate, the county Surrogate that proves who is allowed to sign, and the New Jersey Division of Taxation that releases the State's tax lien on the property.

Each of those runs on its own schedule, and none of them moves faster because you have a signed contract. That is the entire argument for collecting this list before the house goes on the market instead of after.

This is not legal or tax advice and it does not replace an estate attorney. It is the document list as it actually gets used at a New Jersey closing table, with State sources linked so you can verify anything here yourself. Forms and requirements change, so confirm the current version with the Division of Taxation or your attorney.

1. Certified copies of the death certificate

Almost every other document on this list depends on this one, so it comes first.

What you need is a certified copy, not a certification and not a photocopy. The New Jersey Department of Health draws the line clearly: a certified copy carries the issuing office's raised seal and is printed on State security paper, and it functions as a legal document. A certification, by contrast, comes on plain paper without that seal and says on its face that it is not valid for legal purposes (NJ Department of Health, Order a Vital Record). A title company will not accept the plain-paper version.

In practice the funeral director orders the first batch for the family at the time of death. If you need more later, they come from the State's Office of Vital Statistics and Registry, and the local registrar in the municipality where the death occurred also holds the record. The State publishes the application requirements, acceptable identification, and current fees on its site.

Why you need several

Institutions keep the copy you give them. They do not photocopy it and hand it back. Over the course of an estate sale you will surrender one to the Surrogate, one to the Division of Taxation with the waiver affidavit, one to the title company, one to each bank or brokerage holding an account, one to the mortgage servicer, and often one to the utility companies and the insurance carrier. Order more than you think you need at the start. Ordering a second round in the middle of an estate adds a wait you did not need to take, and it is the kind of delay that has no upside.

2. The original will

The will names the executor and directs where the proceeds go. It does not, by itself, give anyone the power to sign a listing agreement, a contract, or a deed. That power comes from the Surrogate's appointment described in the next section. This trips up a lot of heirs who assume that being named in the will is the same as having authority.

The Surrogate needs the original will, not a copy. A photocopy does not get admitted to probate in the ordinary way. If the original cannot be located, stop and call an estate attorney before you do anything else with the house, because that becomes a legal question rather than a paperwork question.

The will matters to the title company for a second reason: the buyer's attorney will read it to confirm that the executor has the authority to sell real property and that the sale is consistent with what the will directs. Many wills contain an express power of sale. Where one does not, the attorney will want to see how the estate is handling that.

3. Letters Testamentary, Letters of Administration, and the short certificate

New Jersey handles probate at the county level. You file with the Surrogate's Court of the county where the decedent lived at the time of death, not where the property sits and not where you live. Every one of the state's 21 counties has its own Surrogate, and the Judiciary publishes a statewide roster of county surrogates with contact information. If nobody is contesting the will, the entire matter stays in that county office.

Letters Testamentary — when there is a will

The Surrogate admits the will to probate and issues Letters Testamentary to the executor the will names. This is the document that converts “named in the will” into “authorized to act.” It is what allows you to sign the listing agreement, the contract of sale, and the deed on behalf of the estate.

Letters of Administration — when there is no will

With no will there is no named executor, so the Surrogate appoints an administrator and issues Letters of Administration instead. Who gets appointed follows an order of priority among the next of kin, and relatives with equal or higher priority are generally asked to renounce in writing. An administrator is also commonly required to post a surety bond, which an executor under a will frequently is not. The bond requirement is the single biggest practical difference between the two paths, and it is worth asking your Surrogate about early.

The short certificate — what the title company actually collects

A short certificate is a one-page certified document from the Surrogate stating that, as of the date printed on it, your appointment is in effect and has not been revoked. Letters prove you were appointed. The short certificate proves you are still the one who can sign, which is the question a title underwriter needs answered on the day of closing. Ask for several copies at the counter. Many institutions will not accept one beyond a certain age, so if the sale takes a while you may need a freshly dated certificate at closing. Your county Surrogate will tell you the per-copy cost.

When a buyer's attorney asks for the letters and a current short certificate, they are not questioning the family. They are protecting their client from a deed signed by someone who lacked authority, which is a title defect that surfaces years later and is expensive to fix. Handing the package over early removes an entire round of back-and-forth. The mechanics of the executor's role are covered in more depth in selling a house as executor in NJ.

4. The New Jersey inheritance tax waiver

This is the document that stops the most closings, and it is the one families are least likely to have heard of.

New Jersey's Transfer Inheritance Tax is not just a bill. It attaches as a lien to property the decedent owned as of the date of death, and that lien stays on the property until the tax is paid, secured, or formally released by the State (NJ Division of Taxation). A tax waiver is the State's written release of a specific asset from that lien. Until the waiver exists, the title company will not insure the buyer's ownership, and without title insurance the sale does not close. That is the mechanism, and it holds even when the estate owes nothing.

Form 0-1 is the waiver itself

The Division's executor guide is direct about two points: Form 0-1 is issued only by the Division of Taxation, and a transfer of real estate requires it (NJ Tax Guide: A Guide to Being an Executor). You cannot download it, complete it yourself, or produce a substitute at the closing table. It gets issued to you.

L-8 and L-9 are the two ways of asking

The L-8 and the L-9 are affidavits. They are how a Class A estate obtains a release without filing a full inheritance tax return. They cover different assets, and confusing them is the most common mistake in an estate sale.

  • Form L-8 is titled the Affidavit for Release of Non-Real Estate Assets: Class A Resident Decedents. It is self-executing, which is the important word. The Division's guide explains that the estate representative fills it out and takes or sends it directly to the bank, transfer agent or financial institution, with nothing filed with the Inheritance and Estate Tax Branch by an estate that qualifies to use it. It releases bank accounts, stock, and similar holdings. It does not touch real estate.
  • Form L-9 is the Resident Decedent Affidavit Requesting Real Property Tax Waiver. This is the real-estate one. It is not self-executing: you file it with the Inheritance and Estate Tax Branch, and the Branch issues you the Form 0-1 for the property. The Division's guide describes it as the form to use when all of the beneficiaries and the estate are exempt from tax. The Division publishes companion versions of the affidavit for other situations, including one for a decedent who was a legal resident of another state. Check which version applies to your facts on the Division's inheritance and estate tax forms page or with your attorney, since the form set is revised from time to time.

If the L-8 cleared the bank accounts easily, do not assume the house works the same way. The L-8 is a form you hand to an institution. The L-9 is a form you file with the State and then wait for a response to. Heirs who breeze through the bank paperwork are regularly caught off guard weeks later when the title company asks for a waiver nobody has applied for.

Who counts as a Class A beneficiary

Both affidavits turn on beneficiary class, so it is worth knowing where you sit. The Division's executor guide groups them this way: surviving spouses, parents, children, grandchildren and similar lineal relatives are Class A; brothers, sisters and children-in-law are Class C; and nieces, nephews, aunts, uncles, friends and unrelated people are Class D. Class C and Class D beneficiaries are subject to inheritance tax, with the details depending on class and amount.

The practical consequence for a home sale is about route, not rate. If every beneficiary is Class A, the estate can generally take the affidavit route to the 0-1. If there is a Class C or Class D beneficiary anywhere in the picture, the Division's guidance points toward filing a return first, and the waiver comes out of that process. That is a longer road than the affidavit route, because the Branch has to review the return before any waiver issues, and a return selected for audit takes longer still. I am not going to publish a number of weeks here, because processing times move and the only reliable figure is the one the Inheritance and Estate Tax Branch quotes you when you ask. Ask them, or ask the estate attorney, and start the tax side early either way.

Rates, exemption amounts, and form revisions change. Do not rely on this page or any other for the current numbers. Confirm them with the New Jersey Division of Taxation's Inheritance and Estate Tax Branch or with the attorney handling the estate.

5. The existing deed and the title search

Pull the current deed before you do anything else, because it answers a question that changes everything: does the estate even own this house?

How the decedent held title decides how the property passes. If the deed reads joint tenants with right of survivorship, or tenancy by the entirety with a surviving spouse, the property passed to the survivor outside the estate at the moment of death. The executor is not the seller in that case, and no Surrogate appointment is needed to convey it. If the decedent held title alone as sole owner, or as a tenant in common, the interest is part of the estate and the estate paperwork above applies. If the property was already deeded out to the heirs after death, then every person named on that deed is a seller and every one of them signs.

The buyer's title company will search the chain of title back through prior owners and look for anything that could survive the sale: open mortgages including reverse mortgages, home equity lines that were never formally discharged, judgments against the decedent or against any heir, municipal liens for unpaid water and sewer, unreleased contractor liens, and the inheritance tax lien discussed above. Old estates in the chain, where a prior owner died and probate was never completed, are a common and fixable surprise. Finding one two weeks before closing is not fixable on that schedule.

6. Property condition disclosure when the seller is an estate

New Jersey sellers commonly complete a Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Statement. Estates are a different situation, and estate sellers are frequently not asked to complete one, either because the contract exempts a fiduciary who never occupied the property or because the executor genuinely has no personal knowledge to disclose. An executor who lives three states away and last set foot in the house years ago cannot honestly answer whether the basement takes water in a heavy rain.

Here is the part that matters more than the form. Not completing a disclosure does not erase the obligation to disclose known material defects. If you know the roof leaks, the oil tank was never removed, or there was a fire in the garage, that gets disclosed regardless of what form is or is not in the file. Concealing a known defect is a legal exposure that follows the estate and the executor personally, and it is not worth the risk on any sale.

There is also a practical effect worth planning for. When a buyer receives no disclosure statement, they compensate by inspecting harder and negotiating more aggressively on whatever the inspection finds. The best counter is documentation: a recent inspection report you commissioned, a written scope from the oil tank removal contractor, the permit history from the building department, anything that replaces guesswork with paper. Our guide to NJ seller disclosure requirements covers this in detail, and selling a house as-is in NJ explains what “as-is” does and does not protect you from.

7. Municipal certificates every New Jersey sale needs

These are the items that get scheduled too late. They have nothing to do with the estate and everything to do with the municipality, and they are needed on every New Jersey residential resale.

  • Smoke alarm, carbon monoxide alarm and fire extinguisher certificate. New Jersey requires a certificate of compliance for smoke alarms, carbon monoxide alarms and a portable fire extinguisher before a change of occupancy in a one- or two-family dwelling. You request the inspection from the local fire official or bureau of fire prevention in the town where the property sits. Requirements and fees are set locally, and the New Jersey Division of Fire Safety is the state-level agency behind the rule (NJ DCA Division of Fire Safety). Inherited homes fail this inspection more often than occupied ones, usually because the alarms are old, missing, or the wrong type for their location. Failing means a re-inspection, and re-inspections get scheduled at the town's convenience.
  • Certificate of occupancy or resale inspection. Many New Jersey municipalities require a certificate of occupancy, a continued certificate of occupancy, or a resale or housing inspection before a residential sale closes. This is entirely a local requirement and it varies substantially from town to town within the same county. Call the building or code enforcement department where the property is located, ask specifically what is required for a resale, and ask what the inspection checks. Some towns look at handrails, egress windows and open permits, and an open permit from work the decedent had done years ago has to be resolved before the certificate issues.

8. Taxes, utilities, payoffs, and insurance on a vacant house

These are the carrying items. They do not usually block a closing on their own, but they cost money every month and they produce paperwork the closing attorney needs.

  • Property tax status. Get the current status from the tax collector, including any quarter that went unpaid during the illness or after the death. Unpaid taxes become a municipal lien and get paid at closing out of proceeds. Also ask whether any senior citizen, veteran or property tax relief deduction was in place, because those often end at death and can leave a balance nobody expected.
  • Water, sewer and any municipal utility. Municipal water and sewer charges can become liens. The closing requires a final reading. Where the town bills these, order the final reading early rather than in closing week.
  • Mortgage payoff. Request a written payoff statement from the servicer. Expect them to require a certified death certificate and proof of your authority before they will speak with you at all, which is another reason to have the short certificate in hand.
  • Reverse mortgage payoff. If the decedent had a reverse mortgage, the loan generally becomes due when the last surviving borrower dies, and the servicer works on defined deadlines that begin running whether or not the family has engaged with them. Contact that servicer as early as you possibly can and get the deadlines in writing. Reverse mortgages are the most time-sensitive item on this page.
  • Homeowners insurance on a vacant property. A standard homeowners policy is written for an occupied house, and most carriers restrict or suspend coverage once a home has been vacant beyond a stated period. That can leave an empty inherited house effectively uninsured for exactly the risks that empty houses face, including burst pipes, water damage and vandalism. Call the carrier, tell them the house is unoccupied, and ask what endorsement or separate vacant-property policy they need. Do this before the utilities get shut off, not after.

What to gather first, and what takes longest

The order matters, because several of these documents are inputs to the others.

  1. Certified death certificates, in quantity. Nothing else starts without them.
  2. The original will and the existing deed. Together they tell you whether probate is even the right path and who the seller actually is.
  3. The Surrogate appointment. Call the county Surrogate, ask what to bring and what it costs, and go. Where a bond is required, this step stretches, so learn early whether it applies to you.
  4. The tax waiver application. Start this the week you have your letters. It is the longest item on the list by a wide margin, and it is the one that is impossible to accelerate once you are behind on it.
  5. Mortgage or reverse mortgage payoff figures. These take a servicer's own processing time and they determine what the sale actually nets.
  6. Municipal inspections. Order these once you have a contract and a target closing date, but confirm the town's requirements much earlier so nothing surprises you.

The items that stall New Jersey estate closings, in order of how often I see them: the tax waiver that nobody applied for, a short certificate that has gone stale, an open permit discovered during the resale inspection, and a deed that turns out to say something different from what the family assumed.

Why gathering these before you list shortens the timeline

None of these documents is difficult on its own. What costs estates months is running them in sequence instead of in parallel, and only starting each one when someone else asks for it.

The waiver application is the clearest example. If it goes in shortly after the Surrogate appointment, it processes quietly in the background while the house gets cleaned out, priced, photographed, listed, and put under contract. If it goes in after the attorney review period ends, the buyer sits and waits with their rate lock running, and rate locks are where patient buyers stop being patient. The document was going to take the same amount of time either way. The only thing that changed was whether it was on the critical path.

The same logic applies to the smaller items. Knowing on day one that the town requires a resale inspection means the open permit gets resolved during the listing period instead of during the closing period. Knowing the deed says tenancy by the entirety means the family stops preparing for a probate that was never necessary.

Start with what the property is worth today, because every other decision follows from that number: whether to sell as-is, whether any work is worth doing, and what each beneficiary is actually receiving. Frequently asked questions

What documents do I need to sell an inherited house in NJ?

Certified copies of the death certificate with the raised seal, the original will if there is one, Letters Testamentary or Letters of Administration from the county Surrogate along with a current short certificate, the existing deed, a New Jersey inheritance tax waiver on Form 0-1 for the real estate, the mortgage payoff statement, and the municipal items every NJ sale needs, including the smoke and carbon monoxide alarm certificate and a certificate of occupancy or resale inspection where the town requires one.

Do I need a tax waiver to sell inherited property in New Jersey?

Yes, for real estate. New Jersey's inheritance tax attaches as a lien to property the decedent owned as of the date of death, and the waiver is the State's written release of that lien. The Division of Taxation's executor guide states that real estate transfers always require Form 0-1 and that Form 0-1 can only be issued by the Division. A title company will not insure the buyer's ownership without it, so the sale cannot close until the waiver exists, even in an estate where no tax is owed.

What is the difference between an L-8 and an L-9 in New Jersey?

The L-8 is the Affidavit for Release of Non-Real Estate Assets for Class A resident decedents. It is self-executing, meaning the estate representative fills it out and hands it directly to the bank or transfer agent, with nothing filed with the State. The L-9 is the Resident Decedent Affidavit Requesting Real Property Tax Waiver. It covers real estate, it is filed with the Inheritance and Estate Tax Branch, and the Branch issues the Form 0-1 waiver in response. The L-8 does not release real estate.

How many certified death certificates do I need to sell a house?

More than most families order. Institutions keep the copy you give them rather than returning it, and in an estate sale you typically surrender one each to the Surrogate, the Division of Taxation, the title company, every bank or brokerage holding an account, the mortgage servicer, the insurance carrier, and often the utilities. Order a generous batch through the funeral director at the start. Additional certified copies come from the State's Office of Vital Statistics and Registry or the local registrar where the death occurred.

Does an executor have to fill out the NJ seller's property condition disclosure?

Estate sellers are frequently not asked to complete one, either because the contract exempts a fiduciary who never occupied the property or because the executor has no personal knowledge of the house. That exemption does not erase the obligation to disclose known material defects. Anything you actually know about, such as a leaking roof, an unremoved oil tank, or past flooding, should be disclosed regardless. Expect buyers to inspect more aggressively when no disclosure is provided, and consider a pre-listing inspection so the conversation is based on documentation rather than speculation.

Where do I file probate in New Jersey?

With the Surrogate's Court of the county where the decedent lived at the time of death, not the county where the property is located and not where the executor lives. Each of New Jersey's 21 counties has its own Surrogate, and uncontested estates are handled entirely by that county office. Call before you go and ask what to bring and what the fees will be, since both are set county by county.
