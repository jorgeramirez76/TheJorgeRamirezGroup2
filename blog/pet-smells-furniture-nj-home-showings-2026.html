How to Get Pet Smells Out of Furniture Before NJ Showings

House Outlook’s newsletter this week linked a guide to getting pet smells out of couches, bedding and the other soft surfaces a dog or cat lives on. Good timing. Fall is showing season in New Jersey, and in my experience pet odor is the single most common thing a buyer mentions in the car after a showing and the single thing sellers are least able to smell themselves. Here is the order I walk clients through to get it out of the furniture before the photographer and the first buyer arrive.

The tip House Outlook sent this week

This week’s House Outlook newsletter pointed to a guide on removing pet smells from furniture, with the fair observation that pet cuddles are great and the odor left behind on couches, bedding and other soft surfaces is not. The advice centered on finding the source, using the right cleaner for the fabric and washing everything that can be washed. All of it applies to a listed house, with one difference: in a sale the person judging the result is a stranger who walked in from fresh air, and they are deciding what your house is worth.

Why pet odor costs New Jersey sellers more than they think

You stop smelling your own pet within a few weeks of living with it. A buyer touring a colonial in Westfield or a condo in Hoboken does not have that adjustment. They notice in the first few seconds at the door, and from that point they read the house differently. The scratched door becomes a pattern. The area rug becomes a question about what is under it. The dog bed in the family room becomes a question about the carpet pad and the subfloor. Buyers rarely say any of this to the listing agent. It shows up instead as a lower offer or a flooring credit after the inspection.

Getting it out of the furniture, in order

  1. Find the source before you clean anything. Get down to cushion level with your nose, and use an inexpensive UV flashlight in a dark room on sofas, mattresses and rugs. Old urine spots glow. You cannot treat what you have not found.
  2. Vacuum every soft surface with the upholstery and crevice tools. Under cushions, down the sides, under the piece. Hair and dander carry odor, and a vacuum with a HEPA filter keeps it from going back into the air.
  3. Check the care code, then use baking soda. Sprinkle it over the fabric, leave it fifteen to thirty minutes and vacuum it off. The couch care code guide explains which codes allow water and which do not, so read the tag before you go further.
  4. Treat the specific spots with an enzyme cleaner. Enzyme products made for pet urine break down the proteins causing the smell; fragranced sprays only cover them for an hour. Test on a hidden spot, dab rather than soak, and let it dry fully.
  5. Wash everything removable. Slipcovers, throw pillow covers, blankets, pet beds and bedding, on the hottest setting the label allows. If the pet bed does not come clean, it leaves the house before photos.
  6. Steam or hire a pro for the big pieces. A sectional, a wool rug or a mattress a cat has used will often need a professional upholstery cleaner. The cost is small next to the credit a buyer asks for.
  7. Change the air, not the scent. Replace the HVAC filter, run the system with the windows open for an hour and keep that up through the listing period. The HVAC and dust guide covers why the filter matters.
  8. If the smell returns, stop cleaning and look deeper. Odor that comes back after a thorough treatment is in the cushion foam, the carpet pad or the subfloor. At that point it is a repair decision, and the pet cleaning routine guide walks through the flooring side.

Jorge’s tip: Have someone who does not live in the house do the sniff test at the front door, after the cleaning and again the morning of the open house. Then put the pet beds, bowls and crate in the car during every showing. A buyer who never sees evidence of a pet rarely goes looking for the smell of one.

What not to do

Do not plug in air fresheners or light candles before a showing. Buyers and agents in Summit, Chatham and Montclair read heavy fragrance as a cover-up, and some buyers react badly to it. Do not shampoo a carpet the morning of a showing; damp carpet smells worse than the dog did. And do not throw a rug over a spot you found with the flashlight. Buyers lift rugs, and inspectors move furniture.

For buyers: what a pet smell tells you

If you walk into a house in Cranford or Edison and notice a pet, ask three questions. What flooring is under the carpet, how old is the carpet pad, and has the HVAC been serviced. Then look at the bottom of doors and the base of the stairs for scratches. Pet odor is not a reason to walk away from a good house, but it belongs in your inspection notes and, if the subfloor is affected, in your negotiation. The inspection red flags guide covers the rest of the walkthrough.

Questions about pet odor and selling a NJ home

Can buyers really smell pets if I cannot?

Yes. People who live with a pet stop noticing its odor within weeks, while a buyer walking in from outside picks it up in the first few seconds. Have someone who does not live in the house do the test at the front door before photos and before each open house.

What is the best cleaner for pet odor in a couch?

An enzyme cleaner made for pet urine, because it breaks down the proteins that cause the smell rather than masking them. Check the sofa’s care code first, test on a hidden spot, and use it on the specific spot, not the whole piece. Baking soda handles general odor in the fabric.

Should I use candles or plug-in air fresheners during showings?

No. Heavy fragrance reads as a cover-up to experienced buyers and agents, and some people react to it. A clean house that smells like nothing is the goal. Open windows for an hour before a showing instead.

Do I need to replace carpet if the pet smell will not go away?

Sometimes. If the odor returns after cleaning, it is usually in the carpet pad or the subfloor, and no surface treatment reaches it. Pulling the carpet, sealing the subfloor and replacing the pad is often less than the credit a buyer asks for after a showing, but get a quote before deciding.

Do I have to disclose that pets lived in the house in New Jersey?

Pet ownership itself is not a defect, but known damage such as urine-stained subfloor or scratched doors is a condition of the house. Talk through the seller disclosure with your agent and attorney and describe the property honestly rather than relying on a cleaning to hide it.
