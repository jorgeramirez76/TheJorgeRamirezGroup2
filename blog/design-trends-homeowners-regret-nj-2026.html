The Problem With Trendy Renovations in NJ Real Estate

There's a pattern I see play out regularly in Union, Essex, and Morris County. A homeowner renovates in 2021 or 2022, spends $80,000-$120,000 making their home look exactly like the HGTV shows they watched during COVID, and then lists the home in 2025 or 2026. The buyers walk through and the first thing they mentally calculate is the cost to undo what was done.

That's the renovation trap. You spend real money creating a home that reflects a specific moment in design history — a moment that may already have passed by the time you sell. And buyers, who have been looking at homes for weeks or months, can identify trend-chasing faster than most sellers expect. When buyers see a heavily trendy renovation, they don't think "nice update." They think "what am I going to have to change."

This isn't an argument against renovating. It's an argument for renovating with the broadest buyer pool in mind, not the most current Pinterest board. Here's what I'm seeing NJ sellers actually regret in 2026.

1. The Grey-Everything Era

Grey paint, grey cabinets, grey countertops, grey flooring, grey tile. The grey wave hit NJ around 2015 and peaked around 2020. Homes renovated at that peak are now showing up on the market, and many buyers are walking in and saying some variation of the same thing: "It feels cold."

The problem with grey is that it was so dominant for so long that it went from fresh to ubiquitous to dated faster than most color trends. Cool grey in particular — the blue-grey tone that was everywhere — photographs flat and reads as cold in older NJ homes that don't get the light and volume of modern construction.

Skip it if you're renovating to sell

Cool grey paint throughout, grey kitchen cabinets in a standard colonial floor plan, grey luxury vinyl plank as the only flooring throughout. These will appeal to some buyers but give others the wrong emotional first impression.

What works instead

Warm whites (Benjamin Moore White Dove, Sherwin-Williams Alabaster), warm greige tones, or muted warm neutrals. In NJ's Colonial and Victorian homes with traditional woodwork, warm tones complement the architecture. They also photograph better in rooms with northern and eastern light exposure, which is common in older NJ home layouts.

2. All-White Kitchens With High-Gloss Cabinets

White kitchen cabinets are not the problem. White kitchen cabinets with high-gloss finish are the problem — and specifically the high-gloss white cabinetry that was on every design show from 2017 to 2022.

The practical issue is function: high-gloss white shows every fingerprint, every water droplet, every scratch. Buyers who have children or actually cook look at a high-gloss white kitchen and immediately start calculating the maintenance burden. In families with kids — which is a majority of NJ buyers in the suburbs — that mental calculation is a negative.

The second issue is that high-gloss white is also aging out of trend faster than its matte counterpart. Buyers in 2026 who've been looking at homes for a while can identify a 2018 kitchen update by the cabinet finish before they even look at the hardware.

Skip it if you're renovating to sell

Full high-gloss white cabinet kitchen with no warmth elements. Especially problematic in NJ family homes in Chatham, Summit, Westfield — where buyer demographics skew toward families with children who can spot a high-maintenance kitchen at first glance.

What works instead

Matte or satin white cabinets, which are both more timeless and more practical. Or go slightly warm: cream, off-white with warm undertones, or a classic shaker in light greige. Pair with unlacquered brass or matte black hardware — both hold up over time better than the chrome and polished nickel trends.

3. Open Concept Taken Too Far

The open floor plan was the gold standard of NJ home renovation for fifteen years. Then COVID happened. Families spent 18+ months entirely inside their homes. They discovered — some of them the hard way — that "open concept" is a great idea until three people are simultaneously on Zoom calls, someone is cooking something fragrant, and someone else wants to watch television.

I am not saying open concept is bad. I'm saying that buyers in 2026 NJ are more nuanced about it than buyers in 2018. They want flow. They don't want to feel like they're living in a hotel lobby. The homes that are getting the best responses are the ones with an open relationship between the kitchen and casual dining — but with some separation from the formal living or office space. Not walls, necessarily. But some visual and acoustic delineation.

The sellers who regret open concept renovations are the ones who removed a wall that created the only separation between their kitchen and their living room, then discovered that buyers in Maplewood and Montclair — who skew toward people moving from dense city apartments — actually want a room they can close the door on.

Skip it if you're renovating to sell

Removing the only wall between the kitchen and the rest of the main floor living space. Eliminating the only room on the main floor that can function as a private office or quiet retreat. In NJ markets where hybrid work is standard, "no private space on the main floor" is now a legitimate buyer concern.

4. Shiplap: When Farmhouse Met New Jersey Colonial

I have nothing against shiplap. Used correctly — a single accent wall in a mudroom, a powder room, a casual family room — it reads as a thoughtful design choice. What I've seen in NJ homes renovated between 2018 and 2022 is shiplap used on three or four walls in rooms that have no architectural relationship to the farmhouse aesthetic. A 1940s Summit Colonial with shiplap in the dining room is not a farmhouse. It's a 1940s Summit Colonial with shiplap in the dining room.

Buyers in the Union and Morris County markets I work in are sophisticated. Many of them are coming from Brooklyn or Manhattan with a design eye. They know what they're looking at. When they see shiplap in an architecturally formal home, they don't see Joanna Gaines. They see the removal project they'll be doing two years after closing.

Skip it if you're renovating to sell

Multiple shiplap walls in a home that has no farmhouse or casual rustic architectural identity. Shiplap throughout an open floor plan. Shiplap in formal rooms (dining room, living room with period architectural details) where it competes with rather than complements the home's character.

5. Over-Personalized "Feature" Rooms

Wine cellars. Cigar lounges. Sports team "man caves" with team colors painted floor to ceiling. Full home gyms with rubber flooring and mirror walls. These rooms serve one very specific buyer — the buyer who has exactly those interests and can imagine themselves using that exact space in that exact way.

Every other buyer looks at that room and calculates the cost to convert it back to usable, flexible space.

In NJ's residential markets — where a four-bedroom Colonial in Chatham or a split-level in Cranford is being evaluated by young families, relocating professionals, and NYC commuters — highly specialized rooms narrow your buyer pool significantly. The more specialized the room, the fewer buyers can see themselves in it. Fewer buyers means less competition. Less competition means less negotiating leverage at offer time.

The NJ buyers I work with want extra bedrooms, home offices, finished spaces that can flex to serve whatever their family needs. A dedicated room that only works as a wine cellar or a gym is a room that has to be mentally subtracted from the bedroom or office count.

6. Trendy Tile That Peaked at the Exact Moment You Installed It

Tile is a long-commitment material. What looked current and beautiful in 2021 can look dated by 2026, and that's exactly the timeline many NJ sellers are on. The patterns I see buyers responding most negatively to in 2026: extremely bold geometric patterns throughout a full bathroom (not an accent, but the entire floor-to-ceiling), subway tile with very dark grout (the dark-grout moment peaked around 2019), and aggressively oversized format tile in small bathrooms where the scale reads as overwhelming.

Classic white subway tile with light grey grout is still the most universally appealing bathroom choice in NJ. Boring? Maybe. But boring is what sells at 9 AM on a Tuesday when a buyer is calculating monthly mortgage payments and deciding whether to write an offer.

What Actually Works for NJ Resale in 2026

To be clear: renovation absolutely adds value when done right. The kitchen and bathroom updates that hold up over time in NJ are the classic ones — well-executed, neutral, with quality materials. Refinished hardwood floors. An updated primary bathroom with a walk-in shower in a neutral tile. A kitchen with semi-custom cabinetry in warm whites or light greiges, quartz or stone countertops, and updated appliances.

Curb appeal improvements are almost always worth doing — fresh exterior paint, updated landscaping, a clean front door with new hardware and lighting. These are the first things buyers see and they set the tone for the entire showing.

The test I apply to any pre-sale renovation question is simple: does this appeal to more buyers or fewer buyers than not doing it? Neutral paint: more buyers. A themed game room: fewer buyers. Updated stainless appliances: more buyers. Custom built-in bar with $20,000 of millwork: dramatically fewer buyers. When in doubt, ask me before you start. I've seen what sells and what sits in every NJ county I work in, and the answer is almost always cheaper than the mistake.

Frequently Asked Questions: NJ Design Trends and Resale Value

What home renovations don't add value in NJ?

In NJ's current market, renovations that tend not to return their cost include: ultra-trendy kitchen designs that date quickly, removing walls for open concept without addressing privacy needs, adding a swimming pool in Union or Essex County where pool season is 4-5 months, over-customizing spaces for personal hobbies, and aggressive accent walls throughout. The renovations that consistently return value are kitchen and bathroom updates in classic finishes, updated primary suites, refinished floors, and curb appeal improvements.

Should I renovate before selling my NJ home?

It depends on what you're renovating and why. Cosmetic updates that improve broad buyer appeal — fresh neutral paint, updated light fixtures, refinished hardwood floors, clean modern bathrooms — almost always pay off. Major structural renovations rarely return full cost at resale. The test: does this appeal to more buyers or fewer buyers? If it's a personal preference that serves a niche buyer, it's unlikely to return its cost.

What paint colors hurt home resale in NJ?

Very dark accent walls, highly saturated statement colors, and cool grey throughout are the biggest negatives in NJ resale today. The grey-everything trend has dated noticeably since 2021. The safest resale palette is warm whites and soft off-whites throughout (Benjamin Moore White Dove, Sherwin-Williams Alabaster), with one or two rooms in a warm muted neutral. These photograph well and appeal to the widest buyer pool in every NJ county I work in.

Is open-concept layout good for NJ home resale?

Open concept lost some of its premium in the post-COVID NJ market. Buyers with children and remote workers now specifically look for acoustic separation and rooms that can be closed off. A well-executed open kitchen-to-casual-dining relationship still resonates. But fully open floor plans with no visual delineation anywhere are meeting more buyer pushback than they did in 2018. Removing the only wall that provides any separation on the main floor is now a risky renovation in most NJ family markets.

Does shiplap hurt home value in NJ?

Shiplap used strategically — one accent wall in a mudroom, a powder room, or a casual family room — is largely neutral in NJ resale. Shiplap on multiple walls throughout a traditional NJ Colonial or Victorian home reads as dated trend-chasing in 2026. Buyers in Summit, Chatham, and Westfield purchasing traditional architecture tend to view heavy shiplap use as something they'll want to remove — and that removal cost enters their offer calculation.
