Capital Gains Tax When Selling a House in New Jersey

Updated July 30, 2026 · by Jorge Ramirez, NJ License #1754604

Most New Jersey sellers owe nothing, and New Jersey does not add a second layer. Federal rules let you exclude up to $250,000 of gain on your main home, or $500,000 filing jointly. New Jersey then follows the federal result exactly — the state’s own filing instructions say that for a principal residence, gain “is calculated the same way as for federal purposes” and “any amount that is taxable for federal purposes is taxable for New Jersey purposes.” So if the exclusion covers your gain, neither government taxes it.

The exclusion is on the gain, not the price

This is the first place people scare themselves. Selling for $800,000 does not mean $800,000 is exposed. Your gain is roughly the sale price, minus selling costs, minus your adjusted basis — and the adjusted basis is what you paid plus every capital improvement you have made since.

A home bought at $400,000 and sold at $800,000 has a gain in the region of $400,000 before adjustments. A married couple filing jointly excludes up to $500,000, so that sale produces no taxable gain at all. That is the ordinary outcome for most people selling a long-held home here.

Two tests, and they are not the same test

You have to clear both, per IRS Topic 701:

  • Ownership test — you owned the home for at least 24 months of the 5 years ending on the sale date.
  • Use test — you lived in it as a residence for at least 24 months of those same 5 years.

The useful detail: the two 24-month periods do not have to be the same months. They both simply have to fall inside the five-year window ending at the sale. So a year rented out in the middle does not automatically disqualify you.

The joint-filer split catches people. For ownership, either spouse qualifying is enough. For use, both spouses must qualify individually. A couple who married recently, where only one of them ever lived in the house, can pass ownership and fail use.

You cannot use it back to back

You are generally ineligible if you already excluded gain from another home sale during the two years before this one. There are exceptions, and the five-year clock can be suspended for certain military and government service — that is what IRS Publication 523 is for, and it is the document to read if your situation is not the simple one.

What New Jersey does differently

Two things worth knowing, both from the state’s own guidance.

There is no preferential New Jersey rate. The Division of Taxation is explicit that New Jersey does not differentiate between short-term and long-term capital gains. Federally, long-term gain gets its own lower rate schedule. In New Jersey a taxable gain is just income, taxed at your ordinary rate — which runs from 1.4% up to 10.75%. So a gain that is partly above the exclusion can be taxed more heavily by New Jersey than a federal-rate intuition suggests.

Basis follows the federal number. New Jersey uses the federal adjusted basis for a principal residence, so there is one basis to get right rather than two. (Rentals, partnership interests and S-corporation shares are the exception — those can require a separate New Jersey adjusted basis.)

Basis is where the money is, and where the records are missing

Adjusted basis is the most commonly understated figure in a home sale, because it depends on receipts nobody kept. Capital improvements raise your basis and therefore shrink your gain: a roof, a kitchen, an addition, new windows, a finished basement, central air. Routine repairs and maintenance do not count. Selling costs, commission included, come off the gain as well.

If you are anywhere near the exclusion limit, the hour you spend reconstructing twenty years of improvement records is the highest-paid hour of the whole transaction. Start it before you list, not at tax time.

This is not the “exit tax.” They are different things and get conflated constantly. The so-called New Jersey exit tax is not a tax — it is an estimated payment withheld at closing from certain sellers, credited against what you actually owe and refunded if you overpaid. You can owe zero capital gains tax and still have money held back, then get it returned. Full explanation in the NJ exit tax explained.

When you actually do owe

Four situations, in rough order of how often I see them:

  • The gain exceeds the exclusion. On a home held twenty or thirty years in Millburn, Westfield, Montclair or Summit, $500,000 of gain is not exotic.
  • It was not your main home. A rental or a second home does not get the exclusion. New Jersey’s definition of principal residence explicitly excludes a vacation home, a second home, and property you rent to someone else.
  • You fail the use test. Fewer than 24 of the last 60 months lived in.
  • You used the exclusion recently. Another excluded sale inside the prior two years.

Even then it is the gain above the exclusion that is exposed, not the whole thing.

Reporting, even when you owe nothing

If you receive a Form 1099-S for the transaction, you report the sale — even if the entire gain is excludable. You also report if you cannot exclude all of it. That happens on Schedule D and Form 8949. Getting a 1099-S is not a signal that you owe something; it is a signal that the IRS knows the sale happened and expects to see it on the return.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do you have to pay capital gains tax when you sell your house in NJ?

Usually not. If the home was your main home, federal rules let you exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or up to $500,000 if you file jointly with your spouse. And New Jersey follows the federal result: the 2025 NJ-1040 instructions state that on the sale of a principal residence, "capital gain is calculated the same way as for federal purposes" and "any amount that is taxable for federal purposes is taxable for New Jersey purposes." So if the federal exclusion covers your gain, New Jersey does not tax it either.

How much gain can I exclude on a NJ home sale?

Up to $250,000 if you file singly and up to $500,000 on a joint return, per IRS Topic 701. That is an exclusion of GAIN, not of sale price — the gain is roughly what you sold for, minus selling costs, minus your adjusted basis. On a home bought for $400,000 and sold for $800,000 the gain is around $400,000 before adjustments, which a married couple would generally exclude in full.

What are the ownership and use tests?

You must meet both. The ownership test asks whether you owned the home for at least 24 months of the 5 years ending on the sale date. The use test asks whether you lived in it as a residence for at least 24 months of those same 5 years. The two 24-month periods do not have to be the same months, but both must fall inside that 5-year window.

What if we file jointly — do we both have to qualify?

The rules split. For the ownership test, either spouse qualifying is enough. For the use test, both spouses must meet it individually. So a couple where only one person ever lived in the house can hit a problem on the use test even though ownership is fine.

Can I use the exclusion more than once?

Not back to back. You are generally ineligible if you already excluded gain from the sale of another home during the two years before this sale. There are exceptions and a suspension of the five-year period for certain military and government service, which is why IRS Publication 523 exists.

Does New Jersey tax capital gains at a lower rate?

No. New Jersey does not differentiate between short-term and long-term capital gains — a taxable gain is taxed as income at your ordinary New Jersey rate, which runs from 1.4% up to 10.75%. There is no preferential New Jersey capital gains rate the way there is federally.

Is capital gains tax the same as the NJ exit tax?

No, and they get confused constantly. The so-called exit tax is not a tax at all — it is an estimated payment withheld at closing from certain sellers, which is credited against what you actually owe and refunded if you overpaid. Capital gains tax is the actual liability. You can owe zero capital gains tax and still have money withheld, then get it back.

What raises my basis and lowers my gain?

Your adjusted basis is what you paid plus capital improvements, and it is the number most sellers understate because they never kept receipts. A new roof, a kitchen renovation, an addition, new windows, a finished basement all add to basis. Routine repairs and maintenance do not. Selling costs such as commission also reduce the gain. New Jersey uses the federal adjusted basis, so there is one number to get right, not two.

When do I actually owe capital gains tax on a NJ sale?

Four common cases: the gain exceeds the exclusion, which in this market happens on long-held homes in towns like Millburn, Westfield and Montclair; the property was a rental or second home rather than your main home; you fail the use test because you did not live there 24 of the last 60 months; or you used the exclusion on another sale within the previous two years.

Do I have to report the sale if the gain is excluded?

If you receive a Form 1099-S for the transaction, yes — you report the sale even when the entire gain is excludable. You also report if you cannot exclude all of the gain. Reporting happens on Schedule D and Form 8949.
