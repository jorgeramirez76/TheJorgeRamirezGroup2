How Much Money Do You Actually Need to Buy a House in New Jersey?

Updated July 2026 · By Jorge Ramirez, Keller Williams Premier Properties

On New Jersey’s median price of about $563,000, plan on roughly $28,000 to $45,000 in cash if you are putting 3% down. That is the down payment plus 2–5% in closing costs. Add about $2,400 you will spend on inspection and appraisal before closing, and whatever reserves your lender wants left in the bank afterward. At 20% down on the same house, you are looking at $124,000 to $140,500.

Where the Money Actually Goes

1. The down payment

The part everyone knows about, and the part people most often overestimate. See below on the 20% myth.

2. Closing costs, 2–5% of the price

Lender origination and underwriting, title search and title insurance, the attorney handling your closing at roughly $1,500–$2,500, recording fees, and prepaid property taxes and homeowner’s insurance. In New Jersey the buyer does not pay the realty transfer fee — that one is the seller’s.

3. Money you spend before closing, and do not get back

This is the bucket that surprises people, because it comes out of pocket weeks before the closing table and it is gone whether or not the deal survives. A home inspection runs about $500–$900, an appraisal about $500–$800. If a deal falls apart at inspection and you start again on another house, you pay both again.

4. Reserves your lender wants to see

Underwriting generally wants to see money still in the account after closing — often a couple of months of payments, more for condos or investment purchases. Draining every last dollar into the down payment can cost you the approval, which is a genuinely painful way to learn this.

The 20% Myth

You do not need 20% down to buy a house in New Jersey. Conventional loans go to 3% down for qualified buyers and FHA to 3.5%, and both are ordinary here.

What 20% actually buys you is the absence of private mortgage insurance, which lowers your monthly payment. That is a real benefit and worth having if you have the cash. But the arithmetic that matters is comparative: on the state median, 20% down is $112,500 against $17,000 at 3%. If reaching $112,500 takes you four more years of saving in a market that keeps appreciating, the PMI you avoided is frequently the cheaper of the two paths. That is a calculation worth doing with your actual numbers rather than a rule of thumb you inherited.

What Assistance Changes

NJHMFA down payment assistance is $10,000–$15,000 as a five-year forgivable loan for eligible first-time buyers, layered on top of an NJHMFA first mortgage. On a $450,000 purchase at 3% down, the down payment is $13,500 — so assistance can cover most or all of it.

What it does not cover is the rest of this page. Closing costs remain yours, and so does the inspection and appraisal money you spend before you get to the table. Income and purchase-price limits apply and are updated annually; the NJ first-time buyer programs page has the county-level income limits and the minimum credit score for each program.

On credit score, briefly. FHA allows 580 with 3.5% down and 500 with 10% down. Conventional and NJHMFA generally start at 620, with materially better pricing above 740. The 580–619 band is where buyers most often get stuck: FHA is open to you, but the NJHMFA assistance layered on top generally is not. Forty points of credit repair can change which programs you qualify for entirely, and that is worth knowing before you start touring rather than after.

The Number That Actually Governs What You Can Buy

Cash to close tells you whether you can complete a purchase. It does not tell you what you can afford to own. In New Jersey those two answers diverge more than in most states, because property taxes are a larger share of the monthly payment here — and because published tax rates cannot be compared between towns, two houses at the same price in neighbouring towns can carry meaningfully different monthly costs.

So run both: the cash you need to get in, and the monthly you can carry once you are there. The affordability calculator handles the second one.

Common Questions

Do you need 20% down to buy in New Jersey?

No. Conventional goes to 3% and FHA to 3.5% for qualified buyers. Twenty percent avoids PMI; it is not an entry requirement.

What is the average down payment in NJ?

The average is not a useful number here because it splits by buyer type — first-time buyers commonly at 3–5%, move-up buyers using sale equity often at 20% or more. What matters is what leaves you with reserves.

Does the buyer pay the NJ realty transfer fee?

No, the seller does. If you are also selling, the realty transfer fee calculator covers that side.

How much should I have saved before I start looking?

Enough for the down payment, the closing costs, the roughly $2,400 you will spend before closing, and your reserves — and ideally a pre-approval in hand, because in this market touring without one wastes everyone’s time including yours.
