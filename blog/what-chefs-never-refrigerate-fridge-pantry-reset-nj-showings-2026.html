What Chefs Never Refrigerate: A Fridge and Pantry Reset Before NJ Kitchen Showings

House Outlook’s featured chef says the refrigerator is for food that spoils, and most of what fills ours never would. For a seller, that list is a one-evening way to make the fridge, the counter and the pantry all look bigger before buyers open the doors.

What House Outlook got right about the fridge

The article House Outlook featured came from a trained chef and former food safety trainer who listed nineteen foods he almost never refrigerates. Among them: tomatoes, onions, garlic, potatoes, bread, honey, coffee, whole melons, bananas and most stone fruit until it ripens. Cold either ruins the texture, as with tomatoes and bread, or does nothing a cupboard would not do, as with honey. The fridge is for things that spoil, and a lot of what fills ours never would.

I read that as a seller’s problem in disguise. Buyers in Westfield, Summit and Montclair open the refrigerator during showings. They open the pantry too. A fridge packed to the door with onions, potatoes, bread and six kinds of hot sauce reads as a small fridge in a kitchen without enough storage, and that is a judgment about your house, not your groceries.

The fridge is an appliance buyers inspect

In most New Jersey sales the refrigerator stays with the house, which means a buyer looks at it the way they look at the furnace. They notice whether the shelves are clean, whether the door seals close, whether there is a smell when it opens, and whether it is so full they cannot see the back wall. A half-empty, wiped-down fridge tells them the appliance works and the kitchen has room. The door shelf guide covers what goes where inside.

Pulling the chef’s list out empties a third of the shelves and puts the tomatoes, bread and bananas on the counter, which is where the next problem starts.

The one-bowl rule for a listed kitchen

A kitchen in a listing photo gets one bowl. Not a bread box, a fruit bowl, a basket of onions and a bag of potatoes along the backsplash. One bowl with a few pieces of fruit that will not be brown by the weekend. Everything else on the chef’s list goes into the pantry or a lower cabinet in a bin you can lift out, which keeps the counter clear per the kitchen staging checklist.

  1. Onions, garlic, potatoes: a ventilated bin on a low pantry shelf, kept apart. A sprouting potato in a listing pantry is a detail buyers remember.
  2. Bread: in a drawer or cabinet, not on the counter.
  3. Bananas and ripening fruit: the one bowl, and only a few. Overripe bananas are the smell buyers notice in a Hoboken or Jersey City one-bedroom before they notice anything else.
  4. Honey, coffee, hot sauce, oils: a single pantry shelf, labels facing out, caps wiped. A buyer who opens the pantry sees shelves instead of stuff.
  5. Whole melons and squash: a basket on the pantry floor, or do not buy them until after closing.

Jorge’s tip: Do the fridge the night before the photographer, not the morning of. Take everything out, toss what is expired, wipe the shelves, put back only what spoils, and leave the door shelves half empty. Sellers in Cranford and Scotch Plains who hold that habit for three weeks are the ones whose kitchens show well on a weekday evening with no notice.

Odor is the real risk

The chef’s list is mostly things that smell as they ripen or cure. Onions, garlic and bananas on an open counter are the first thing a buyer notices walking into a closed-up house in Edison or Woodbridge, and the fix is not a candle. Store them in the pantry in a closed bin, take out the trash before every showing, and read the home odors guide for the rest. If the smell is coming from the fridge itself, find the source, because a buyer who cannot see the cause assumes the worst about the appliance.

For buyers: what the fridge tells you

When you tour a house in Millburn or Morristown and the fridge is empty and spotless, understand that the seller has been coached, and good for them. Then check what staging cannot fake: whether the door seals grip a sheet of paper, whether the freezer has heavy frost, and whether the water line behind it has been leaking onto the floor. Ask whether the fridge is included and get it in writing, per the what stays with the house guide.

Questions about the fridge, the pantry and showing a NJ kitchen

Should I empty the refrigerator before showings?

Not empty, but half full and clean. Buyers open it, and a fridge they can see the back of reads as a kitchen with enough storage. Take out anything that does not need cold, toss what is expired, wipe the shelves and leave the door shelves with room to spare.

Do buyers really open the fridge during a showing?

Yes. In most New Jersey sales the refrigerator conveys with the house, so buyers treat it as an appliance to inspect. They check cleanliness, smell, whether the seals close and how much room there is. Expect the pantry and the cabinets to be opened as well.

What foods should never go in the refrigerator?

The chef House Outlook featured keeps tomatoes, onions, garlic, potatoes, bread, honey, coffee, hot sauce, whole melons, bananas and unripe stone fruit out of the fridge because cold hurts their texture or does nothing for them. In a listed home, those items belong in a closed pantry bin, not on the counter.

Does the refrigerator stay with the house in New Jersey?

Usually, but it depends on the contract. Appliances are typically listed as included or excluded in the listing and confirmed during attorney review. If you plan to take the fridge, say so up front. If a buyer wants it included, get it in writing before signing.

How do I get rid of a food smell before a showing?

Find the source first. Ripening produce on the counter, an onion bin, a full trash can or something old at the back of the fridge are the usual causes. Remove it, wipe the surfaces and run the exhaust fan. Candles and sprays layered over a smell make buyers suspicious rather than comfortable.
