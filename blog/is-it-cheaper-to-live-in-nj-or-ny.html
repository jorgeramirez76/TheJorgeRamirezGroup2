Is It Cheaper to Live in New Jersey or New York?

Updated July 30, 2026 · by Jorge Ramirez, NJ License #1754604 · NJ licensed agent since 2017, License #1754604

For most people who work in Manhattan: cheaper on income tax, more expensive on property tax. Moving across the river removes the New York City resident income tax entirely — 3.078% to 3.876% of taxable income. A married couple with $250,000 of taxable income keeps about $9,466 a year that a New York City resident hands over. Whether you actually come out ahead depends on the property tax bill of the specific house you buy, because New Jersey’s property taxes are the highest in the country.

The one tax that actually disappears

Most comparisons of the two states get lost comparing bracket to bracket. That is not where the money is. The decisive item is that New York City’s personal income tax is levied on city residents. New York State’s own filing instructions label the section “New York City Resident Tax,” and the nonresident return, Form IT-203, is described as the return “for nonresidents and part-year residents of New York State.”

So if you live in Hoboken and work in Midtown, the sequence is:

  • You file New York Form IT-203 as a New York nonresident and pay New York State tax on your New York-source wages.
  • You pay no New York City resident tax. That is the saving, and it is the whole ballgame.
  • You file a New Jersey NJ-1040 and claim a credit for taxes paid to another jurisdiction on Schedule NJ-COJ. The credit is capped at the New Jersey tax on that same income.

Because New York’s tax on the same wages is usually the higher of the two, that credit commonly erases the New Jersey liability completely. You do not pay twice.

New Jersey and New York have no reciprocal agreement. New Jersey’s only reciprocal personal income tax agreement is with Pennsylvania — and the NJ-1040 instructions are explicit that even that one “does not apply to the wage or income tax of Philadelphia.” New York wages run through the nonresident return and the NJ-COJ credit instead.

What that looks like in dollars

Here is the part most comparisons get wrong, and it makes the arithmetic simpler rather than harder. Your New York State tax is the same either way. If your wages are earned in New York, New York taxes them whether you sleep in Manhattan or Hoboken — as a resident on the IT-201, or as a nonresident on the IT-203. That figure cancels out of the comparison.

What does not cancel is the New York City resident tax. That is the whole saving, and it is computed straight from the city’s rate schedule:

HouseholdNYC resident taxSame household in NJAnnual saving
Married filing jointly, $250,000 NYC taxable income$9,466$0$9,466
Single filer, $120,000 NYC taxable income$4,526$0$4,526

Those come from the 2025 city schedule: 3.078% on the first band, rising to 3.876% above $90,000 of city taxable income for joint filers and above $50,000 for a single filer. Unlike the state tax, the city tax has no separate high-income computation — the rate schedule is the whole story.

New Jersey does tax the same income, but you claim a credit for what you paid New York on Schedule NJ-COJ, capped at the New Jersey tax on that income. Because New York’s tax on New York wages is normally the higher of the two, that credit commonly wipes out the New Jersey liability entirely at these income levels. It is a credit, though, not an exemption — at high enough income New Jersey’s own rates catch up and a balance starts to be due, so treat $0 as true for a typical commuter household rather than a universal rule.

Why we are not printing a single “total tax bill” figure. Above $107,650 of New York adjusted gross income, New York does not let you use the rate schedule at all — the IT-201 instructions send you to a separate tax computation that claws back the benefit of the lower brackets. That worksheet keys off adjusted gross income, not taxable income, so any total we printed would depend on deductions we cannot know. The saving above is unaffected, because the state tax is identical on both sides of the move.

The bracket comparison, for completeness

New Jersey’s schedule starts lower and stays lower through the middle of the income range. For joint filers, New Jersey opens at 1.4% and does not reach 6.37% until $150,000, while New York opens at 4% and is already at 6% above $161,550.

This is the part that gets reported backwards, including by me in an earlier draft of this page. New Jersey is not simply the cheaper state on income tax. It is much cheaper at the bottom — 1.4% to 3.5% where New York already charges 4% to 5.5% — but for joint filers New York’s marginal rate is actually the lower of the two from roughly $80,000 to $323,200, which is where a great many households in these towns sit. New Jersey charges 5.525% and then 6.37% through that stretch while New York is on 5.5% and then 6%.

New Jersey pulls back ahead from about $323,200 to $500,000 (6.37% against New York’s 6.85%), then falls behind again above $500,000, where New Jersey jumps to 8.97% and then 10.75% above $1,000,000 while a New York joint filer stays on 6.85% until $2,155,350.

So the state-versus-state comparison genuinely cuts both ways depending on your income. That is exactly why it is not the thing that decides this question — the New York City resident tax is, because a New Jersey resident stops paying it entirely at every income level.

Sales tax: a real but smaller edge to New Jersey

New Jersey’s sales tax is 6.625%. New York City’s combined state and local rate is 8.875%, per New York’s own jurisdiction rate table. On $40,000 of taxable household spending that is a difference of about $900 a year.

Two New Jersey wrinkles worth knowing. The state exempts most clothing and footwear and most grocery food outright. And a number of economically distressed cities are designated Urban Enterprise Zones, where qualifying retail sales are charged at half the statewide rate — Elizabeth, Newark, Paterson and Jersey City among them.

Where New Jersey costs more, and it is the deciding number

Property tax. New Jersey’s are the highest in the nation, and this single line can erase the income tax saving without much trouble. A New York City renter has no property tax line at all. A city condo or co-op owner generally pays a lower effective rate against a lower assessment, offset by maintenance charges.

Here is that in numbers, because the point is easy to make abstractly and easy to ignore. New Jersey publishes the average residential property tax bill for every municipality. In 2025 the average bill in Hoboken was $9,531 and in Montclair $22,487 — a gap of $12,956 a year, which is about 1.4 times the $9,466 city tax you saved by leaving New York. Jersey City averages $11,249 against Millburn’s $26,292, a $15,043 gap. Those are town averages and they reflect each town’s average home value rather than the bill on an identically priced house — but they show the scale of the thing that decides this comparison. Pick the wrong town and the income tax saving is gone before you unpack.

This is why a state-versus-state average is close to useless for an actual decision. The comparison turns on the tax bill of one specific house. Look at what New Jersey property tax rates actually mean in dollars before you shortlist towns, and check the commuter towns with the lowest property taxes and read how to appeal a New Jersey assessment if the bill on a house you love looks wrong.

One more federal wrinkle. Moving does not change your federal tax. But the federal deduction for state and local taxes has been capped since 2018, which limits how much of a large New Jersey property tax bill you can write off — so the sticker cost of that bill is closer to the real cost than it used to be.

How to actually run the comparison

Take your New York City resident tax from last year’s return — that is your annual saving, near enough. Then get the real tax bill on two or three houses you would genuinely buy. Then add the commute, on the New Jersey side of the ledger rather than the New York side — a monthly rail pass runs roughly $400 to $450, so about $5,000 to $5,400 a year before parking, and that is a real cost of living across the river. If the property tax plus the commute exceeds your city tax saving by more than the rent-versus-mortgage difference, New York was cheaper for you. If it does not, New Jersey comes out ahead — but price all three, because any one of them can swallow the whole saving.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it cheaper to live in NJ or NY?

Do I pay New York City income tax if I live in New Jersey and work in Manhattan?

No. The New York City personal income tax applies to New York City residents. If you live in New Jersey you file New York Form IT-203 as a New York nonresident, which covers New York State tax on your New York-source wages but not the city resident tax. New York State's own instructions label that section "New York City Resident Tax."

Do I get taxed twice if I live in NJ and work in NY?

No. You pay New York State nonresident tax on your New York-source wages, then file a New Jersey return and claim a credit for taxes paid to another jurisdiction on Schedule NJ-COJ. The credit is limited to the New Jersey tax on that same income. Because New York's tax is usually higher than New Jersey's on the same wages, the credit commonly wipes out the New Jersey liability entirely.

Does New Jersey have a reciprocal tax agreement with New York?

No. New Jersey's only reciprocal personal income tax agreement is with Pennsylvania, and even that one does not cover the Philadelphia wage tax. New York wages are handled through the nonresident return and the Schedule NJ-COJ credit, not reciprocity.

Which state has lower income tax rates, NJ or NY?

Neither, cleanly. Is sales tax lower in New Jersey?

Yes. New Jersey's sales tax is 6.625% and it exempts most clothing and footwear and most grocery food. New York City's combined state and local rate is 8.875%. New Jersey also has Urban Enterprise Zones in a number of cities where qualifying retail sales are charged at half the statewide rate.

Where does New Jersey cost more than New York?

Property tax, and it is not close — New Jersey's are the highest in the country. A New York City renter pays no property tax line at all, and a city condo or co-op owner typically pays a lower effective rate on a lower assessment. Does moving to New Jersey lower my federal taxes?
