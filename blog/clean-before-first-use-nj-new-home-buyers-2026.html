10 Things to Clean Before First Use in Your New NJ Home

The keys are yours. Before you run the dishwasher, wash the first load, or bake in the new pans — here's what to clean first, and what each one tells you about the home you just bought.

"Broom clean" is not the same as clean

Every NJ purchase contract I've handled in 15 years — more than 500 of them across Union, Morris, Essex, Hudson, and Middlesex counties — requires the seller to deliver the home in broom-clean condition. Buyers hear that and assume the home will be ready to live in. It won't be. Broom clean means the seller's belongings and debris are gone. It says nothing about the dishwasher filter, the inside of the fridge, or the washing machine gasket.

I tell every buyer closing in Montclair, Westfield, Morristown, or Jersey City the same thing at the final walkthrough: the first weekend in the house is for cleaning things before you use them. Partly that's hygiene. But there's a bigger reason. The first clean is when you find out whether each appliance and system actually works the way the inspection said it did — while you still have the seller's disclosure, the inspection report, and any home warranty in hand.

Here's the list. It covers the home you inherited and the new things you bought for it.

Jorge's observation: The buyers who find problems in week one are the ones who cleaned. The buyers who find problems in month six are the ones who assumed. Week-one problems have options — a warranty claim, a conversation with your attorney about a disclosure. Month-six problems are just repairs.

The 10 things to clean before first use

  1. The refrigerator — shelves, drawers, gasket, and drip pan Pull every shelf and drawer and wash them in warm soapy water. Wipe the interior with a baking soda solution, not bleach. Clean the door gasket where crumbs and mildew collect, and check the drip pan underneath. While it's empty, listen to it run. A fridge that cycles constantly or a drip pan full of standing water is something to note now, not after the first grocery trip.
    Time: 45 minutes · Reveals: Compressor and drain condition
  2. The dishwasher — filter first, then a hot empty cycle The filter at the bottom of the tub is the part previous owners never cleaned. Pull it, rinse it, scrub the mesh. Wipe the door gasket and the spray arms. Then run a hot cycle with a dishwasher cleaner or a cup of white vinegar, empty. If it drains slowly or leaves standing water, you've found a problem worth a phone call.
    Time: 30 minutes · Reveals: Drain and pump condition
  3. The washing machine — drum, gasket, and dispenser drawer Front-loaders in NJ homes are mildew factories if the door has been kept closed. Wipe the rubber gasket fold with a vinegar solution, pull and wash the dispenser drawer, and run a hot cleaning cycle empty. If the gasket is black and won't come clean, it needs replacing — a $50 part that saves every load of laundry from smelling like a basement.
    Time: 30 minutes · Reveals: Mildew and seal condition
  4. The oven, range, and range hood filter Run the self-clean cycle with the windows open, or use a non-toxic oven cleaner if the oven is older. Pull the burner grates and soak them. Degrease the range hood filter or replace it. This is also when you learn whether the oven holds temperature — put an oven thermometer inside during the first preheat. An oven that runs 25 degrees off is a common finding inspections don't catch.
    Time: 1–2 hours (mostly unattended) · Reveals: Oven calibration
  5. New metal bakeware Cookie sheets, muffin tins, and cake pans often ship with a thin protective coating from manufacturing. Heated for the first time, that coating can smoke, smell, and affect how food tastes. Wash everything in warm soapy water and dry it completely before it goes near the oven. Same goes for new cast iron, which needs a wash and a seasoning before use.
    Time: 10 minutes · Prevents: Smoke and off-flavors on first bake
  6. New pillows, sheets, and towels They sat in warehouses and on store shelves for months, picking up dust, handling, and chemical odors from packaging. Sheets and towels get a hot wash before the first night. Pillows with removable covers get the cover washed; polyester, down, and feather fills tolerate a gentle machine wash in a mesh bag, while memory foam and wool should be spot-cleaned only. New towels also shed less and absorb more after a first wash.
    Time: One laundry cycle · Prevents: Dust and packaging odors in bed
  7. New sponges, dish brushes, and cutting boards They collect manufacturing dust like anything else, and a cutting board that hasn't been washed carries residue from the factory finish. Wash before first use. Wooden boards get a wash and a coat of food-safe mineral oil.
    Time: 5 minutes · Easy to forget, easy to do
  8. Bathroom fixtures, exhaust fans, and the medicine cabinet Disinfect every surface, then pop the cover off each bath exhaust fan and vacuum the blades. A choked fan is the reason the previous owner's bathroom had that mildew line on the ceiling, and it's the first thing to fix before the moisture becomes your problem. Check inside the medicine cabinet and vanity for anything left behind.
    Time: 30 minutes per bathroom · Reveals: Ventilation condition
  9. HVAC filter and supply registers Replace the furnace filter on day one — you have no idea when it was last changed, and a clogged filter is the most common reason a system underperforms. Vacuum every supply register and return grille. If what comes out of the registers is thick, dark dust, the ductwork may have never been cleaned, and that's worth a quote.
    Cost: $15–$30 for a filter · Reveals: Duct condition
  10. Door hardware, light switches, and the garage door opener These are the highest-touch surfaces in the home and the ones nobody cleans before handing over the keys. Wipe every switch plate, doorknob, and handle. Then rekey or replace the exterior locks and reset the garage door opener codes. You have no way of knowing how many copies of the old keys exist in Cranford or Chatham or wherever the seller's relatives live.
    Cost: $100–$250 to rekey · Non-negotiable for security

Why I hand this list to buyers at the final walkthrough

A home inspection tests whether things turn on. It doesn't clean them, and it doesn't run a dishwasher long enough to find a slow drain or open a washing machine gasket to find mildew. Those discoveries happen when you clean. If you make them in the first week — while the seller's disclosure is fresh, the inspection report is on the counter, and any home warranty is active — you have options. If you make them in month six, you just have a repair bill.

The other reason is simpler. Buyers in Summit, Maplewood, and Hoboken have just spent months and a great deal of money getting into this home. The first night should feel like yours. Clean sheets, a fridge that doesn't smell like someone else's groceries, an oven that doesn't smoke when you preheat it. That's a good first night, and it takes one weekend to earn it.

Frequently asked questions

Isn't the home supposed to be clean at closing in NJ?

Standard NJ contracts require broom-clean condition: the seller's personal property and debris are gone. That's a low bar. It doesn't mean the dishwasher filter was cleared, the fridge coils vacuumed, or the washer gasket wiped. Assume the appliances need a first clean before use — both for hygiene and because that first clean is where you learn whether each one actually works properly.

What should I clean first after closing on a NJ home?

Start with what you'll use on day one: the refrigerator, the dishwasher filter plus a hot empty cycle, and the washing machine drum and gasket. Then the range and oven before the first meal. New bakeware, pillows, sheets, towels, and sponges get washed before they go into service. The whole list fits in the first weekend.

Why do new pillows and bakeware need washing before use?

New metal bakeware often ships with a thin protective coating that can smoke, burn, or affect taste the first time it's heated — warm soapy water removes it. Pillows and bedding sit in warehouses for months picking up dust and packaging odors. Removable covers can be machine washed; polyester, down, and feather fills tolerate a gentle wash, while memory foam and wool should be spot-cleaned only.

Does this checklist help me find problems the inspection missed?

Yes. An inspection tests whether appliances turn on and run a cycle; it doesn't clean them. Running a hot empty dishwasher cycle, checking the fridge drip pan, and wiping the washer gasket are the moments you discover a slow drain, mildew, or a leak. Finding those in week one — with the disclosure and inspection report in hand — is much better than finding them in month six.

Should I hire a move-in cleaning service in NJ?

A professional move-in clean before the furniture arrives is one of the best few hundred dollars a NJ buyer can spend, especially for kitchens and bathrooms. But most services do surfaces, not appliance interiors, filters, and gaskets. Use this list as the hand-off: ask the cleaner to include these items specifically, or handle them yourself the first weekend.
