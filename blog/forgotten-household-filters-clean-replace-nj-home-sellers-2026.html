9 Forgotten Filters in Your Home: The Cheap Maintenance That Shows Up in a NJ Inspection

House Outlook’s list of nine forgotten household filters is a maintenance article, but I read it as a selling article. When a buyer’s inspector in New Jersey opens the furnace door, the first thing in hand is the filter, and a gray, bowed, clogged one tells a story about the whole house before a single system is tested. Here is the list, what each filter says to a buyer, and the order I have sellers tackle them in the month before listing.

The House Outlook list

The article covers the filters people forget because they are out of sight: the furnace or air handler filter, the range hood grease screens, the dishwasher filter, the washing machine inlet screens and pump trap, the vacuum filter, the refrigerator water filter, the dryer lint screen and vent, and the humidifier pad. It gives intervals for each. I would add one more that matters in New Jersey, the whole-house or well sediment filter, and I would reorder the list by what a buyer actually sees.

Why filters matter in a New Jersey sale

An inspector cannot tell from the outside whether a furnace in a Morristown colonial was serviced every fall. The filter is the proxy. Clean and dated, the inspector moves on. Clogged, the report suggests a service call and the buyer starts asking for the age of every system. The same thing happens in the kitchen. Buyers in Edison and Woodbridge open the dishwasher at showings; a filter full of food is the first thing they smell. A range hood screen dripping grease over a gas range in a Union or Elizabeth two-family reads as a fire hazard, because it is.

Nine filters, in the order a buyer finds them

  1. Furnace or air handler filter. Replace it, write the date on the frame in marker, and leave a spare on the shelf. The inspector pulls this one first.
  2. Dryer lint screen and vent. Clean the screen, then disconnect the duct and clear it to the outside hood. Lint in the vent is the inspection finding sellers argue about most and should not; the dryer vent guide covers the method.
  3. Range hood grease screens. Soak them in hot water and dish soap or run them through the dishwasher if the manufacturer allows it. Check that the hood actually vents outside; many in older Hudson County condos just recirculate.
  4. Dishwasher filter. Twist it out of the floor of the tub, rinse, run an empty hot cycle. Buyers open the dishwasher. The dishwasher cleaning guide has the full reset.
  5. Refrigerator water filter. If the fridge stays with the house and the indicator light is red, replace it. A buyer who sees the light assumes nothing else was replaced either.
  6. Washing machine inlet screens and pump trap. Shut the water, pull the hoses, clean the screens, and clear the pump trap at the bottom of a front-loader. If the hoses are rubber and older than five years, replace them with braided steel while you are there.
  7. Humidifier and dehumidifier. Replace the pad on a furnace humidifier and clean the filter and coil on a basement dehumidifier. A moldy pad smells through the whole house.
  8. Whole-house or well sediment filter. In parts of Morris County and the outer towns on well water, a brown sediment cartridge in the basement is the first thing a buyer’s inspector photographs. Replace it and keep the well test paperwork with it.
  9. Vacuum filter. The only one on the list buyers never see, but a clogged vacuum spreads dust during every showing cleanup. Clean it before the dusting routine starts.

Jorge’s tip: Put the receipts and the dates in a folder on the kitchen counter during showings, with the service records for the furnace and water heater. A buyer who sees a dated furnace filter and a folder of receipts stops looking for problems and starts picturing moving in. That folder is the cheapest staging in the house.

What this does not fix

A new filter does not fix a furnace at the end of its life, a dryer vent that runs thirty feet through a crawl space, or a range hood that was never ducted outside. It removes the cheap findings from the report so the real ones stand on their own, and it gives you a chance to find the real ones first. If the furnace is older than twenty years, read the furnace check guide and decide with your agent whether to service, disclose or price around it.

For buyers: pull the filter yourself

At a second showing in Summit, Chatham or Millburn, ask to see the furnace filter and the dryer vent hood outside. A clean, dated filter and a vent flap that opens freely tell you more about how the house was kept than the fresh paint does. If the filters are clogged, assume the service records are thin and ask your inspector to spend extra time on the systems.

What this does for the sale

None of this changes the appraisal. It changes the length of the inspection report and the tone of the repair request that follows it. In my experience, a buyer who finds a clean house behind every door negotiates on the big items only. A buyer who finds neglect behind every door negotiates on everything.

Questions about filters, maintenance and inspections

Will replacing filters raise my sale price?

No. It shortens the inspection report and changes the tone of the buyer’s repair request. Price is set by the house, the comparable sales and the market, but a long list of small findings is how deals lose ground in attorney review.

How often should I change the furnace filter?

Follow the manufacturer’s interval for your filter type, often every one to three months for a standard one-inch filter and longer for thicker media filters. Before listing, replace it regardless and write the date on the frame.

Does a home inspector check the dryer vent?

Most do, and a lint-packed vent is a common finding. Clean the lint screen, clear the duct to the outside hood, and make sure the exterior flap opens. Replace plastic or foil duct with rigid or semi-rigid metal.

Do I have to replace the refrigerator water filter before selling?

Only if the refrigerator stays with the house and the indicator shows it is due. It is a small cost that keeps a buyer from assuming the rest of the house was neglected. Confirm in the contract which appliances are included.

Should I leave the receipts for buyers to see?

Yes. Keep the filter receipts, furnace and water heater service records and any well or water test results in one folder for showings and for the inspection. Documented maintenance answers questions before they are asked.
