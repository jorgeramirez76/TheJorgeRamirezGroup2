15 Things NJ Home Sellers Must Get Rid of Before the First Showing

Published  ·  August 2026

Short answer

Buyers aren't just evaluating your home — they're scanning it for evidence of how well it's been maintained. Worn textiles, broken items, expired products, excess furniture, and too many personal photos all send the same signal: this home hasn't been carefully tended. These 15 things to remove before your first NJ showing are low effort, zero cost, and directly affect what buyers feel when they walk through.

Buyers decide in the first eight minutes. What they notice isn't what you spent money on — it's what you forgot to put away. Here's the list.

What Buyers Actually See When They Walk In

After 15 years of listing NJ homes and reading showing feedback, one pattern is consistent: buyers rarely say "the kitchen is too small." They say "it felt cluttered" or "there wasn't enough counter space" — which is often a different problem entirely. The room is the right size. What's in it is too much.

Buyers are unconsciously scanning for evidence of care. A house full of worn items, expired products left in plain sight, broken things that were never fixed, and years of accumulated objects reads as a home that hasn't been well maintained — even when the underlying structure is perfectly sound. The connection between clutter and perceived condition is direct and consistent.

Here are 15 specific things to remove before your first NJ showing. None of them cost money. All of them affect what buyers feel and what they offer.

The Kitchen

1. Everything on the Countertops (Almost)

Kitchen countertop clutter is the number one thing buyers comment on in showing feedback for NJ homes. Coffee makers, toasters, knife blocks, paper towel holders, fruit bowls, mail, charging stations — all of it makes the kitchen feel smaller and less functional than it is. For the listing period, keep only what's essential: one appliance you use daily, a clean dish soap and sponge holder at the sink. Pack the rest or put it in a cabinet. Buyers need to see counter space, not your kitchen collection.

2. Expired Pantry Items and Cluttered Cabinets

Buyers open cabinets. Every cabinet. They want to see storage capacity, and what they actually see is how organized the current occupants are. A pantry packed to the ceiling with items stacked behind other items — some of which expired two years ago — signals a home where the owners have outgrown the storage. Go through your pantry before listing. Purge anything expired. Organize what remains so shelves have breathing room. Then buyers see storage capacity, not chaos.

3. Bulky Small Appliances You Don't Use Weekly

The air fryer you bought in 2022 and use twice a year, the stand mixer still in the box, the juicer that's been under the counter since the pandemic. If it's not daily or weekly use, it goes into storage for the listing period. Every item on a counter subtracts perceived space. In NJ kitchens where square footage is already limited — common in older Colonials in Cranford, Westfield, and Rahway — clear counters create the illusion of a kitchen that works.

The Bathrooms

4. Every Worn Towel and Bath Mat

Bathrooms are make-or-break rooms in NJ home showings. The first thing buyers notice after the tile and fixtures is the linens. Ratty towels, fraying bath mats, mismatched sets — they go in the trash or storage for the listing period. Buy two sets of matching white or neutral towels for each bathroom. Stack them neatly on towel bars or fold them on the edge of the tub. It costs $40-$80 per bathroom and changes the perception of the space immediately. White towels read clean, fresh, and hotel-quality. Buyers notice.

5. Everything on the Counter and in the Shower

Seven shampoo bottles in the shower. Ten product containers on the bathroom counter. A hairdryer cord draped over the sink. Before every showing, the bathroom counter should have almost nothing on it: hand soap, one candle or small plant if you have it. The shower should have one or two bottles maximum — buy a shower caddy and hang it out of sight. Buyers open shower doors and medicine cabinets. What they find there affects their comfort level with the home.

6. The Cabinet Under the Sink

Buyers check under bathroom sinks for water damage. What they find is usually a pile of products, cleaning supplies, and miscellaneous items packed in with zero organization. Clean it out. A few organized items in a small basket is fine. A full cabinet of products and garbage bags signals no attention to the space — and makes buyers wonder what's behind the clutter.

Bedrooms and Closets

7. Half Your Closet

Closet space is one of the top three buyer priorities in NJ homes across every price point. A closet that's packed to capacity signals inadequate storage. Before listing, remove approximately half of what's in every closet — off-season clothes, rarely worn items, anything that doesn't need to be accessible. What remains should be organized with space between items. Buyers who open a closet and see breathing room mentally add storage to the home. Buyers who open a packed closet start thinking about where they'll put their things.

8. Too Many Personal Photos

A few family photos in a living space are warm and human. An entire gallery wall in the hallway, every surface in the bedroom covered with pictures, and personalized art throughout the house makes it impossible for buyers to see themselves in the home. They're visitors in your house rather than prospective owners of their future home. Remove photos from common areas and minimize them in bedrooms. You're not erasing your life — you're helping buyers imagine theirs.

9. Worn or Mismatched Bedding

Beds are prominent features of bedrooms. The bedding is what buyers see first and what photographs in every listing photo. Pilling, fading, mismatched pillowcases, or bedding that looks like it's been through five years of use undermines an otherwise nice room. Before listing, invest in a crisp neutral duvet or comforter for each bedroom — white, ivory, or a soft gray. Coordinating shams are a bonus but not required. This runs $60-$150 per bed and is one of the higher-ROI pre-listing investments I recommend.

Living Spaces

10. Extra Furniture That Fills Every Wall

Most NJ sellers have too much furniture, not too little. Multiple coffee tables, end tables on both sides of every chair, furniture filling every corner — it makes rooms feel cramped and busy. Before listing, remove at least one major piece from your main living area and consider removing additional accent pieces. A sofa, a chair or two, a coffee table, and one side table is usually enough for a living room. Buyers can imagine adding things. They can't ignore what's already there.

11. Broken Items That Have Been Waiting to Be Fixed

That drawer that sticks. The cabinet door that swings open on its own. The towel bar that pulls off the wall. The light fixture with a dead bulb. Every broken item in a home has two effects: it reduces buyer confidence in the property's overall condition, and it becomes a negotiating item after inspection. Fix the small things before listing. Hire a handyman for a half-day if needed. The cost is typically $150-$300 for a list of minor items. The return is buyers who walk through without cataloguing problems.

12. Exercise Equipment That Lives in the Open

A treadmill in the living room or master bedroom is one of the most common space misusers I see in NJ listings. It makes rooms feel smaller, suggests the home doesn't have dedicated space for activities, and photographs terribly. Move it to the garage or basement for the listing period, or store it if needed. If the equipment is in a dedicated room — a proper home gym setup — that's different. A treadmill jammed in the corner of a bedroom is not a selling point.

Common Areas, Entry, and Storage

13. The Entry Dumping Zone

In most NJ homes, the entry or foyer is where everything accumulates: shoes in a pile by the door, coats hung on every hook and piled on chairs, bags hanging from the banister, mail on a side table or bench. Buyers form their first impression of a home within the first 30 seconds. If the entry looks like a storage unit, the rest of the showing starts from a deficit. For the listing period, the entry should have one or two shoes maximum, coats out of sight, nothing on the stairs or railing. It's a two-minute reset that changes the entire opening of every showing.

14. Garage Clutter and Evidence of Limited Storage

NJ buyers in the $600K+ range expect functional garages. When they walk into a garage so packed they can't see the floor or the walls, two things happen: they conclude there's inadequate storage in the home, and they wonder what's being hidden. Clean the garage before listing. Organize tools, sports equipment, and seasonal items on shelving. A garage that has breathing room suggests a home with enough storage for the owners' lives — which is exactly what buyers are looking for.

15. Products and Items That Have Expired — Everywhere

Expired medications in the bathroom cabinet. Outdated cleaning supplies under the sink. Old sunscreen in a basket. Buyers who are thorough (and they are) open everything and look at everything. Expired products — particularly medications and health items — signal neglect and raise unconscious discomfort. Go room by room before listing and remove anything with an expiration date that's passed. While you're at it, organize what remains. An organized medicine cabinet and pantry signals owners who manage their home carefully. It matters more than you'd think.

The practical approach: Rent a storage unit or order a portable storage pod for the listing period. Move out about 30-40% more than feels comfortable. What's left should be everything you actually use and nothing you've been tolerating. In NJ markets where buyers often make decisions on a Saturday afternoon after seeing 10 homes, the cleanest, most spacious-feeling home in the price range wins disproportionately. Decluttering isn't just aesthetics — it's competitive strategy.

The Buyer's Point of View: What They're Actually Scanning For

I walk through homes with buyers every week. The conversation that happens in the car afterward tells me exactly what they noticed. It's almost never about square footage. It's almost always about feeling. "The kitchen felt cramped." "The bathroom looked tired." "I couldn't tell how much storage there was." "It felt like a lot was going on."

What they're actually reacting to is: a cluttered kitchen that wasn't prepared for a showing. Worn bathroom linens that weren't replaced. Closets so full of stuff that storage capacity wasn't apparent. Too many personal items and accumulated objects that made the house feel lived-in rather than available.

None of these things are structural. None of them require renovation. They require editing — which costs time, not money. That's what makes pre-listing decluttering one of the highest-ROI actions available to a NJ seller. You're spending a weekend of effort to potentially affect a number with a comma in it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I get rid of before selling my NJ home?

Before listing, remove: excess furniture making rooms feel crowded, all worn or mismatched textiles, personal photos in volume, broken items, expired products throughout the home, oversized appliances on counters, and anything in closets and storage you're not actively using. The goal is for buyers to see the home, not your belongings. Less is consistently more in NJ showings.

Do personal photos hurt a home sale in NJ?

In volume, yes. A few framed photos are warm and human. An entire gallery wall and surfaces covered with pictures makes it harder for buyers to imagine themselves in the space. NJ buyers — especially those relocating from out of state who may only visit once — need to project themselves into the home. Heavy personalization makes that harder and shows up in showing feedback consistently.

How much decluttering is enough for a NJ home listing?

A useful benchmark: if you're planning to move, start with everything you wouldn't put in the moving truck on day one. Rent a storage unit or pod if needed. I tell sellers: remove 30-40% more than feels comfortable. A decluttered home that reads as spacious and well-maintained gets more attention and stronger offers than a cluttered home at the same price in NJ's competitive market.

Should I remove all furniture before listing my NJ home?

Not all — but often more than feels right. The most common pre-listing problem I see is too much furniture, not too little. One sofa and two chairs in a living room reads as open. Two sofas, three chairs, and side tables everywhere reads as crowded regardless of actual square footage. In smaller NJ homes in the 1,600-2,000 sq ft range, removing one major piece per room often makes a measurable difference in how the home photographs and shows.

What do NJ buyers notice most during a showing?

Based on 15 years of showing feedback: kitchen countertop clutter, bathroom condition (towels and grout), closet space, the first 30 seconds at the entry, and any visible repair items. Buyers are scanning for evidence of care. Worn textiles, expired products, broken items, and clutter all signal the same thing: this home hasn't been carefully maintained. Remove them before the first showing.
