The "Five Things" Method: How Overwhelmed NJ Sellers Get Show-Ready Fast

A showing request comes in for 5:30. It's 4:15. The living room looks like a family lives in it. You don't need a plan — you need five categories. Here's the method that turns panic into a 40-minute routine.

Why a messy room freezes sellers — and costs them money

In my years of selling homes across Union, Morris, Essex, Hudson, and Middlesex counties, I can tell you the hardest part of selling isn't pricing or negotiation. It's the six weeks of living inside a home that has to look like nobody lives there. Sellers in Westfield, Maplewood, and Morristown tell me the same thing: the showing request comes in, they look at the room, and they freeze. Where do you even start?

That freeze is the real problem. Buyers who walk into a home with dishes in the sink, laundry on the couch, and a coffee table buried in mail don't think "busy family." They think "what else hasn't been taken care of?" That doubt shows up in offers. I've watched it cost sellers real money in markets where a clean, cared-for home is supporting full asking price.

The fix I now recommend to every client comes from KC Davis, a licensed therapist and the author of How to Keep House While Drowning. She calls it the Five Things method. It was built for people who feel overwhelmed by mess — which, during a listing, is every seller I've ever worked with.

Jorge's observation: The reason a messy room feels impossible is that you're trying to make a hundred small decisions at once. The Five Things method turns those hundred decisions into five. That's the whole trick, and it works every single time.

The Five Things method, adapted for NJ showings

Every messy room contains only five kinds of things. You walk through the room five times, handling one category per pass. Don't touch anything outside the current category. Here's how I coach sellers to run it.

  1. Trash Grab a bag and sweep the room for anything that gets thrown out: packaging, junk mail, empty cups, receipts, the kids' snack wrappers. Don't sort, don't read, don't reconsider. Trash only. This pass takes five minutes and instantly makes the room look 30 percent better because visual noise is mostly trash.
    Time: 5 minutes · Tools: One trash bag
  2. Laundry Every piece of clothing, towel, blanket, or throw that doesn't belong where it is goes into one basket. Jackets on chairs, socks under the coffee table, the gym bag by the door. One trip to the laundry room or closet. Buyers notice clothing more than almost anything else because it reads as intimate — it makes them feel like they're intruding on your life instead of picturing their own.
    Time: 5 minutes · Tools: One laundry basket
  3. Dishes Every cup, plate, bowl, and water bottle in the room goes to the kitchen. Straight into the dishwasher — not the sink. An empty sink is one of the details buyers photograph and send to their agent, and a full one gets noticed just as fast. If the dishwasher is full and can't run before the showing, hand-wash and put away. Never leave a drying rack full.
    Time: 5–10 minutes · Rule: Empty sink, always
  4. Things that have a home This is the pass where the room comes together. Remote to the console. Shoes to the closet. Books back on the shelf. Chargers to the drawer. Kids' toys to the bin. These items already have a designated spot — you're just returning them. If you staged the home properly before listing, this pass should be fast because everything you kept was given a place.
    Time: 10–15 minutes · Impact: The room looks staged again
  5. Things that don't have a home Everything left over is here because nobody ever decided where it lives. During a listing, you don't solve that problem at 4:45 PM. You put it in the clutter bin — or, better, the dedicated clutter closet I recommend every seller set up before going live. After the showing, you can decide whether each item gets a home, gets donated, or gets boxed for the move.
    Time: 5–10 minutes · Tools: One lidded bin or a clutter closet

Where sellers go wrong with it

The method fails when people mix categories. You pick up a mug, notice a sweater on the way to the kitchen, grab the sweater, spot the mail under it, start reading the mail, and ten minutes later you're standing in the hallway holding three things with no progress. One category per pass. Be disciplined about it. That's the entire reason it works.

The second mistake is running it room by room instead of category by category through the whole main floor. If you have 45 minutes, do the trash pass through the living room, kitchen, and entryway together, then the laundry pass through all three, and so on. Buyers see the home as a sequence — the entry, the living room, the kitchen — so those spaces need to be finished together.

The third mistake is skipping the setup. The method is ten times faster when every kept item already has a home and there's a clutter bin ready for the leftovers. That setup is what I do with sellers in Chatham, Cranford, and Glen Ridge during the pre-listing declutter — it's what makes the difference between a 40-minute reset and a two-hour scramble.

Why this matters for your sale price

I've sold homes in Summit and Short Hills where buyers said, unprompted, that the home "felt taken care of." That feeling comes from consistency: every showing, the home looked like the listing photos. It's the opposite of what happens when a seller gets burned out in week three, stops resetting before showings, and the home starts to show as lived-in. Buyers who see a home slipping wonder what else is slipping. That's when lowball offers and heavy inspection lists start.

The Five Things method is what keeps you consistent for 30, 45, or 60 days. It's sustainable because it's fast and because it never asks you to make a decision under pressure. That's why I teach it to every seller before we go live.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Five Things method for cleaning?

Created by therapist and author KC Davis, it breaks any messy room into five categories: trash, laundry, dishes, things that have a home, and things that don't. You sweep the room for one category at a time instead of trying to clean everything at once. It removes the decision fatigue that makes a messy room feel impossible — which is why it works so well for NJ sellers living in a listed home.

How long does the Five Things method take before a showing?

For a NJ home that's already decluttered and staged, a full pass through the main living areas takes 30 to 45 minutes. Trash and dishes are fast. Laundry is one armful. The last two categories take the longest, which is why every seller should have a clutter bin or closet ready before listing.

Does a messy home really affect the offer price in NJ?

Yes. Buyers in Summit, Westfield, Montclair, and Cranford make fast judgments about maintenance based on how a home is kept. Dishes on the counter and laundry on the couch make buyers wonder what else has been neglected — and that doubt shows up as lower offers or heavier inspection requests.

Should I skip this and just hire a cleaner before showings?

A professional deep clean before photos and the first weekend is a great investment. But you can't hire a cleaner for every showing over a 30-to-60-day listing. The Five Things method is the maintenance system between professional cleans — it's what lets a busy family keep a listed home show-ready without burning out.

What's the difference between things that have a home and things that don't?

Things that have a home have a designated spot — the remote goes on the console, shoes in the closet, mail in the tray. You just return them. Things that don't have a home are floating around because nobody decided where they live. Before listing, they get a home, get donated, or get boxed. During showings, they go into a clutter bin so they're out of sight in seconds.
