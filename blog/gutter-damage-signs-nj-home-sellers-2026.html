Gutter Damage Signs NJ Home Sellers Can't Ignore — What Inspectors Find Every Time

Gutters are the most overlooked item on most NJ sellers' prep list. They're the first thing the buyer's inspector documents. Here's what to look for — and what it costs you if you don't.

Why Gutters Show Up on Almost Every NJ Inspection Report

In my full-time NJ real estate work since 2017, I've reviewed inspection issues across Union, Essex, Morris, and Middlesex counties. Gutter deficiencies recur in those reports. Not because NJ homeowners are negligent — but because gutters are out of sight, require a ladder to check, and are the last thing anyone thinks about until the inspector hands the buyer a 40-page report with photographs of sagging sections and overflow staining.

Here's what makes this costly: buyers do not pay gutter repair prices at the negotiating table. They pay worst-case prices. A clogged gutter that would cost $200 to clean becomes a $1,500 credit demand when it's on the inspection report alongside photos of water staining along the foundation. The inspector has done their job. The buyer's agent does theirs. And the seller pays a multiple of what a simple seasonal cleaning would have cost.

NJ's housing stock doesn't help. Union County colonials, Essex County Victorians, and Morris County Capes were built in eras when gutters were steel or aluminum, installed once, and never thought about again. Many of those same gutters — now 30 to 50 years old — are still on the rooflines. They work until they don't, and the failure mode is always water in a place it shouldn't be.

If you're planning to sell, a $200 gutter cleaning and a 30-minute visual inspection from the ground can remove an entire category of inspection finding before the buyer's inspector ever shows up. Here's what to look for.

7 Gutter Warning Signs NJ Sellers Need to Address

1 Sagging or Pulling Away from the Fascia

Stand at the corner of your house and look down the roofline. Gutters should run in a straight, slightly angled line — sloping gently toward the downspout. If you see sections dipping, bowing, or pulling away from the fascia board, the mounting hardware has failed or the fascia itself has rotted. This is a structural issue, not a cosmetic one.

NJ Seller Takeaway

Sagging gutters pool standing water, which accelerates corrosion and adds weight — pulling more sections loose. A gutter company can reset sections and replace mounting straps for $75 to $200 per section. Ignore it and a buyer's inspector will photograph it from the ground with a telephoto lens. It always comes up in the report.

2 Overflow Staining on the Siding or Foundation

Look for vertical streaks of discoloration — brown, green, or black — running down your siding from the gutter line. Look along the foundation for soil erosion, mud splatter patterns, or a distinct line where water has been pouring over the gutter edge during rain. These are the physical signatures of chronic overflow — gutters that are clogged, undersized, or improperly sloped.

Overflow staining is more than cosmetic. It tells the inspector — and the buyer — that water has been consistently concentrated at your foundation and along your siding, two of the most expensive repair zones in the house.

NJ Seller Takeaway

Clean the staining from siding with a diluted TSP solution before listing photos are taken. Then fix the underlying cause. A pressure wash that removes the stain but leaves the clogged gutter tells the inspector everything they need to know during a rainstorm.

3 Fascia Board Rot

The fascia — the horizontal board running along the roofline where gutters mount — takes the most abuse from gutter overflow. Probe the fascia along problem gutter sections with a flathead screwdriver. If the wood is soft, spongy, or the screwdriver penetrates more than a quarter inch, the board is rotted. Rotted fascia cannot hold a gutter securely and creates an entry point for water into the roof assembly.

NJ Seller Takeaway

Fascia replacement runs $10 to $20 per linear foot for material plus labor. On a full-size NJ colonial, replacing a 10-foot section is a half-day job for a carpenter. It is not optional if the rot is present — inspectors probe fascia boards, and rotted wood triggers follow-up questions about whether water got into the roof deck or eaves.

4 Downspout Terminating at the Foundation

Walk around your house and find every downspout. Then see where each one ends. If it terminates flush with the foundation — or discharges onto a 12-inch concrete splash block — you have a problem. NJ inspectors look for downspouts that discharge at least 6 feet from the foundation, with 8 to 10 feet preferred in clay-heavy soil areas like much of Union and Essex counties.

Proper downspout extensions redirect water away from the foundation — Photo via Unsplash

NJ Seller Takeaway

Downspout extensions cost $15 to $30 at any hardware store and install in minutes. Underground drainage extensions run $200 to $500 installed. Either solution removes a flagged item that, without the fix, gives buyers evidence that your foundation may have chronic water exposure — which triggers requests for a structural engineer and potentially thousands in credit demands.

5 Missing or Disconnected Gutter Sections

Walk the full perimeter of your home and confirm that every section of gutter connects continuously to the next and terminates at a working downspout. Missing sections, gaps at joints, or downspouts that are disconnected from the gutter above mean rainwater is cascading directly off the roofline and concentrating at the foundation — sometimes for years before the homeowner notices.

NJ Seller Takeaway

A missing 10-foot gutter section can typically be replaced for $75 to $150 in materials plus a couple hours of labor. Left alone, it becomes the photograph on page 6 of the inspection report with a caption about "significant moisture intrusion risk."

6 Standing Water in Gutters Days After Rain

If you can safely look into your gutters from a ladder — or spot obvious debris from the ground — look for sections where water is sitting rather than draining. Standing water means one of three things: the gutter is clogged with debris, the slope is incorrect, or the downspout is blocked. All three are fixable. None should be left for the inspector to find.

NJ Seller Takeaway

A full gutter cleaning — including flushing downspouts and checking slope — runs $150 to $300 for a standard NJ colonial. If you're listing in the next 60 days, schedule one now. This single service eliminates the most common gutter deficiency that appears on NJ inspection reports.

7 Basement Moisture After Rainstorms

This one works backward from the symptom to the cause. If your basement gets wet during or after heavy rain — a NJ summer reality with the storms we get in July and August — gutters are the first thing to eliminate. Before you spend money on interior waterproofing systems, have someone flush your gutters and extend the downspouts. In many NJ homes I've listed, "basement water" turned out to be a $200 gutter cleaning and $80 in downspout extensions. Interior waterproofing would have cost $8,000.

NJ Seller Takeaway

Buyers react strongly to basement moisture. It triggers phrases like "foundation issues" and "water intrusion" on inspection reports, which immediately signals structural problems to buyers — even when the actual cause is a clogged gutter. Fix the gutter first, confirm the basement stays dry through a few rainstorms, then list.

What Happens When You List With Gutter Problems

A buyer's inspector will photograph every gutter deficiency they find. Those photographs go into a report that the buyer reviews with their agent. The agent uses that report to draft an inspection response — typically requesting credits, repairs, or both. In my full-time NJ real estate work since 2017, gutter deficiencies that would have cost $200 to $800 to fix proactively routinely generate $1,500 to $5,000 in post-inspection credit requests from buyers. Fix the gutters. It's the easiest math in real estate.

The NJ Seller's Gutter Checklist

Before your listing goes live, walk through this checklist:

  • Clean all gutters and flush downspouts — schedule a professional cleaning if you haven't had one in the past 12 months
  • Check slope — gutters should have a slight downward pitch toward each downspout (about 1/4 inch per 10 feet)
  • Probe fascia boards — look for soft spots along the entire roofline
  • Extend all downspouts — minimum 6 feet from the foundation, 10 feet preferred
  • Remove overflow staining — clean siding stains before listing photos
  • Check all gutter joints and end caps — reseal with gutter caulk if leaking
  • Confirm no standing water — check 24 hours after the next rain
  • Watch the basement after rain — monitor for moisture before listing

This checklist takes an afternoon and costs between $150 and $500 for a typical NJ colonial, depending on what you find. Compare that to the inspection negotiation you'll face if you skip it — and the answer is obvious.

What Good Gutters Do for Your NJ Home's Value

Here's the part most sellers don't think about: gutters aren't just about avoiding inspection problems. A home with clean, properly functioning gutters that discharge water well away from the foundation is a home with a dry basement, intact siding, and no foundation concerns. That's not just a checkbox — it's a story your inspector tells the buyer's agent on your behalf.

Functional gutters remove one of the most reliable reasons they find. That's worth $200 and an afternoon every single time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do NJ home inspectors check gutters?

Yes. NJ home inspectors routinely check gutters and downspouts as part of the exterior inspection. They look for sagging, improper slope, detachment from the fascia, overflow staining, downspout termination issues, and evidence of water intrusion at the foundation. Gutter deficiencies appear on the majority of inspection reports in NJ's older housing stock.

How much does gutter repair cost before selling in NJ?

Basic gutter cleaning runs $150 to $300. Reattaching sagging sections is $75 to $200. Replacing a full damaged section runs $200 to $500. Full gutter replacement on an NJ colonial is $1,500 to $3,500. These are all less than the post-inspection credit demands buyers typically make when gutters show up as a deficiency — which often run double or triple the actual repair cost.

Can clogged gutters cause basement water in NJ homes?

Yes — this is one of the most common cause-and-effect chains NJ inspectors document. Clogged or overflowing gutters deposit water at the foundation perimeter. Over time, that water migrates into the basement through cracks or the cove joint at the base of the wall. Before spending money on interior waterproofing, clean your gutters and extend the downspouts.

Should I replace gutters before selling my NJ home?

Not necessarily. If gutters are structurally sound, a cleaning plus reattachment of loose sections and proper downspout extension is usually sufficient. Replace only if gutters are heavily corroded or missing sections. A $2,500 replacement is worth it if it removes a buyer objection that was costing you $5,000 in credits.

How far should downspouts extend from the foundation in NJ?

NJ inspection standards call for at least 6 feet from the foundation. Eight to 10 feet is better in clay-heavy soils common in Union, Essex, and Morris counties. Downspouts that terminate at the foundation wall are routinely flagged on NJ inspection reports and should be extended before listing.
