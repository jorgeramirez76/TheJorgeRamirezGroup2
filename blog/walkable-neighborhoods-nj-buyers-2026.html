The Number That Should Catch NJ Sellers' Attention

59%

of Americans say they would choose a smaller yard if it meant living in a more walkable neighborhood with nearby amenities — July 2026

That number is not surprising to me. I've been watching it play out in the NJ market for three years. Buyers who tell me they're open to different towns consistently put walkability near the top of the list — often above lot size, sometimes even above square footage. Hybrid work schedules changed the math. When you're home three or four days a week instead of five, you want something to walk to.

What makes this stat worth paying attention to is that it's gotten stronger, not weaker, as hybrid work has settled into a permanent pattern. We are not going back to five days in the office. That means walkability in NJ suburbs is not a pandemic-era trend — it's a durable shift in what buyers are willing to pay for.

Here's what that means practically, whether you're buying or selling in New Jersey right now.

NJ Towns That Command a Real Walkability Premium

Not all NJ walkability is created equal. Plenty of towns claim to be walkable because they have a CVS and a pizza place. Real walkability means a downtown you'd actually choose to spend an afternoon in — restaurants, coffee, retail, bars, ideally a train station, and enough critical mass of activity that something is open on a Tuesday evening.

These are the towns in the counties I work — Union, Essex, Morris, Middlesex, Hudson — that have genuine, priced-in walkability premiums:

~85 Walk Score

Summit

The downtown core is one of the best in suburban NJ — a full block of restaurants, specialty retail, coffee shops, and bars within a five-minute walk of the train station. Homes within a half-mile of the Summit downtown command roughly $100K-$200K more than equivalent homes on the town's edges. The walkability premium is baked into Summit's pricing and has been growing as hybrid workers make up an increasing share of buyers.

~80 Walk Score

Westfield

Westfield's Elm Street downtown is a benchmark for what NJ suburban walkability can look like. Proper retail concentration, multiple restaurants and coffee shops, a thriving Saturday morning scene. The town has invested in the downtown for decades and it shows. The walkability premium here runs $75K-$150K depending on proximity to the downtown core versus the more residential outer blocks.

~75 Walk Score

Madison

Madison's Main Street is genuinely underrated — a full mix of restaurants, coffee, specialty shops, and a consistent energy even on weekday evenings. The Midtown Direct train station is right in the center. For buyers in the $900K-$1.1M range, Madison offers Chatham-adjacent schools with better walkability and a lower starting price point.

~72 Walk Score

Maplewood

Maplewood Village is the reason Brooklyn transplants keep choosing this town. The walkability is authentic — independent restaurants, a bookstore, coffee that isn't a chain, a wine bar. Combined with the 30-minute Midtown Direct ride, it's the clearest value proposition in the NJ commuter market for buyers who want urban habits without urban prices. The walkability premium in Maplewood is embedded in the entire town's pricing relative to its neighbors.

~70 Walk Score

South Orange

South Orange's village is directly at the train station, which is the gold standard of NJ walkability — you walk out of the train and into the downtown without a car. Prices trail Maplewood by a small margin, making it the most affordable Midtown Direct walkable town. Buyers who do their homework on South Orange often outbid more casual buyers by moving faster on the right property.

What NJ Buyers Actually Mean by "Walkable"

I ask buyers this question directly: what does walkable mean to you? The answer is almost never a Walk Score number. It's a list that usually includes:

  • A coffee shop within a 10-minute walk where they'd actually want to go on a Saturday morning
  • At least three restaurants they'd genuinely choose for dinner — not just the Italian-Chinese-pizza triumvirate that every NJ town has
  • A train station they can walk to (not drive to)
  • A grocery store within a 15-minute walk, or at least a small market
  • Some feeling that people are actually out and about on weekday evenings and weekends

The towns that consistently deliver all five of those things at the $700K-$1.5M price point are Summit, Westfield, Madison, Maplewood, South Orange, Montclair, and Cranford. Morristown is also strong. Jersey City and Hoboken are at the top of the list but at a different price point and lifestyle profile.

Towns that buyers expect to be walkable but aren't fully there yet: Scotch Plains, Parsippany, Livingston. Good towns with solid schools and low crime — but buyers who prioritize daily walkability end up disappointed when they see the actual downtown.

If You're Selling in a Walkable NJ Town, Use It

Your Walkability Score Is a Marketing Asset — Treat It That Way

Most NJ listing descriptions mention the train. They don't say that you can walk there. They don't describe what you can do in the 15-minute radius on foot. They don't list the specific restaurants within walking distance that buyers will want to know about.

A listing in Summit, Westfield, Madison, or Maplewood that reads "walk to downtown's award-winning restaurants, coffee shops, boutique retail, and the NJ Transit station — 0.3 miles" is marketing to the 59% of buyers who made their preference explicit. A listing that says "great location, close to shopping" is not.

Tell buyers exactly what they can walk to. Name the restaurants. Estimate the walk time to the train. Mention the specific errand infrastructure — dry cleaner, pharmacy, market — within walking distance. That specificity communicates that the walkability is real, not aspirational.

The Other Side: What Buyers Give Up for Walkability in NJ

I want to be straight with buyers who are drawn to walkable NJ towns: there is a real trade-off, and it's usually lot size and square footage per dollar.

The premium for walkability in Summit, Westfield, and Maplewood is real — $100K to $250K compared to an equivalent home in a less walkable town in the same county. That premium buys you a lifestyle, not a bigger yard. Homes near walkable NJ downtowns tend to be on quarter-acre to half-acre lots. The half-acre to one-acre lots are in the outer sections of town, further from the downtown, and they come with reduced walkability.

For buyers who are truly hybrid — home three or four days a week, kids in after-school activities, morning coffee a priority — the premium is worth it. For buyers who travel constantly for work, spend weekends at a second home, and are primarily buying for the school district, a less walkable town often delivers more house for the money.

The 59% who prefer walkability are not wrong. They're describing a genuine preference that has a real dollar value in NJ's market. Understanding what you're trading for that preference is what makes the decision a good one instead of a regrettable one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which NJ towns have the best walkability scores?

Among the NJ suburbs I work in, Hoboken and Jersey City have the highest Walk Scores — both in the 90s, essentially car-free. In the traditional commuter suburbs, Summit (downtown area Walk Score ~85), Westfield (~80), Maplewood (~72), South Orange (~70), Madison (~75), and Cranford (~68) are the strongest performers. These are also the towns where the walkability premium is most clearly priced into the market.

Does walkability actually increase property values in NJ?

Yes, measurably. Homes within a half-mile of a walkable downtown in NJ commuter towns consistently sell at a premium compared to equivalent homes on the edges of the same town. In Summit, homes near the downtown core command roughly $100K-$200K more than similar homes in less walkable sections. In Westfield and Madison, the downtown walkability premium runs $75K-$150K. The premium has grown as hybrid work patterns have made daily walkability more valuable.

What do NJ buyers mean when they say they want a walkable town?

Most NJ buyers using the word "walkable" mean they want to walk to at least three of these: a coffee shop, a restaurant, a grocery store, a train station, a dry cleaner, a pharmacy, and a bar. They are not asking for a Walk Score of 90. They are asking for a downtown within a 10-minute walk that has life in it — businesses open on weekday evenings and Saturday afternoons.

Should I buy in a walkable NJ town or a larger-lot suburban town?

It depends on how you actually spend your time. If you work remotely 3 or more days a week, walkability has direct daily value — you use it. If you're a 5-day commuter who is rarely home during the week, a larger lot and more space may deliver more value per dollar. Buyers who choose walkable NJ towns tend to be hybrid workers, couples without young children, and families where at least one person works locally or from home.

How does Walk Score affect NJ home sales?

Walk Score appears on most major listing platforms — Zillow, Realtor.com, Redfin — and buyers in the $700K-$1.5M NJ range are aware of it. A Walk Score above 70 in a listing description is a searchable filter for many buyers. More importantly, a low Walk Score — below 50 — is a red flag that prompts buyers to ask what they'd actually be able to do on foot, which in many NJ towns is the correct question to ask.
