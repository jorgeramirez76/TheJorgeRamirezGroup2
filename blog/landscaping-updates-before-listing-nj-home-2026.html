Landscaping Updates Worth Doing Before an NJ Home Hits the Market

Buyers form an opinion from the street before they reach the door. Most of what shapes that opinion costs a weekend and a few bags of mulch, not a landscape architect.

Start from the curb, not the catalog

Before you buy a single plant, stand across the street and look at your house the way a buyer scrolling listings does. Then walk the path a buyer walks: driveway, walkway, steps, door. Whatever you notice in that ninety seconds is the list. In my experience showing homes in Westfield, Summit and Morristown, buyers rarely comment on a specimen tree, but they always notice a bare lawn, an overgrown shrub across a window or a heaved walkway.

September is a good month to be doing this in New Jersey. Cool-season grasses recover well in early fall, and the Rutgers New Jersey Agricultural Experiment Station is the reference I trust for lawn and planting timing rather than a garden-center display.

Eight updates that earn their place on the list

  • Repair the lawn. Mow, edge along the walk and driveway, and overseed thin or bare patches now so they fill in before photographs. Skip the heavy fertilizer sales pitch; seed, water and patience do the work.
  • Define the beds and refresh the mulch. A clean edge and a thin, even layer of mulch make old plantings look cared for. Keep mulch a few inches off siding and away from the foundation so you are not inviting moisture or insects.
  • Prune what blocks windows, walkways and the house number. Light shaping only. Hard-pruning spring bloomers now costs you the flowers and can leave a shrub looking butchered in the listing photos.
  • Remove dead and dying plants. A brown evergreen or a half-dead hydrangea reads as neglect. Pull it, fill the gap with mulch or a modest replacement, and move on.
  • Fix the walkway, steps and railings. A lifted paver or a loose railing is a trip hazard first and an inspection note second. Reset what you can and level what you cannot.
  • Check house numbers, the mailbox and exterior lights. Fall showings happen after work, in the dark. A buyer who cannot find the house or see the front step arrives frustrated.
  • Add seasonal color at the entry, in moderation. Two planters or a few mums by the door are plenty. Twelve pots of mums look like a sale display.
  • Give the backyard a purpose. Rake, define the patio or seating area, and store the extras. The outdoor furniture guide covers what to clean, store or leave for photos.

What to skip, and what not to hide

Do not install a bed of new shrubs the week before listing. They look like what they are, and a buyer may wonder what was there before. Do not lay sod over a low, wet corner; it will be soggy again by the inspection and you will have spent money to cover a drainage question instead of answering it. Larger hardscape, irrigation and retaining walls belong in a plan for the next owner's enjoyment, not in a rush before photos.

Grading, standing water and a wet basement wall are conditions to fix or disclose, not to mulch over. Sort them with the maintenance triage plan and discuss disclosure with your agent and attorney.

Town-by-town notes

Mature-tree streets in Scotch Plains, Chatham and Millburn mean leaves will keep coming through the listing period; plan for a rake or a blower before every weekend of showings, and use the fall curb appeal checklist for the appointment-day reset. In Hoboken and Jersey City, the front is a stoop and a tree pit: sweep it, clear the planter of dead annuals and make sure the entry light works. Older colonials in Cranford and Union often have foundation plantings that have outgrown the house; a modest cutback and a clean bed edge does more than replacing them.

None of this is a promise about price. It is about removing the reasons a buyer talks themselves out of the showing before they see the kitchen. If you want a second set of eyes on which items matter for your street, that is a short conversation, and it starts with a home valuation discussion.

Questions about pre-listing landscaping

Is September a good time to seed a lawn in New Jersey?

Early fall is generally a favorable window for cool-season grasses in New Jersey. Check the Rutgers New Jersey Agricultural Experiment Station lawn guidance for your grass type and seed bare patches early enough to fill in before listing photographs.

Should I hire a landscaper before listing?

For the eight items in this list, most sellers can do the work themselves or with a maintenance crew. Hire a professional for tree work, drainage or hardscape repair, and get written estimates so the cost can be weighed against the plan for the sale.

How much mulch should I add?

A thin, even layer over the existing bed is enough for presentation. Keep it a few inches away from siding and the foundation, and do not pile it against tree trunks. The goal is a clean edge and a tidy bed, not depth.

Does new landscaping increase my home's value?

This article does not claim a price effect. Basic exterior maintenance removes distractions and questions during showings and inspections; large landscaping projects should be evaluated as a household decision, not as a pre-sale investment.

Can I hide a drainage problem with new plantings or sod?

No. Standing water, poor grading and a wet foundation wall are conditions to correct or disclose. Covering them delays the question until the inspection and can create a disclosure problem. Discuss known conditions with your agent and attorney.
