What Stays With the House When You Sell in New Jersey

Last updated  ·  August 2026

Quick answer: In New Jersey, anything permanently attached to the home is generally treated as part of the house and stays, while freestanding items go with the seller. Appliances like refrigerators, washers, and dryers sit in a gray area, so the sales contract's list of included and excluded items is what actually controls.

By Jorge Ramirez | August 2026

Every so often a deal in Union or Essex County gets tense over something small. Not the price. Not the inspection report. A chest freezer in the basement. The washer and dryer. The pendant lights over the kitchen island that the seller bought for themselves and never once imagined leaving behind.

It sounds minor until it is happening four days before closing, when both sides are tired and neither one wants to feel taken advantage of. Almost all of it is preventable with a single conversation before the house is ever listed.

The general rule: if it is attached, it usually stays

New Jersey, like most states, draws a line between real property and personal property. Real property is the land and everything permanently attached to it. Personal property is what you could pick up and carry out the door.

So the furnace stays. The built-in dishwasher stays. Ceiling fixtures, the garage door opener, the wall-mounted bracket the television hangs from, the shrubs along the front walk — all generally treated as part of the house. Your furniture, your rugs, your patio set, your grill on wheels all leave with you.

That rule gets you most of the way there. The trouble lives in the exceptions.

The gray areas that actually cause problems

These are the items that come up again and again in New Jersey transactions:

  • Refrigerators and freezers. A built-in or paneled refrigerator reads as part of the kitchen. A freestanding one plugged into an outlet does not, and a spare freezer in the basement or garage almost never does.
  • Washers and dryers. They are connected to plumbing and venting, which makes buyers assume they stay. Sellers moving to a home without laundry hookups often assume the opposite.
  • Light fixtures with sentimental value. The chandelier from a grandparent's house is the classic one. It is attached, so a buyer will reasonably expect it.
  • Window treatments. Custom blinds and shades cut to fit the windows are usually treated as staying. Curtains on a rod are more often personal.
  • Mounted televisions, shelving, and mirrors. The mount stays, the screen usually goes — but say so, because that is not obvious to everyone.
  • Outdoor items. Planted trees and shrubs stay. Potted plants, playsets that are not anchored, and fire pits are a conversation.
  • Basement and garage extras. Workbenches, utility sinks, second refrigerators, shelving systems, and shop equipment sit right on the line depending on how they are installed.

The contract is what actually decides it

Here is the part worth understanding clearly: the attached-versus-freestanding rule is a default, not a verdict. The standard sales contract used across New Jersey includes a section that lists items included in the sale and items specifically excluded. Whatever is written there overrides any assumption either side was carrying.

New Jersey also gives both sides an attorney review period after signing — typically a short window measured in business days — where the attorneys can modify or cancel the contract. That window is the practical deadline for getting inclusions and exclusions right. After it closes, changing the list means asking the other side for a favor rather than clarifying a term.

This is why a good listing conversation starts with an unglamorous question: what in this house are you planning to take with you? Not because anything is wrong with taking it. Because a buyer who learns about it at the final walkthrough experiences it as a loss, and a buyer who reads it in the contract on day one simply files it as a fact.

What New Jersey requires you to leave behind

A few items are not negotiable at all. Before the sale of most one- and two-family homes in New Jersey, the seller has to obtain a certificate from the local fire official confirming compliance on smoke alarms, carbon monoxide alarms, and a portable fire extinguisher. That extinguisher is not decoration and it is not yours to pack. It needs to be present, properly mounted, and of the required type when the inspector visits and when the buyer takes possession.

Many municipalities layer on their own requirements too. Certificate of occupancy or continued occupancy inspections vary meaningfully from town to town across Union, Essex, Morris, Middlesex, Hudson, and Somerset counties — some are a formality, some are a genuine punch list. Knowing which kind your town runs, early, is worth more than almost any other piece of preparation.

Leased equipment is its own category

Some things in the house may not belong to you at all, and those deserve their own attention:

  • Leased solar panels. A lease or power purchase agreement has to be disclosed and either transferred to the buyer or resolved before closing. Buyers and their lenders will have questions, and the answers take time to gather. Start this one early.
  • Propane tanks. Frequently owned by the supplier rather than the homeowner.
  • Water treatment and softener systems. Sometimes owned outright, sometimes on a service contract.
  • Alarm and monitoring equipment. The panel may be leased even when the wiring is not.

A short exercise before you list

Walk your own house the way a buyer would, room by room, and make two lists: what goes with you, and what stays. Then look at the first list honestly. Is there anything on it you are keeping out of habit rather than attachment? A second refrigerator you are only moving because it is technically yours, or a set of blinds that will not fit the next place, can be genuinely valuable to a buyer at effectively no cost to you.

And the reverse question matters just as much: if something on that list truly is not leaving, would you rather negotiate it now, when you have leverage and options, or in the last week, when you do not?

If you are the buyer

Do not rely on the listing photos or your memory of the showing. If a specific appliance or fixture matters to you, have it written into the contract by name before attorney review ends. Then use the final walkthrough for exactly what it is for: confirming that what the contract promised is still standing there. Bring the inclusions list with you.

The part that usually goes unsaid

Nearly every one of these disputes is really about expectations rather than objects. Someone assumed. Nobody wrote it down. By the time it surfaces, real money and real moving trucks are involved, and a modest item is suddenly carrying the weight of the whole transaction.

So it is worth asking yourself early: what does your move actually look like? What are you taking, what are you replacing, and what are you honestly done with?

Frequently Asked Questions

Do appliances stay with the house when you sell in New Jersey?

It depends on the contract. Built-in appliances like dishwashers, wall ovens, and cooktops are generally treated as part of the house and stay. Freestanding appliances such as refrigerators, washers, dryers, and basement freezers are commonly negotiated, and whatever the sales contract lists as included or excluded is what controls.

Are the washer and dryer included in a New Jersey home sale?

Not automatically. Because they connect to plumbing and venting, buyers often assume they stay, while sellers moving to a home without hookups often assume they go. Name them explicitly in the contract as included or excluded so nobody is guessing at the walkthrough.

What happens if a seller removes something that was supposed to stay?

It usually gets resolved before closing, often by replacing the item or crediting the buyer at settlement, and the attorneys handle it. It can also delay a closing, which is why the inclusions list matters more than most sellers expect.

Do I have to leave a fire extinguisher when I sell my New Jersey house?

Yes. For most one- and two-family homes, New Jersey sellers must obtain a certificate from the local fire official covering smoke alarms, carbon monoxide alarms, and a compliant portable fire extinguisher. The extinguisher needs to be present and properly mounted for the inspection and for the buyer.

Can I keep a chandelier or light fixture I am attached to?

You can, but handle it before you list. The cleanest approach is to swap it for a comparable replacement fixture before photos are taken, so buyers never see the one you are keeping. Otherwise it must be written into the contract as an exclusion.

What happens to leased solar panels when I sell in NJ?

The lease or power purchase agreement has to be disclosed and then either transferred to the buyer or resolved before closing. The provider's transfer process and the buyer's lender both take time, so this is one to start on well before you have an accepted offer.
