Selling a House as Executor in New Jersey

Start with who owns the property and what authority the fiduciary actually has. Those questions come before listing strategy, tax-waiver paperwork or a closing date.

Published August 6, 2026 · Legally sensitive sources checked August 27, 2026 · By Jorge Ramirez, NJ license #1754604

Direct answer: New Jersey law gives a fiduciary a power to sell estate or trust property only within stated limits. The recorded title, governing instrument, appointment, any court order and whether the property was specifically disposed of all matter. Have the transaction-specific authority confirmed before anyone signs for the estate.

Separate ownership from fiduciary authority

The word executor does not answer who owns the real estate or whether a proposed sale fits the estate documents. Review these five records together:

Recorded title

The current deed shows whether the decedent owned the property individually, with another owner, through a bona fide trust, or through an entity. The estate cannot assume it is the record owner.

Governing instrument

The will, trust or other governing instrument may grant, limit or redirect authority. A specific disposition of the property can change the analysis.

Appointment evidence

The Surrogate or court record identifies the appointed fiduciary and the type of letters issued. A current short certificate may be used as evidence of that appointment when requested.

Court orders and disputes

A court order, contested probate matter, co-fiduciary issue or other restriction can affect who may act and on what terms.

N.J.S. 3B:14-23 states that, in the absence of contrary or limiting provisions in the appointment, governing instrument or later court order, a fiduciary acting in good faith and reasonable discretion has powers that include selling estate or trust property, except property specifically disposed of. That statute is a starting point, not a property-specific legal opinion.

Use the correct probate record

The New Jersey Court Rule 4:80 application page explains that applications for probate and several forms of letters are filed with the Surrogate's Court unless the matter proceeds by complaint in Superior Court. The correct filing, venue and appointment depend on the decedent and the estate.

Use the NJ Courts directory of County Surrogates to identify the correct office and ask that office for its current filing instructions. Do not plan a listing or closing around a generic online timeline.

Form 0-1 is not a universal answer

The NJ Division of Taxation waiver page explains when a tax waiver releases the State's lien and lists explicit non-waiver situations. For example, it says no waiver is needed for real property held as tenancy by the entirety when the spouse or civil-union partner survives, or for an asset held in a bona fide trust. It also lists property owned by an LLC or closely held corporation among the non-waiver situations, while noting that a waiver may be relevant to shares of a New Jersey corporation.

If New Jersey real property remained in the decedent's name and no exception applies, a waiver may be part of the transfer path. The Division alone issues Form 0-1. L-9 is a request for a waiver, not the waiver. The current Form L-9 instructions describe who may use it, its required attachments and conditions that make it unavailable.

L-8 is for qualifying non-real-estate assets. It does not release real property. The Division's filing-requirements page separates resident and nonresident returns, L-8, L-9 and L-9 NR. Use the official flow that matches the decedent, beneficiaries and property ownership.

Property disclosure for an estate seller

The current Seller's Property Condition Disclosure Statement, effective April 20, 2026, includes a separate signature area for an executor, administrator or trustee who never occupied the property and lacks the personal knowledge needed to complete the statement. It also says information is provided to the best of the seller's knowledge, is not a warranty and is not a substitute for an inspection.

Do not turn limited knowledge into guessed answers or a blanket exemption. Give the current form, known records and property facts to the brokerage firm and the professional advising the estate so the transaction uses the right completion and signature path.

A property-planning packet

Once legal authority and the transfer path are confirmed, a real estate planning packet can stay factual:

  • the current deed and any available survey;
  • the will, trust or order relevant to the property, shared only with the appropriate professionals;
  • appointment evidence requested for the transaction;
  • the applicable Taxation filing or waiver correspondence;
  • the current property-condition form and supporting records;
  • mortgage, municipal, insurance and repair documents actually available for the house.

Questions to resolve before marketing

Does the estate own the property?

Confirm the recorded title instead of inferring ownership from family history or the will.

What authorizes this fiduciary to act?

Read the appointment, governing instrument and any court order together with the statutory limits.

Which Taxation path applies?

Check the Division's current waiver exceptions and filing requirements. Do not use L-8 for real estate or treat an L-9 application as the issued waiver.

What property facts are actually known?

Use records and the current disclosure form. Do not convert an unknown answer into a factual representation.

Scope: This is general information, not legal or tax advice. Recorded ownership, probate authority, beneficiary rights, tax-waiver requirements and contract obligations depend on the specific estate and transaction. If you choose to consult an attorney or tax professional, give that professional the deed, governing documents and current agency forms.
