Selling a House as Executor in New Jersey

Last updated  ·  August 2026

If you have been named executor and the house has to be sold, this walks through what your authority actually covers, who signs what, and the two documents that hold up most estate closings in New Jersey.

By Jorge Ramirez | Keller Williams Premier Properties | Updated August 2026 · NJ licensed agent since 2017, License #1754604

Can an executor sell a house in New Jersey?

As executor you can sell a New Jersey house once the county Surrogate has appointed you and issued your letters. That appointment — not the will alone — is what lets you sign the listing, the contract, and the deed. Before closing, the estate also needs a Form 0-1 tax waiver from the New Jersey Division of Taxation.

Almost everything else that feels confusing right now comes down to those two things: proving you have authority, and clearing the state's tax lien so the deed can transfer. The rest of this page explains both in plain terms.

None of this is legal or tax advice, and it is not a substitute for an estate attorney. What follows is the process as it actually runs at a New Jersey closing table, with sources cited so you can check anything yourself.

What gives an executor authority to sell?

Being named in a will does not make you executor. The county Surrogate makes you executor. New Jersey handles this at the county level: every one of the state's 21 counties has its own Surrogate, and you file in the county where the person lived at the time of death. That venue rule is in the court rules governing applications to the Surrogate's Court — the application is filed with “the Surrogate's Court of the county in which the decedent was domiciled at death,” and you verify the statements in it under oath (NJ Courts, Rule 4:80).

The New Jersey Judiciary describes the Surrogate's Court as a county office headed by the County Surrogate, an elected official who acts as the judge for uncontested probate and estate matters. If nobody is fighting about the will, your entire case stays with that county office and never sees a Superior Court judge. You can find your county's office in the Judiciary's statewide roster of county surrogates.

What you bring to the Surrogate

The document list is short and specific. The Union County Surrogate, for example, asks the executor to bring the original will, a certified copy of the death certificate, the full names and latest addresses of the closest surviving next of kin, and sufficient funds for the probate fees (Union County Surrogate). Other counties ask for essentially the same package. Fees are set county by county, so call your Surrogate's office and ask what the total will be before you drive over — it saves a second trip.

Note the word original. A photocopy of the will does not get probated in the ordinary way. If the original cannot be found, that becomes a legal question and you need an attorney before you do anything else with the house.

There is also a waiting period built into New Jersey law before a will can be admitted to probate. It is short, and it exists so anyone with a reason to object has a window to do so. Ask your county Surrogate for the earliest date they can accept the will — they will tell you exactly, and it is the kind of detail worth hearing from the office that will actually process the file rather than from a website.

Letters Testamentary or Letters of Administration — which one do you need?

Which document you get depends on one thing: whether there is a valid will.

There is a will

The Surrogate probates the will and issues Letters Testamentary to the executor named in it. The New Jersey Judiciary puts it simply: Letters Testamentary are issued when a will is probated by the county Surrogate's Court. Probate itself, in the Union County Surrogate's own words, is “the process by which a purported will is adjudicated to be valid.”

There is no will

There is no executor to appoint, so the Surrogate appoints an administrator and issues Letters of Administration. Who gets appointed follows an order of priority among the next of kin, and the people ahead of the applicant — or at the same level — are generally asked to renounce in writing. An administrator is also commonly required to post a surety bond, which an executor under a will often is not. That bond is a real cost and a real delay, and it is the main practical difference between the two paths.

Once you have letters, you also want short certificates. A short certificate is the one-page certified document the Surrogate issues that proves, as of its date, that your appointment is in effect. It is what the title company, the buyer's attorney, the bank, and the utility companies will each want a copy of. Ask for several while you are at the counter. Some institutions will not accept one older than a few months, so if your sale drags on you may need a fresh one at closing. Your Surrogate will quote the per-copy cost.

What will the title company ask you for?

This is where most executors get surprised, because the buyer's title company is not evaluating you — it is deciding whether it will insure the buyer's ownership afterward. It asks for the same four things nearly every time:

  • Your short certificate. Proof that you, specifically, can sign for the estate right now.
  • A certified death certificate. Get more certified copies than you think you need; nearly every institution keeps one.
  • The existing deed. How the person held title decides whether the estate even owns the property. If the deed says joint tenants with right of survivorship, or tenancy by the entirety with a surviving spouse, the house passed outside the estate and you are not the one selling it.
  • The New Jersey tax waiver, Form 0-1. This is the one that stops closings.

Why real estate needs a Form 0-1 waiver

New Jersey places a lien on a decedent's property. The Division of Taxation states that the Transfer Inheritance Tax “is a lien on all property owned by the decedent as of the date of their death for a period of 15 years” unless it is paid or secured by bond (NJ Division of Taxation). The waiver is the state's written consent to release a specific asset from that lien.

The Division's own executor guide is blunt about real estate: “Real Estate transfers always require Form 0-1.” And Form 0-1 “can only be issued by the Division of Taxation” — you cannot download it, fill it in, or hand-wave it at the closing table (NJ Tax Guide: A Guide to Being an Executor).

The Form L-8 that the bank accepted will not work here. L-8 is the self-executing waiver you hand directly to a bank or transfer agent for financial accounts. It does not cover real estate. Executors who cleared the bank accounts easily are often blindsided when the house turns out to need a different form entirely.

The path to the 0-1 depends on who inherits. If every beneficiary is a Class A beneficiary — spouse or civil union partner, child, grandchild, great-grandchild, parent, grandparent — and no New Jersey inheritance or estate tax is due and no return is required, the estate can file Form L-9, the Affidavit for Real Property Tax Waiver, to obtain the 0-1 for the real estate. Form L-9 states that condition on its face: it “may be used only if all beneficiaries are Class A, there is no New Jersey Inheritance or Estate Tax, and there is no requirement to file a tax return.”

If there are Class C beneficiaries (siblings, children-in-law) or Class D beneficiaries (nieces, nephews, aunts, uncles, friends, unrelated people), the Division's guide says a full return is required first. That is a longer road, and it is the single best reason to start the tax side early rather than after you have a signed contract. The Division tells executors to “plan for processing to take at least several months” once a return is filed. Their Inheritance and Estate Tax hotline is 609-292-5033.

Form L-9 also has to arrive complete. It asks for the property by county, street address, lot, block and municipality, and it must come with copies of the will and any trust agreements, the deed, your letters, and the death certificate. The form warns that it “will be returned if it is not fully and properly completed.” A returned form costs you weeks.

What does the executor actually sign?

You sign in your representative capacity, not personally. On the listing agreement, the contract, and the deed, your signature block names you as executor or administrator of the estate rather than as an individual owner. Your attorney will format this; it matters because it is what keeps the estate, and not you, on the hook for the obligations in the contract.

A few practical points that come up at almost every estate closing:

  • Disclosure still applies. An executor who never lived in the house genuinely does not know much about it, and that is a legitimate answer — but known material defects still have to be disclosed. Our guide to NJ seller disclosure requirements covers how this works when the seller has limited personal knowledge.
  • Insurance changes when the house empties out. Standard homeowner policies treat a vacant house differently from an occupied one. Call the carrier and tell them the situation before you assume the coverage carried over.
  • The residency certification. New Jersey closings include a seller's residency certification form, and an estate sale has its own considerations there. See the NJ “exit tax” explained for what that form is and is not.
  • Notice after probate. The court rules require the personal representative to mail notice of probate to beneficiaries and other interested persons within 60 days of the probate date, with proof of mailing filed with the Surrogate. Your attorney usually handles this, but it is your obligation.

What an executor should not do

Two mistakes cause most of the trouble I see.

Signing anything before you have letters. Cash buyers find estate properties fast — sometimes before the family has even met with the Surrogate. A contract signed by someone who does not yet have authority is a problem you will have to unwind later. Wait for the appointment.

Deciding alone when the will says otherwise. An executor's job is to carry out the will and treat the beneficiaries fairly. If several people inherit, keep them informed in writing about price, offers, and timing even where you technically have authority to act. Most estate disputes I have watched started as a communication problem, not a money problem. The mechanics of that situation are laid out in selling an inherited house with multiple heirs in NJ.

Getting the house ready without overspending

Estates rarely have cash sitting around for renovations, and the beneficiaries are often in different places about what should happen to the house. The honest answer is that most inherited homes do not need much: a clean-out, a deep clean, and accurate pricing usually do more for the number than a kitchen would.

What the property is worth today is the fact everything else hangs on — whether you sell as-is, whether it makes sense to do any work, and what each beneficiary is actually receiving. Start there. Frequently asked questions

Can an executor sell a house in NJ without all the beneficiaries agreeing?

Usually yes, if the estate still owns the house and the will gives the executor authority to sell. The executor signs for the estate. But the executor owes the beneficiaries a duty to act in their interest, and a sale that looks self-dealing or below market can be challenged. If title has already been deeded out to the heirs, the estate no longer owns it and every person on the deed has to sign. An estate attorney can tell you which situation you are in by reading the will and the deed.

Do I need Letters Testamentary before I can list the house?

You need them before you can sign a binding contract or a deed on behalf of the estate, and a title company will ask for a current short certificate at closing. Some executors begin the preparation work — cleanout, pricing, photos — while the Surrogate paperwork is processing, then list once the letters are in hand. Talk to your attorney about the sequence that fits your estate rather than assuming either extreme.

Why does the title company want a tax waiver if no inheritance tax is owed?

Because New Jersey's inheritance tax attaches as a lien to the decedent's property from the date of death, and the waiver is the state's written release of that lien for a particular asset. The Division of Taxation's executor guide states plainly that real estate transfers always require Form 0-1, and Form 0-1 is issued only by the Division. Even when the tax comes to zero — a Class A estate, for instance — the estate still has to obtain the waiver, typically by filing Form L-9.

How long does it take to get appointed by the Surrogate?

That varies by county, by whether there is a will, and by whether a bond is required. Rather than trusting a general number, call your county Surrogate's office — the statewide roster is on the NJ Courts website — and ask what their current turnaround is and what the fees will be. They answer this question all day and the answer is free.

What if I am the executor but I live out of state?

Out-of-state executors sell New Jersey property regularly. The Surrogate filing and the closing can generally be handled with an attorney, remote notarization where permitted, and mailed documents, though the original will has to reach the Surrogate. The bigger practical issue is the property itself — who checks on it, who lets contractors in, who handles the cleanout. That is worth solving before you list.
