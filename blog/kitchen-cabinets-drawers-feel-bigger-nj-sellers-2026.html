How to Make Kitchen Cabinets and Drawers Feel Twice as Big Before NJ Buyers Open Them

Buyers don't measure your kitchen. They open a drawer. Five moves that professional organizers use every day will make an older NJ kitchen feel like it has twice the storage — for under $100 and one afternoon.

Buyers judge kitchen storage by opening a drawer

Here is something I've watched happen in kitchens from Cranford to Montclair to Metuchen: a buyer walks in, likes the light, likes the layout, and then opens the drawer next to the stove. If a spatula springs out at them, the conversation changes. Ten minutes later they're in the car telling their agent the kitchen is "too small."

The kitchen didn't get smaller. The drawer just told them a story about it. A lot of the housing stock across Union, Morris, Essex, Hudson, and Middlesex counties was built before anyone owned an air fryer and 40 reusable water bottles. Buyers judge that storage by what they see when they open the door, not by cubic feet, and that's something you can control.

Professional organizers use a handful of moves to make drawers and cabinets feel double their size. I've adapted them for sellers, because the goal isn't a perfect pantry. It's a buyer who closes the cabinet thinking "there's room."

Jorge's observation: Nobody has ever told me a kitchen was too small because of the square footage. They tell me it's too small because of what they saw when they opened something. Fix what they see, and the same kitchen sells as "plenty of storage."

Five moves that make cabinets and drawers feel twice as big

  1. Edit to 70 percent before you organize anything Organizing a packed cabinet just gives you a neatly packed cabinet. Pull everything out first. Anything you haven't used since spring, the third set of mixing bowls, the Thanksgiving platters, the bread maker: box it, label it, and move it to the garage or a storage unit. You're moving anyway, so this is packing, not decluttering. Then put back only enough to fill each cabinet about two-thirds. Visible shelf space is what buyers read as "extra room."
    Cost: A few moving boxes · Impact: The single biggest change buyers notice
  2. Add risers and stacking shelves so every cabinet works twice Most upper cabinets have a foot of dead air above the plates. A wire shelf riser turns one shelf into two: plates below, bowls above. Under the sink, a simple stacking shelf gets the cleaning supplies off the cabinet floor. Buyers open that cabinet in almost every showing because they're looking for leaks. Let them find a tidy, dry, organized space instead.
    Cost: $10–$25 per riser · Time: 5 minutes each
  3. Divide every drawer into zones An undivided drawer is a pile. Expandable bamboo dividers for utensils, a diagonal knife insert, and a shallow tray for the junk drawer turn piles into zones. When a buyer pulls one open, they see order instead of chaos. Aim to leave one empty zone per drawer. Empty space is the signal you're after.
    Cost: $15–$40 for a full set of dividers · Time: One afternoon for the whole kitchen
  4. Use the back of cabinet doors and the wall The inside of a cabinet door is free real estate. Slim adhesive racks hold pot lids, cutting boards, and foil boxes, which frees an entire shelf. A magnetic knife strip on the backsplash clears a drawer. For sellers, the rule is simple: keep it minimal and matching. One or two clean door racks look smart. Six of them look like you ran out of room.
    Cost: $10–$30 · Impact: Frees a full shelf or drawer
  5. Decant and match containers in the pantry and food cabinets Half-empty cereal boxes, torn bags of rice, and mismatched jars eat space and look messy. Moving dry goods into matching clear canisters (square ones stack tighter than round) roughly doubles what a pantry shelf holds and makes it look like a magazine photo. Group the rest by category in a few bins. You only need to do this for the shelves a buyer will see when they open the pantry door.
    Cost: $30–$60 for a starter set · Impact: Pantry reads as spacious and cared-for

Why this matters more in NJ than it would somewhere else

A lot of the buyers I work with are coming from Manhattan, Brooklyn, Hoboken, and Jersey City. They've spent years cooking in kitchens with three cabinets and a hot plate. A Westfield colonial or a Maplewood Tudor is already a big step up. What they're afraid of is buying a house that looks spacious and then finding out the kitchen is a squeeze.

When they open a drawer and see divided, breathable storage, that fear goes away. When they see a packed cabinet, the fear gets a number attached to it: the cost of a kitchen renovation. That number comes straight off the offer, or it turns into a buyer who moves on to the next listing in Chatham or Summit.

Frequently asked questions

Do NJ buyers really open kitchen cabinets and drawers during showings?

Yes, almost every one of them. Buyers open the cabinet under the sink, the drawer next to the stove, and the pantry. It is one of the few ways they can judge storage without a tape measure. A cabinet that is packed to the door tells them the kitchen is too small. A cabinet with visible shelf space tells them there is room to spare, even when the square footage is identical.

How full should kitchen cabinets be when a NJ home is listed?

Aim for about 70 percent full. Cabinets that are stuffed read as inadequate storage, and cabinets that are completely empty read as staged and unconvincing. Roughly two-thirds full, with items grouped and visible shelf space in front of them, is the point where buyers believe the kitchen has more room than they need.

What is the cheapest way to make kitchen drawers look bigger before listing?

Expandable drawer dividers and shelf risers. A set of bamboo dividers for the utensil and gadget drawers plus two or three wire risers for the dish and glass cabinets typically costs well under $100 and takes one afternoon. That combination does more for the way storage shows than any other change short of a renovation.

Should I move small appliances out of the kitchen before showings?

Move out anything you do not use weekly. The bread maker, the extra blender, the holiday platters, and the backup coffee maker should go to the garage, a storage unit, or a clearly labeled bin in the basement. Every item you remove frees a shelf, and every free shelf makes the cabinets feel larger to a buyer.

Does organizing the inside of cabinets actually affect the sale price of a NJ home?

It affects it indirectly but consistently. Buyers who believe a kitchen has enough storage do not mentally deduct the cost of a renovation from their offer. In older homes across Westfield, Cranford, Maplewood, and Montclair, where kitchens are often original or modestly updated, organized cabinets are frequently the difference between a buyer saying the kitchen works and saying it needs to be redone.
