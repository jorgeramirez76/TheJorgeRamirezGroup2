7 Colors Designers Use to Make a Room Look More Expensive, and How NJ Sellers Should Use Them Before Listing

House Outlook linked a Livingetc piece on the colors designers reach for when a room needs to look more expensive than it cost. The list is good: warm neutrals, cream, deep green, chocolate brown, charcoal, a rich ochre and a dusty slate blue. A listing is not a design magazine, though. The question for a seller in Summit or Maplewood is not which color is prettiest, but which one makes a buyer stand in the doorway a second longer. Here is my read, color by color.

Why these colors work

The designers in the article are not picking shades at random. Every color on the list has a warm undertone, some depth to it, and enough quiet that it does not fight the furniture. Rooms look expensive when the walls, the trim and the floor agree with each other, when the color continues from one room into the next, and when nothing is shouting. That is also, almost exactly, what a listing photographer wants. The difference is that a designer is decorating for one family and a seller is painting for a hundred strangers.

The listing rule

Here is how I sort the list for a client. The quiet colors go on the walls and trim, where they stay with the house. The saturated ones go into things that leave with you: a throw, two pillows, a runner, the inside of a bookcase. A buyer in Montclair or Glen Ridge expects some character and will forgive a bold dining room. A buyer touring new construction in Edison or Woodbridge expects clean and bright, and a chocolate brown family room costs you a showing. Know which buyer you are painting for.

The seven colors, and where each one goes

  1. Warm neutrals. Greige, mushroom, warm stone. The safest wall color on the list and the one I recommend most. It photographs as light without going cold, hides scuffs better than white, and flows through an open plan without a seam.
  2. Cream and off-white. For trim, ceilings and north-facing rooms that turn gray under a pure white. In the pre-war colonials of Westfield and Cranford, cream trim against a warm neutral wall looks original. See the white paint guide for choosing the undertone.
  3. Deep green. One wall, a powder room, a built-in or a front door. On every wall of a small room it swallows the light; on one wall behind a bed or a sofa it is the photo buyers remember.
  4. Chocolate brown. Beautiful on a leather chair, a wood table or a velvet pillow. On the walls of a listing it reads dark in photos and dated to half the buyers walking through. Keep it in the furniture.
  5. Charcoal. A kitchen island, a fireplace surround, interior doors or the exterior trim. As a wall color it works only in a room with big windows and a reason, like a den or a dining room in a Morristown Victorian.
  6. Ochre and mustard. Accessories only when selling. A throw, a piece of art, a bowl on the island. It is the warm note that makes a neutral room look styled, and it goes in the moving box.
  7. Slate blue. A bedroom, a bathroom vanity, a study. The one saturated color on the list that most buyers accept on a full room, as long as the shade is dusty rather than bright.

What photographs well

Listing photos flatten color. Warm neutrals and creams gain depth on camera; dark browns and charcoals lose it and turn into a flat block. If you keep a deep color, give the photographer a lamp on in that room and shoot it in daylight. Ask your agent to look at the color through a phone camera before you commit, because that is how every buyer will see it first. The rule-of-three guide covers how to style those accents without clutter.

Jorge’s tip: Paint the sample on a large board, not the wall, and carry it to the darkest corner and the brightest window of the room at the time of day showings usually happen. A color that works at noon in a Hoboken loft can look muddy at 5 p.m. in a Scotch Plains split-level.

Before you paint

Repainting the main rooms is one of the few pre-listing jobs that reliably changes how a house shows, and one of the cheapest. Fix nail holes and water stains first, use the same sheen throughout, and write down every color and finish for the buyer; the paint record is a small courtesy that lands well at closing. If the house has colors from the list that hurts value, that is where the budget goes first.

For buyers: color is the cheapest thing in the house

Do not walk away from a solid house in Chatham because the living room is brown. A gallon of warm neutral paint fixes it in a weekend. Walk away from the things paint cannot fix: the roof, the water in the basement, the furnace. The seller who painted everything a rich designer color may have done you a favor by scaring off the competition.

Questions about paint colors and selling a NJ home

What is the best wall color for selling a house in New Jersey?

A warm neutral such as greige or a soft warm stone, with cream or off-white trim. It photographs as bright without looking cold, works with most furniture, and lets buyers picture their own things in the room.

Can I keep a dark accent wall when I list my home?

One deep green, slate blue or charcoal wall in a room with good light usually helps a listing photo. Dark color on every wall of a small or north-facing room usually hurts it. If in doubt, photograph the room with a phone and judge from the picture.

Should I repaint the whole house before selling?

Repaint the rooms buyers see first and the rooms with scuffs, bold colors or water stains: the entry, living room, kitchen and primary bedroom. A closed-off guest room in decent shape can wait.

Do I need to tell the buyer what paint colors I used?

It is not required, but leaving a written list of colors, finishes and where each was used is a courtesy buyers appreciate, and it helps with touch-ups before the final walkthrough.

Does the paint color change what a buyer will pay?

Not directly. Color changes how many buyers come through and how they feel in the first minute, which affects offers. A house that photographs well draws more showings, and more showings are what move the price.
