The NJ “Exit Tax” Explained — And Why It Is Not Actually a Tax

Updated July 2026 · By Jorge Ramirez, Keller Williams Premier Properties · NJ licensed agent since 2017, License #1754604

New Jersey does not have an exit tax. There is no fee for leaving the state and no extra tax for selling on your way out. What people call the “exit tax” is an estimated income tax payment that non-resident sellers make at closing — a prepayment of tax on the gain the state was already owed. Most sellers who hear the term and panic are exempt from it entirely — a departing homeowner selling their own principal residence usually has nothing withheld at all.

Where the Nickname Came From

The phrase spread because of how the money moves. When a seller who lives outside New Jersey closes on a New Jersey property, a payment is withheld from the sale proceeds at closing and sent to the state with the deed. From the seller’s chair it looks exactly like a toll for leaving: you sell, you move away, the state takes a cut on the way out.

But the underlying obligation has nothing to do with moving. New Jersey taxes income earned from New Jersey sources, and the gain on a New Jersey property is New Jersey-source income whether you live in Summit or Scottsdale. For residents, the state collects that through the normal return. For someone who is about to be outside its reach, it collects at closing instead. Same tax, earlier collection point.

That distinction matters practically, not just semantically. If you think it is a penalty for leaving, you will treat it as money gone. If you understand it is a prepayment, you will plan for the reconciliation — and possibly the refund.

Who Actually Pays It — and Who Is Exempt

The trigger is residency, but residency is only the first question. New Jersey’s residency-certification form (GIT/REP-3) carries sixteen numbered boxes, fifteen of them exemptions, and the form states that boxes 2 through 16 apply to residents and non-residents alike. That single line is what most “exit tax” panic overlooks.

  • You are staying in New Jersey. You certify residency and nothing is withheld. Selling in Westfield and buying in Chatham does not expose you to this at all.
  • You are moving out of state and selling your principal residence. This is the case people assume is the worst, and it is usually the opposite. Box 2 exempts a property used exclusively as a principal residence as defined in Internal Revenue Code section 121 — and because boxes 2 through 16 are available to non-residents too, a departing homeowner selling the home they actually lived in typically has nothing withheld at closing.
  • You are selling a property that was not your principal residence. A rental, a second home, or an inherited house you never lived in. This is where the withholding genuinely lands.

Other exemptions on the same form cover a 1031 exchange, a transfer from an executor to an heir, a transfer incident to divorce, a sale producing no net proceeds, and certain military circumstances. None of them are age-based — there is no senior exemption from this withholding, though the separate realty transfer fee does have one for qualifying sellers aged 62 and over.

How the Number Is Calculated

The withholding is the greater of two figures:

2%of the gross sale price
10.75%of the taxable gain
whichever is higheris what gets withheld

The word greater is where sellers get surprised. Consider a $600,000 sale. Two percent of the sale price is $12,000. If your actual taxable gain is modest — you bought recently, or you put significant money into the house — the tax you genuinely owe on that gain may be a small fraction of $12,000. You still have $12,000 withheld, because the 2% floor applies regardless.

That is not the state keeping $12,000. It is the state holding $12,000 until you file and prove what you actually owe.

Getting It Back

You file a New Jersey income tax return for the year of the sale, calculate the real tax on the real gain, and reconcile against what was withheld. If more was withheld than owed, the difference is refunded.

Two things determine how much comes back, and both are worth getting right before closing rather than after:

  • Your cost basis. Basis is not just the purchase price. Capital improvements — a new roof, a kitchen, an addition, a finished basement — increase it, and a higher basis means a smaller gain. Sellers who never kept receipts routinely overstate their gain and over-withhold.
  • Your selling costs. Commission, the realty transfer fee, and other costs of sale reduce the gain as well.

If you have owned a New Jersey house for twenty years and renovated it twice, the paperwork proving that is worth real money at tax time. Find it before you list.

Can You Avoid It?

“Avoid” is the wrong frame, because for most sellers there is nothing to avoid — they qualify for an exemption and simply need to claim it correctly on the residency-certification form at closing. The mistake that actually costs people money is not failing to dodge the withholding; it is not realising an exemption applied and letting it be withheld anyway.

What you cannot do is misrepresent your residency or the character of the property. If the house was genuinely an investment rather than your home, the withholding applies and there is no clever way around it. Treat any advice that depends on describing a rental as a principal residence the way you would treat any other suggestion to misstate a fact on a tax form.

The useful step is practical: know before closing which box applies to you, and make sure your attorney has the documentation to support it.

The Separate Question: Capital Gains

The withholding is a collection mechanism. Capital gains tax is the actual liability underneath it, and the two get conflated constantly.

On a principal residence you lived in for two of the previous five years, federal law excludes the first $250,000 of gain for a single filer and $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly. For a large share of New Jersey sellers, that exclusion covers the entire gain and the federal capital gains question ends there.

Above the exclusion, gain is subject to federal capital gains rates. New Jersey then taxes capital gains as ordinary income at state rates rather than at a separate preferential rate. And the exclusion is tied to a principal residence — it does not shelter gain on an investment property, which is its own analysis.

Questions Sellers Actually Ask

Is the NJ exit tax legal?

Yes, but not as a tax on leaving — because it is not one. New Jersey is collecting income tax on New Jersey-source income at the last practical moment before the seller is outside its jurisdiction. The mechanism is ordinary; the nickname is what makes it sound punitive. And most home sellers are exempt from the withholding altogether.

When did the NJ exit tax begin?

The non-resident withholding took effect for transfers on or after August 1, 2004. It is not a recent change, and it is not a new tax — it changed when the state collects income tax it was already owed, not whether it was owed.

Does the withholding apply if I sell and buy again in New Jersey?

No. If you remain a New Jersey resident, you certify residency at closing and no withholding applies. Moving from one New Jersey town to another does not trigger it.

Is there a senior exemption?

Not from this withholding — none of the sixteen numbered boxes, fifteen of them exemptions is age-based. Older sellers are usually covered by the principal-residence exemption instead. Do not confuse this with the separate NJ realty transfer fee, which does have a reduced rate for qualifying sellers aged 62 and over.

How do I estimate my actual net proceeds?

Start with the NJ seller net proceeds calculator, which accounts for commission, the realty transfer fee, attorney fees and payoff. The NJ selling costs guide breaks down each line, and the realty transfer fee calculator handles that tier separately.

What is the NJ exit tax?

There is no separate NJ exit tax. The nickname refers to an estimated gross income tax payment that some sellers make at closing: 2% of the sale price or 10.75% of the gain, whichever is higher. It is a prepayment of New Jersey income tax on the gain, not an additional tax — and most home sellers are exempt from it entirely.

How does the NJ exit tax work?

At closing, a non-resident seller's payment is withheld from sale proceeds and remitted to the state along with the deed recording. You then file a New Jersey income tax return for that year, calculate what you actually owe on the gain, and reconcile the difference. If the withholding exceeded your actual liability, the excess is refunded.

Do you get the NJ exit tax back?

Often a substantial part of it, yes. Because the withholding is calculated as the higher of 2% of the sale price or 10.75% of the gain, sellers with a small gain frequently have far more withheld than they owe. That difference comes back after you file your New Jersey return for the year of the sale.

How is the NJ exit tax calculated?

It is the greater of two figures: 2% of the gross sale price, or 10.75% of the taxable gain. On a $600,000 sale the 2% floor is $12,000, so a seller with a small gain pays the $12,000 floor even though the tax actually owed may be far less.

How do you avoid the NJ exit tax?

For most sellers there is nothing to avoid. New Jersey's residency-certification form (GIT/REP-3) carries sixteen numbered boxes — box 1 is a resident certification and boxes 2 through 16 are exemptions, which apply to residents and non-residents alike. Box 2 exempts a property used exclusively as a principal residence under Internal Revenue Code section 121, so a departing homeowner selling the home they lived in typically has nothing withheld. The costly mistake is not claiming an exemption you qualify for. What you cannot do is misstate your residency or the character of the property.

Is there an NJ exit tax exemption for seniors?

No — none of the sixteen numbered boxes, fifteen of them exemptions is age-based. Older sellers are usually covered by the principal-residence exemption instead. This should not be confused with the separate NJ realty transfer fee, which does have a reduced rate for qualifying sellers aged 62 and over.

Do you pay capital gains tax when you sell a house in NJ?

Only on gain above the federal exclusion. If the home was your principal residence for two of the previous five years, the first $250,000 of gain is excluded for a single filer and $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly. Gain above that is subject to federal capital gains rates, and New Jersey taxes capital gains as ordinary income at state rates.

Selling and Leaving New Jersey?

The withholding is usually the smallest part of the decision. Timing, pricing and how your gain is documented matter more — and those are worth talking through before you list, not after.
