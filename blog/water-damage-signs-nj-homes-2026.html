7 Water Damage Warning Signs NJ Buyers and Sellers Both Miss

Water is the number one inspection issue in New Jersey's older housing stock. These are the signs that don't make it onto buyers' radar — until the inspector's report does.

Updated July 9, 2026
By Jorge Ramirez · Licensed NJ Real Estate Agent · Keller Williams Premier Properties

Photo via Unsplash

In 15 years of selling homes across Union, Morris, Essex, and Middlesex counties, water has caused more deal problems than any other single issue. Not because it's catastrophic when found — most water problems are fixable — but because sellers didn't know they had it, buyers didn't catch it early, and then the inspector's report made it into a negotiation that nobody was prepared for.

New Jersey's housing stock leans old. Homes built in the 1920s through 1970s dominate in the towns where I work most — Cranford, Maplewood, Summit, Chatham, Montclair, Madison. These houses have block foundations, copper plumbing, cast iron drains, and decades of seasonal freeze-thaw cycles. Water finds a way. The question is whether you're going to find it before a buyer's inspector does.

Here are the seven signs that consistently get missed — by sellers who lived in the house for years and buyers who walked through on a dry Tuesday in June.

The 7 Warning Signs

1Efflorescence on Foundation or Basement Walls

That white or gray powdery deposit on your concrete block or poured concrete foundation isn't cosmetic. Efflorescence forms when water moves through masonry, picks up mineral salts, and deposits them on the surface as it evaporates. It's a reliable indicator that water has been migrating through your foundation wall. In NJ's clay-heavy soil — common across much of Union and Morris counties — the hydrostatic pressure that drives water through block walls is significant after heavy rain.

What It Costs When Buyers Find It

An inspector who documents efflorescence gives buyers grounds to request a waterproofing system. Interior systems run $4,000–$12,000. Sellers who address this before listing with a documented system and transferable warranty come out much better than sellers who try to paint over it and hope nobody notices. The painted powder is visible to any inspector with a flashlight.

2A Musty Smell That Comes and Goes

Homeowners who have lived in a house for years often stop smelling the baseline. That slightly earthy, musty smell in the basement or in lower-level closets is what I'm looking for the moment I walk into a home — before I've seen anything else. Buyers notice it too. And their instinct, correctly, is to associate it with mold or chronic moisture. The smell comes from microbial activity on wet surfaces — drywall, wood framing, carpet padding. It means moisture is present or has been recently.

What It Costs When Buyers Find It

Mold testing runs $300–$500. Remediation costs depend on scope but easily reach $3,000–$10,000 for moderate cases. If the musty smell is in your home and you've stopped noticing it, have someone else walk through with you — or ask me to take a walk before your listing photos are taken.

3Water Stains on Attic Sheathing

This is the one sellers almost never think to check. Most homeowners don't go into their attic unless they have a problem. But inspectors do — and brown or black staining on the roof sheathing (the plywood or boards directly under the shingles) is documented in every inspection report where it appears. It indicates either a roof leak or inadequate ventilation causing condensation. Both require investigation. In NJ's older homes with cedar shake or original asphalt shingles, this is extremely common.

What It Costs When Buyers Find It

Active roof leaks require repair ($500–$3,000 for localized repairs, $10,000–$20,000 for full replacement depending on age and material). Staining from past leaks that are now resolved still creates buyer anxiety and inspection credits. Pull down the attic access hatch and take a look before you list. A flashlight and five minutes can save you thousands in negotiations.

4Staining Around the Sump Pump Pit or Floor Drain

The presence of a sump pump in a NJ basement is normal — it's not automatically a red flag. But staining around the pit, tide marks on the walls above it, or rust staining on the concrete floor near the drain tells inspectors that the system has been tested. High-tide marks in particular indicate how high water has risen during significant rain events. That information matters to buyers.

What It Costs When Buyers Find It

A sump pump system with a battery backup and a documented history of proper function is a positive. Staining without a functioning system — or with a pump that hasn't been tested recently — is a liability. Test your pump. Replace it if it's over 7 years old. Battery backups are around $300 and eliminate the "what happens in a power outage" question that always comes up.

5Soft Spots in Bathroom or Laundry Room Floors

Step around the base of your toilet. Step around your tub surround. Walk the perimeter of your laundry room. If the floor has any give — any spongy softness underfoot — there's subfloor damage from moisture, typically from a slow toilet wax ring leak or an improperly sealed tub surround. This is one of the most common items I see flagged in NJ inspection reports, particularly in homes that have had the same bathroom fixtures for 20+ years.

What It Costs When Buyers Find It

Subfloor repairs run $500–$3,000 depending on scope. If the damage reaches the joists, the cost goes up significantly. Fixing a soft spot before listing typically costs less than the inspection credit buyers request when they find it — and it removes a documented defect that gives buyers leverage to renegotiate.

6Paint Bubbling or Peeling on Exterior Window Trim

Exterior paint failure on window trim is one of the most overlooked signs of water intrusion. When caulking around window frames fails — which it does on a 5–10 year cycle on most NJ homes — water infiltrates the trim board and the surrounding sheathing. The paint bubbles first, then peels, then the trim rots. By the time you have visibly soft trim, there's often structural damage to the sheathing underneath. This is especially common on older NJ homes with original cedar or pine trim.

What It Costs When Buyers Find It

Recaulking exterior windows runs $500–$1,500 for a typical NJ home and should happen every 5–7 years. Replacing rotted trim and sheathing runs $1,500–$5,000+ depending on scope. Inspectors photograph every instance of bubbling paint and rotted trim. The list of exterior deferred maintenance adds up fast in NJ's older housing stock.

7Grading That Slopes Toward the Foundation

Walk around the outside of your house and look at how the ground slopes relative to your foundation. It should slope away from the house in all directions — about 6 inches of drop over the first 10 feet. Many NJ homes have settled over decades to the point where sections of the grading now slope toward the foundation, directing rainfall directly against the basement wall. This is often the root cause of the efflorescence and basement moisture I described earlier, and it's fixable with topsoil and regrading.

What It Costs When Buyers Find It

Regrading around a foundation typically runs $500–$2,000. It's one of the highest-return fixes a NJ seller can make because it demonstrates an understanding of what causes water intrusion — and because it directly protects the buyer from the problem recurring. Inspectors photograph poor grading every time they see it.

The pattern I see repeatedly: Sellers who lived in a home for 15–20 years stopped noticing the signs that buyers see fresh. A pre-listing walk-through with someone who has seen hundreds of inspection reports — not just your own home — is worth the time. I catch things sellers have walked past for years.

What to Do Before You List

If you found yourself recognizing any of the signs above in your own home, the next move is straightforward. Don't guess at the severity. Get a licensed home inspector — not a contractor who will quote you a job — to walk the home and give you an objective assessment before your buyer's inspector does it under contract. A pre-listing inspection in NJ typically runs $400–$600 and gives you the full picture before you're negotiating under time pressure.

Document every repair you make with a receipt. In NJ real estate, documented repairs are worth significantly more at the negotiating table than undocumented claims that something was fixed. A waterproofing warranty that transfers to the buyer is worth money. A contractor receipt with a clear description of work is worth money. A verbal "we fixed that" is worth nothing.

Disclosure matters too. New Jersey requires disclosure of known water intrusion, flooding, and drainage issues on the Seller's Disclosure form. Sellers who disclose accurately, show their documentation, and price accordingly consistently close more smoothly than sellers who try to hide history. The inspector always finds it. Always.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most common water damage issue in NJ homes?

Basement water intrusion is the most common water issue in NJ homes, particularly in older housing stock built before 1980. It shows up as efflorescence on block foundation walls, staining around floor drains, or musty odors that persist even in dry weather. NJ's clay-heavy soil and significant rainfall mean virtually every older home has some history of water management issues — the question isn't whether it happened, it's whether it was properly addressed.

Does water damage hurt a home's value in NJ?

Undisclosed or unresolved water damage can cost NJ sellers $5,000–$30,000 in inspection credits or price reductions, depending on severity. Documented, properly remediated water damage with receipts and a professional warranty typically has minimal impact on sale price. The difference is transparency and documentation — sellers who disclose upfront with repair receipts consistently fare better than sellers who get caught hiding history at inspection.

What is efflorescence and should I be worried about it?

Efflorescence is the white powdery deposit on concrete or masonry surfaces. It forms when water moves through the block, picks up mineral salts, and deposits them on the surface as it evaporates. On foundation walls, it's a reliable indicator that water has been migrating through the wall. NJ inspectors document it every time they see it. Sellers who have it should address the source before listing — not just paint over the powder.

How much does waterproofing a NJ basement cost?

Interior waterproofing systems (French drains, sump pumps, vapor barriers) typically run $4,000–$12,000 in NJ depending on basement size and severity. Exterior waterproofing runs $10,000–$25,000+. Many NJ sellers opt for an interior system with a transferable warranty — it's faster, less invasive, and the transferable warranty directly addresses buyer concerns at inspection.

Should I disclose past water damage when selling my NJ home?

Yes, and it's legally required. The NJ Seller's Disclosure form requires disclosure of known water intrusion, flooding, or drainage issues. Failing to disclose known defects creates legal liability that follows you after the sale. Disclose what you know, show your repair documentation, and price accordingly. Buyers who discover undisclosed water history are far more disruptive than buyers who knew going in.
