7 Bathroom Decor Items NJ Home Sellers Need to Remove Before Showings

Buyers decide whether a bathroom feels clean or grimy within seconds of walking in. These 7 items — most of them things you bought to make the bathroom look nicer — are working against you.

Why Bathrooms Make or Break NJ Home Showings

I've walked through thousands of NJ homes. Buyers are polite in the living room, careful in the kitchen, but completely honest in the bathroom. That's where they stop to look. They open the medicine cabinet. They check under the sink. They notice the grout. They breathe.

Bathrooms are small, enclosed spaces. Every detail is close-up. A buyer who can overlook a dated kitchen can't ignore mildew on a shower curtain liner or a soap dispenser with crust around the pump. The bathroom experience is sensory in a way other rooms aren't — smell included.

I'm not talking about renovation. In most NJ homes in the $600K to $1.3M range, a well-cleaned, carefully staged bathroom beats a hastily renovated one every time. What you remove from that counter matters more than what you add. Here's what needs to go before your listing photos are taken and before the first showing.

The 7 Items That Undermine Your Bathroom's First Impression

1 Decorative Soap Dishes and Dispensers With Residue

That ceramic soap dish with the scalloped edges looked nice when you bought it. Now it has a ring of soap scum and a pool of pink bacterial residue underneath. Same goes for the lotion dispenser with dried drips down the side and a crust around the pump. These items signal that the bathroom gets surface cleaned but not actually cleaned.

NJ Seller Takeaway

Remove every soap dish, lotion bottle, and dispenser from your vanity counter while the home is listed. Replace with a single clean dish and a fresh bar of white soap, or nothing at all. Buyers don't need to see your full skincare routine. They need to see a counter that looks easy to keep clean.

2 Countertop Toothbrush Holders

Toothbrush holders are the most reliably grimy item in any bathroom. Even the ones you clean regularly accumulate toothpaste drip, water stain, and the general residue of daily use. Buyers who see a toothbrush holder on the counter are instantly reminded that other people's mouths happen in this space. It's not rational, but it's consistent — and I've heard it from buyers enough times to take it seriously.

NJ Seller Takeaway

Store toothbrushes and toothpaste in the medicine cabinet or under the sink for the duration of the listing. It takes 10 seconds to move them before each showing and costs nothing. A cleared counter looks larger and cleaner in listing photos — which drives showings, which drives offers.

3 Wicker and Fabric Storage Baskets

Wicker baskets under the sink or on shelving are a staging staple that's become a staging cliché — and for good reason. They absorb humidity. They collect dust and mold at the base. They hide clutter rather than eliminating it. In NJ's older housing stock, bathrooms with wicker baskets stuffed under a pedestal sink trigger the question: what's in there, and why isn't it somewhere else?

NJ Seller Takeaway

Empty the wicker baskets, clean out under the sink completely, and leave it visible and clean. Buyers who open that cabinet and see nothing but empty space respond differently than those who see a basket full of mystery products. Empty reads as "organized and maintained." Basket reads as "I don't want you to see this."

4 Used Candles With Wax Drips and Sooty Wicks

A candle in a bathroom is a staging move that can work — one clean candle on the vanity or tub ledge signals relaxation and care. But a candle that's been burned down halfway, has wax drips running down the side, and a blackened wick? That signals neglect. The same item that improves a staged bathroom can damage an unstaged one.

A cleared, clean vanity counter makes bathrooms photograph better and show better — Photo via Unsplash

NJ Seller Takeaway

Replace used candles with new ones — or remove them entirely. If you want a candle in the bathroom for listing photos and showings, buy a fresh one in white or ivory. Never light it before a showing. An unlit, fresh candle signals luxury. A burned-down candle signals a room that hasn't been properly staged.

5 Bath Rugs That Have Seen Better Days

Bath rugs absorb everything: moisture, soap, body products, and bathroom odor. Even rugs that look acceptable in your daily life photograph as dingy and smell musty to buyers walking in with fresh eyes. The shape of a worn bath rug — compressed pile in a shoe-sized oval in front of the toilet — is something buyers notice and cannot unsee.

NJ Seller Takeaway

Buy a new bath rug for the listing period. Spend $20 to $30. White or light gray. Put it down only for photos and showings — pick it up after and store it. Replace it mid-listing if it starts to show wear. A crisp bath rug signals bathroom cleanliness the way a freshly ironed shirt signals professionalism. Buyers notice.

6 Decorative Potpourri Bowls and Dried Flowers

Potpourri is one of those things that ages badly and fast. What smelled pleasant when it was fresh has a dusty, faded, slightly chemical smell after six months in a bathroom. The petals lose color, the basket collects dust, and the scent becomes something buyers can't name but react to negatively. Same goes for dried flower arrangements that have spent two years absorbing bathroom humidity.

NJ Seller Takeaway

Remove dried flowers and potpourri entirely. Do not try to refresh them. If you want fragrance in the bathroom, ventilate thoroughly before each showing and clean the space. A bathroom that smells like nothing smells better to buyers than one that smells like anything — especially old potpourri or artificial fragrance masking something underneath.

7 Decorative Towels That Aren't Actually Clean

Many sellers have a set of decorative guest towels that never get washed between showings. They've been hanging there since before the listing, touched by dozens of hands, and they look it. A hand towel that looks like it needs to be washed — even slightly — tells buyers the bathroom isn't as clean as it appears at first glance.

NJ Seller Takeaway

Wash or replace your display towels between every 3 to 5 showings. This is not excessive — this is the same thing hotel housekeeping does. If you're listing in a Union County or Essex County market where buyers have options in the $700K to $1.1M range, the difference between a fresh white towel and a slightly dingy one is part of the impression that separates your home from the competition.

The Bathroom Smell Problem NJ Sellers Don't Talk About

There is one more issue that doesn't involve decor: smell. NJ homes — particularly colonials and Capes with bathrooms on exterior walls or above crawl spaces — can develop a distinct musty smell that residents stop noticing entirely. Buyers walking in notice it immediately. Before your first showing, have someone who hasn't been in your home for several days walk through and tell you honestly what the bathroom smells like. If there's any mustiness, find the source (usually grout, caulk, or a slow drain) and address it before listing. No staging decision is more important than eliminating an odor.

What a Properly Staged NJ Bathroom Does for Your Sale

I list homes across Summit, Westfield, Maplewood, Chatham, Cranford, and the surrounding Union, Essex, and Morris County communities. In every market, the homes that sell fastest and closest to asking price are the ones where sellers did the work before the listing went live. The bathroom is one of the highest-leverage rooms — and most of the leverage comes from removing things, not adding them.

A stripped-down, sparkling clean bathroom photographs bigger than it is. It shows better than a decorated one. It gives buyers nothing to object to and everything to imagine as their own. That imagination — the buyer picturing themselves in the space — is exactly what drives offers.

Frequently Asked Questions

How should I stage a bathroom when selling my NJ home?

Remove all personal items from countertops — toothbrushes, medications, razors, soap dispensers. Replace with one or two simple items: a clean white candle, a small plant, or a neatly folded hand towel. Clean grout lines, replace worn caulk, and hang fresh white towels. Buyers form a lasting impression of a bathroom in under 10 seconds, and a cleared, spotless bathroom reads as well-maintained — which is worth money in NJ's $700K+ market.

Do NJ buyers really care that much about bathroom condition?

More than almost any other room. In buyer surveys, bathrooms and kitchens consistently rank as the two rooms that most influence buying decisions. A dated or grimy bathroom triggers discount requests and post-inspection credits. A clean, well-presented bathroom supports full-price offers. Across Union, Essex, and Morris counties, the bathroom condition conversation comes up in almost every transaction I'm part of.

Should I renovate my bathroom before selling in NJ?

Usually not. In most cases, deep cleaning, recaulking, regrouting, and removing dated decor produces 80% of the buyer impact at 10% of the renovation cost. Only renovate if the bathroom is severely dated or structurally compromised. Talk to your agent before spending money — the math varies by price point and neighborhood, and a $15,000 bathroom renovation doesn't always return $15,000 at closing.

What do NJ buyers look at in bathrooms during showings?

Everything. Tile and grout condition, caulk around the tub and vanity, faucet fixtures, toilet condition, ventilation fan noise, water pressure, and ceiling for water staining. They open the medicine cabinet. They check under the sink. A bathroom walkthrough tells a sophisticated buyer a lot about how a home has been maintained — which affects what they're willing to pay.

How much does bathroom staging cost for an NJ home sale?

Effective bathroom staging is primarily labor, not money. Recaulking: $10 to $30 in materials. New white towels: $20 to $40. Grout cleaning products: $15 to $25. New toilet seat (if needed): $30 to $80. Total out-of-pocket for most NJ sellers: $100 to $400. The return on removing dated decor and deep cleaning is far greater than any single bathroom purchase.
