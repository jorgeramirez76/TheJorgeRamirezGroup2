Why Closing Your Bedroom Door at Night Could Save Your Life

The Fire Safety Habit Nobody Talks About

Most of us know the standard fire safety checklist. Smoke detectors on every level. Fire extinguisher in the kitchen. Working CO detector near the bedroom. Test the batteries twice a year. Good list. Follow it.

But there's one fire safety action that costs nothing, takes two seconds, and according to fire safety researchers is one of the most impactful things you can do — and most homeowners have never heard of it. Close your bedroom door before you go to sleep.

That's it. That's the whole tip. But the reason behind it, backed by data from the Fire Safety Research Institute and FEMA, is worth understanding — especially if you own an older NJ home.

What the Research Actually Shows

The Fire Safety Research Institute (FSRI) — the research arm of UL, the organization that tests and certifies products for safety — ran live fire tests in actual structures to measure what happens inside rooms during a house fire. The results are stark enough to change how you sleep every night.

In rooms with an open door, temperatures reached approximately 1,000°F during the test fires. In adjacent rooms with a closed door, temperatures stayed around 100°F. That's a 900-degree difference created by a single interior door.

Carbon monoxide and smoke concentration followed the same pattern. Rooms with open doors filled with lethal concentrations of CO and combustion gases within minutes. Rooms with closed doors had significantly lower concentrations — enough to buy critical time to wake up, call 911, and get out.

The FSRI turned this research into a public campaign called "Close Before You Doze." The science is solid, the action is free, and it takes two seconds every night.

Why This Matters Especially in NJ Homes

New Jersey has a significant proportion of older housing stock. Many homes in Union, Essex, Morris, and Middlesex counties — the counties I work in every day — were built between 1940 and 1980. Those homes were built before modern fire-resistant construction standards. They have open stairwells that function as chimneys during fires, drawing heat and smoke upward toward sleeping areas. They have older electrical panels that carry increased ignition risk. They have fewer fire-blocking elements inside wall and floor assemblies.

In a newer home with modern construction, a fire in the kitchen might take 15 to 20 minutes to become life-threatening throughout the house. In a 1955 Colonial or 1968 split-level, the spread can be dramatically faster. Anything that buys your family more time to wake up and escape is worth taking seriously.

What Closing the Door Actually Does

A closed interior door does three things during a house fire. First, it limits the oxygen supply available to fuel the fire and its spread toward your room. Second, it blocks superheated air and combustion gases from entering the space where you're sleeping. Third, it slows the fire itself — a fire needs air to grow, and a closed door restricts that flow.

None of this makes a fire disappear. None of it substitutes for smoke detectors, an escape plan, or getting out of the house. What it does is buy you time — and in a house fire at 2am, time is exactly what you need to save your family.

The Complete NJ Home Fire Safety Checklist

Closing your bedroom door is one piece of a complete fire safety approach for NJ homeowners. Here's the full list:

NJ Home Fire Safety — What to Check Tonight

  • Close bedroom doors before sleeping — every room where anyone is sleeping, every night.
  • Smoke detectors on every level — including the basement and inside every bedroom. Test monthly. Replace every 10 years.
  • CO detector at sleeping level — not on the ceiling. Carbon monoxide is approximately the same weight as air, so ceiling placement is fine, but bedside level gives faster detection while you sleep.
  • Replace smoke detector batteries annually — New Year's Day or Daylight Saving Time are common reminders. Don't wait for the low-battery chirp at 3am.
  • Know two ways out of every room — especially upstairs bedrooms. A collapsible fire ladder stored under a second-floor bed is a $30 item that belongs in every NJ home.
  • Kitchen fire extinguisher — a 5-pound ABC extinguisher, mounted and visible, not buried in a cabinet.
  • No charging devices on upstairs carpets overnight — phone and laptop charging fires are a documented and growing ignition source.
  • Dryer lint trap cleaned every single cycle — dryer vent fires are among the most common appliance fires in NJ homes. Have the vent duct cleaned professionally every 1–2 years.

What This Means for NJ Home Buyers and Sellers

If you're buying a home in NJ, fire safety is part of the home inspection conversation — but only for the visible stuff. Inspectors check smoke detectors, CO detectors, and obvious electrical issues. They're not testing fire escape routes, checking door quality, or thinking about the age of the electrical panel from a fire risk perspective.

That's on you as the buyer. Ask about the age of the electrical panel. Ask about the dryer vent configuration. If the home has an open floor plan staircase or an older gas appliance setup, factor in what you'd need to do to bring it up to your comfort level.

If you're selling, working smoke and CO detectors in the right locations are part of what a buyer's inspector will verify. Make sure you have them. Beyond that, the condition of your home — older electrical, dryer vents, HVAC equipment — gets scrutinized. Buyers are paying $750K to $1.5M for NJ homes right now. They are not cavalier about what they're inheriting.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does closing your bedroom door really make a difference in a house fire?

The data is unambiguous. The Fire Safety Research Institute tested conditions during actual structure fires and found rooms with open doors reached approximately 1,000°F, while rooms with closed doors stayed around 100°F. FEMA reports 51% of fatal home fires occur between 10pm and 6am. A closed door gives you time to wake up, call 911, and escape.

What is the "Close Before You Doze" campaign?

Close Before You Doze is a public fire safety campaign supported by the Fire Safety Research Institute and UL that encourages people to close bedroom doors before sleeping. Research backing the campaign shows a closed door can create a 900°F temperature difference compared to an open door room during a house fire, significantly improving survival odds.

Are NJ homes especially at risk for fire?

NJ has a large percentage of older housing stock — many homes in Union, Essex, Morris, and Middlesex counties were built before 1980. Older homes often lack modern fire-resistant building materials, have open stairwells that act as chimneys, and may have outdated electrical systems that increase ignition risk. This makes fire safety habits especially important here.

Does a closed bedroom door affect carbon monoxide safety?

Yes. Research shows carbon monoxide levels inside a closed room during a house fire are significantly lower than in rooms with open doors. A closed door buys time on both heat and toxic gas exposure — in addition to, not instead of, having working CO detectors on every level of your NJ home.

What other fire safety measures should NJ homeowners take?

Install smoke detectors on every level and inside every bedroom. Test them monthly. Replace the entire unit every 10 years. Install CO detectors at sleeping areas. Have a fire escape plan your family has actually practiced. Keep a fire extinguisher in the kitchen. Have your electrical panel inspected if your home is 30+ years old.
