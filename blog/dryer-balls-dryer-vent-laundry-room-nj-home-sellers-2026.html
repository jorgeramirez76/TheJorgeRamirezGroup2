Dryer Balls, Dryer Vents and the Laundry Room: What NJ Buyers and Inspectors Check

Wool dryer balls wear out, and the way to tell is simple. What matters more when you sell is the vent behind the dryer, because a New Jersey home inspector will look at it, and a buyer will open the laundry room door either way.

Why a laundry question belongs in a real estate blog

House Outlook’s newsletter linked a Better Homes & Gardens piece this week asking whether wool dryer balls go bad. The reason a real estate agent cares is what sits behind the dryer. A home inspector in New Jersey checks the dryer vent on nearly every house I sell, from a split-level in Union to a colonial in Chatham, and a buyer opens the laundry room door whether it is upstairs, in the basement or in a closet off the kitchen.

Do wool dryer balls go bad?

Yes, slowly. A set of wool balls lasts roughly two to three years of regular use. They are done when they pill heavily, shed fibers onto clothes, lose their bounce or start to unravel. Until then they can be refreshed: run them through a hot wash cycle, then dry them on high heat by themselves.

The reason I push sellers toward dryer balls instead of sheets has nothing to do with laundry. Dryer sheets leave a waxy film on the lint screen and on the moisture sensor bars inside the drum. The screen looks clean but barely passes air, and the sensor misreads wet clothes as dry. Cycles run longer, the dryer runs hotter, and the electric or gas bill creeps up. A buyer who asks about running costs in Edison or Woodbridge is asking about exactly that.

Jorge’s tip: Once a month, wash the lint screen with warm soapy water and a soft brush, then hold it under the tap. If water pools on top instead of running through, that film is the problem, and it has been costing you drying time for months.

The dryer vent check every NJ inspector does

An inspector looks at three things. The duct between the dryer and the wall, the run inside the house, and the hood on the outside wall.

  • Plastic or thin foil flex duct. It traps lint in the ridges and it burns. Inspectors flag it and recommend rigid or semi-rigid metal duct. In older homes in Elizabeth, Union and Bloomfield I still see the white plastic kind behind the dryer.
  • A long, crushed or sagging run. A second-floor laundry room in a Westfield or Millburn colonial is a selling point, but its duct has to travel a long way to daylight. Every extra foot and elbow holds lint.
  • The exterior hood. The flap should open when the dryer runs and close when it stops. A hood packed with lint, a missing flap, or a screen full of fuzz gets a photo in the report.
  • Signs in the room. Lint on the floor behind the dryer, a damp laundry room, or clothes that take two cycles all point to a restricted vent.

The U.S. Fire Administration lists lint buildup and failure to clean as a leading cause of dryer fires. It is a safety item in the report, and safety items become buyer requests. My post on hidden fire hazards in NJ homes covers the rest of that list.

Condo and apartment dryers in Hoboken and Jersey City

Many units along the Hudson have ventless condensing dryers or a shared vent run. Buyers ask whether the dryer is vented and where. Know the answer, and if a condensing dryer has a water tank or a lint filter behind a lower panel, clean both before showings.

Staging the laundry room

Buyers in Summit and Morristown pay attention to laundry rooms because they use them every day. Clear the top of the machines, put the dryer balls in a small bowl instead of leaving a torn box of sheets, and get the piles off the floor. Wipe the drum and the gasket, run an empty hot cycle on the washer, and leave the doors open a crack so nothing smells musty. The washer setting guide and my post on outdated laundry habits cover the machines themselves.

Pre-listing dryer checklist

  1. Pull the dryer out, disconnect the duct and clean the lint out of the dryer outlet and the wall connection.
  2. Replace any plastic or foil flex duct with rigid or semi-rigid metal, with as few elbows as possible.
  3. Clean the full run to the exterior with a vent brush kit, or hire a vent cleaner and keep the receipt for the listing file.
  4. Go outside, run the dryer, and confirm the hood flap opens and warm air comes out. Replace a broken hood.
  5. Wash the lint screen, wipe the moisture sensor bars with rubbing alcohol, and switch from sheets to wool balls.
  6. Refresh or replace the dryer balls, then time a normal load. It should finish in one cycle.

What it does and does not do

A clean vent and a fresh set of dryer balls will not raise an appraisal. They remove a safety item from the inspection report before a buyer sees it, shorten drying cycles, and make a small room read as cared for. Put it on the same weekend as the seller maintenance list and the inspection red flags walk-through, and the laundry room will not cost you anything in negotiation.

Questions about dryers, vents and selling a NJ home

Do wool dryer balls go bad, and how do I know?

Yes. A set lasts roughly two to three years of regular use. They are finished when they pill heavily, shed wool onto clothes, lose their bounce or begin to unravel. Until then they can be refreshed rather than replaced.

How do I refresh wool dryer balls?

Run them through a hot wash cycle, then dry them alone on high heat. That tightens the wool and removes the residue they pick up from detergent and fabric. If they still shed or feel loose afterward, replace the set.

Are dryer sheets a problem before selling a home?

They leave a waxy film on the lint screen and the moisture sensor bars, which makes cycles run longer and hotter and raises energy use. Wash the screen with warm soapy water, wipe the sensor with rubbing alcohol and switch to wool balls before the listing period.

Will a NJ home inspector check the dryer vent?

Almost always. Inspectors flag plastic or foil flex duct, long or crushed runs, a lint-packed exterior hood and a missing flap. Lint buildup is a leading cause of dryer fires, so it is written up as a safety item, and safety items turn into buyer repair requests.

Should I replace the dryer vent duct before listing my NJ home?

If it is plastic or thin foil, yes. Replace it with rigid or semi-rigid metal duct with as few elbows as possible, clean the run to the exterior, and confirm the outside hood opens when the dryer runs. Keep the receipt in your listing file.
