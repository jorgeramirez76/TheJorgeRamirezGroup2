Appliances NJ Homeowners Should Never Leave Plugged In

Most NJ homeowners don't think twice about what stays plugged in overnight. Inspectors and insurance underwriters do. Here's what to change — and why it matters when you sell.

The Plugged-In Problem Nobody Talks About Until the Inspection

I've walked through hundreds of NJ homes over 15 years. The plugged-in toaster sitting under the cabinet with the paper towel roll two inches away. The space heater in the finished basement that hasn't moved since February. The hair dryer coiled up on the bathroom vanity, plugged in, sitting next to a full cup of water. None of these are dramatic. All of them are hazards.

According to FEMA, roughly 24,200 residential electrical fires happen in the US every year, causing nearly 300 deaths and over $1.2 billion in property damage. Appliances with heating elements — the ones most people leave plugged in out of convenience — are among the leading contributors. In NJ's older housing stock, where wiring is aging and outlets are sometimes overloaded, that risk runs higher than average.

There's also a financial angle that most homeowners don't think about until they're selling. Plugged-in appliances draw phantom power around the clock — electricity consumed while the device is "off" but still connected. The US Department of Energy estimates this standby draw accounts for 5–10% of your total electricity bill. In NJ, where PSE&G and JCP&L rates are among the highest in the Northeast, that translates to $150–$300 a year for the average household. Not catastrophic — but avoidable.

Here's what to look at and change, whether you're living in your home long-term or getting ready to list it.

1 Toasters and Toaster Ovens

This is the one that surprises sellers most often. Toasters and toaster ovens accumulate crumbs and grease around their heating elements over time. A stray electrical fault — or simply a power surge — can ignite that buildup when the appliance is plugged in but not in use. The problem is compounded when toasters are stored under upper cabinets or in tight corners with limited ventilation.

In NJ's kitchen-renovated 1960s and '70s ranches and split-levels, I see this exact setup constantly — toaster tucked under the cabinet, paper towels on a roll six inches to the right. That's a creditor scenario waiting for an inspection report.

The Fix:

Unplug your toaster and toaster oven after every use. Store them away from combustible materials when not in active rotation. Before listing, clean the crumb tray completely — it's one of those small details that signals maintenance habits to buyers who take a close look.

2 Space Heaters

Space heaters are ubiquitous in NJ homes, particularly in older colonials and ranches with drafty rooms, unfinished basements, and radiator systems that don't reach the back bedrooms. They're also a leading cause of residential fires, and that risk doesn't disappear when the unit is switched off — a faulty cord or nearby flammable material is still a hazard while the heater is connected to power.

NJ inspectors don't just look at where space heaters are used during a showing. They look at where they're stored. A space heater sitting between a plastic storage bin and a cardboard box in the basement utility room is a flag — especially if there's an extension cord involved.

The Fix:

Unplug space heaters when not in active use. Store them with their cords coiled neatly and away from combustibles. If you're prepping for a listing, put them in a closet or the garage — out of sight and off any outlet. And never run a space heater on an extension cord.

3 Hair Dryers and Curling Irons

Bathrooms in older NJ homes were not designed with the number of personal care appliances we run today. A single 15-amp circuit in a 1958 Colonial was never meant to power a 1,875-watt hair dryer, a curling iron, and an electric shaver simultaneously. But more critically — heat-generating personal care appliances left plugged in on a bathroom vanity, near a sink or shower, create both electrical and fire risk.

Buyers touring homes notice this. I've had buyers comment on bathroom countertop clutter mid-showing. It reads as disorganized, yes — but to the safety-conscious buyer, it also reads as careless.

The Fix:

Unplug heat-generating personal care appliances after every use. During showing prep, store them in a drawer or cabinet. It's a small detail that makes bathrooms show cleaner and signals that the home has been well cared for.

4 Slow Cookers and Instant Pots

Slow cookers have their own dedicated use case — you leave them plugged in and on for hours while you're out. That's fine when the appliance is functioning properly. The issue is slow cookers left plugged in and unattended when there's nothing in them, or aging appliances with frayed cords left on a cluttered counter. Residual heat from a recent use can also be a factor if combustible materials are nearby.

From a staging perspective: a slow cooker or Instant Pot left on the counter during showings signals a kitchen that lacks counter space. In NJ's older kitchens where counter space is already at a premium, every appliance left visible shrinks the perceived usable workspace.

The Fix:

Store these appliances in a cabinet when not in use. During active listing, get them off the counter entirely. It improves both safety and the visual perception of kitchen space — and both of those things matter to buyers.

5 Phone Chargers, Laptop Adapters, and Power Strips

Phone chargers draw phantom power as long as they're plugged into the wall, whether a phone is connected or not. That's a minor issue individually. The larger problem is the ecosystem that builds up around a home's outlets over time: a power strip running a laptop, phone charger, smart speaker, tablet, and a desk lamp — all plugged into a single 15-amp outlet that wasn't rated for that load.

Overloaded circuits are one of the most commonly documented electrical deficiencies in NJ home inspections. Buyers' inspectors look at outlet locations, extension cord usage, and power strip setups. When they see a multi-outlet strip daisy-chained into another strip, it goes in the report — and the buyer brings it up.

The Fix:

Use smart power strips with built-in surge protection and individual switches. Unplug chargers when not in use. Before listing, audit every outlet situation in the home — particularly home offices, bedrooms, and finished basement entertainment setups. If you're running too many things off a single circuit, an electrician can add an outlet for under $200.

The Real Estate Connection: Why This Matters When You Sell

Here's the part that most homeowner advice articles leave out. When a buyer's inspector documents electrical concerns — overloaded outlets, extension cord use as permanent wiring, appliances near water sources without GFCI protection — it goes into a written report with photographs. That report gives the buyer documented leverage to request a credit or price reduction.

In my experience, a single electrical flag in an inspection report leads to buyer credit requests ranging from $500 to $3,000, depending on the severity and what else is in the report. The issue itself might cost $75 to fix — but the documented uncertainty about what else might be lurking in the electrical system gives buyers much more room to negotiate.

Sellers who address electrical setup issues before listing — unplugging hazard-prone appliances, replacing overloaded extension cord situations with proper outlets, clearing appliances from water proximity — remove that ammunition before buyers ever get to the inspection stage. The inspection happens regardless. The question is what's in it when it does.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do plugged-in appliances cause fires in NJ homes?

Yes. FEMA data shows roughly 24,200 residential electrical fires per year nationally. Appliances with heating elements — toasters, space heaters, curling irons — are leading contributors when left plugged in unattended. In NJ's older housing stock with aging wiring, the risk is above the national baseline.

Do NJ home inspectors flag plugged-in appliances?

Inspectors flag the conditions created by poor appliance habits — overloaded outlets, extension cord misuse, appliances near water sources without GFCI protection. Those findings give buyers negotiating leverage and can cost sellers $500–$3,000 in post-inspection credits. Addressing them before listing prevents that entirely.

What is phantom power and how much does it cost NJ homeowners?

Phantom power is electricity appliances draw while turned off but still plugged in. The DOE estimates it's 5–10% of total household electricity use. In NJ, with PSE&G and JCP&L rates among the highest in the Northeast, that's approximately $150–$300 per year per household. Smart power strips with individual switches eliminate most of this waste without requiring any unplugging routine.

Does appliance safety affect my NJ homeowners insurance?

Yes. NJ insurance underwriters assess electrical risk when pricing policies. Documented electrical hazards — aging panels, extension cord use as permanent wiring, appliances near water sources — can raise premiums or affect coverage terms. Buyers who find these issues at inspection may also push for credits or price reductions. Fixing them before listing keeps both the inspection and the insurance transition smooth.

Should I address electrical issues before selling my NJ home?

Absolutely — especially the low-cost ones like outlet organization, GFCI protection near water, and appliance clutter. These cost almost nothing to fix and they remove documented leverage from buyer inspection reports. A pre-listing walk-through with an honest eye toward what buyers' inspectors will flag is one of the best things a seller can do before going to market.
