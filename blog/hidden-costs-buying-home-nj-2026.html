NJ Home Buying Costs: Cash to Close & First-Year Budget (2026)

Know what you will pay before closing, what remains due on closing day, and what needs to stay in reserve after you get the keys.

Start with three separate budgets

A purchase price does not tell you how much cash you need or what the home will cost each month. Use three lists: payments before settlement, the remaining cash to close, and money reserved for ownership. This guide focuses on those numbers; the NJ first-time buyer guide covers the broader purchase process.

The CFPB uses 2–5% of the purchase price as a rough closing-cost planning range, excluding the down payment. It is a national starting point, not a New Jersey quote. Replace it with your own estimates as you select a property and loan. CFPB: budgeting for a home purchase.

Avoid double-counting: A deposit is money already paid toward the transaction. An appraisal paid before closing can still appear on the closing paperwork. Mark every worksheet item as paid, due at closing or reserved for later.

Before closing: ask what is due and when

Inspection and follow-up evaluations

Request a written inspection quote and scope. Ask which additional evaluations are appropriate for this property, such as radon, sewer, septic, well or specialist reviews. An inspection evaluates condition; a lender’s appraisal serves a different purpose. Repair findings can affect negotiations or financing. CFPB: inspections and appraisals.

Deposit, appraisal and legal services

Record the deposit amount and due date from your contract. Ask the lender when any appraisal fee is collected. If hiring an attorney, request a fee agreement explaining what it covers and which third-party charges are separate. The NJDOBI home-buying guide recommends legal representation and explains attorney review; it does not establish a blanket requirement that every buyer and seller hire separate attorneys. A real estate agent cannot provide legal advice.

At closing: read the actual line items

Use the CFPB Loan Estimate explainer alongside your lender’s form. Confirm the loan amount, estimated payment, closing charges and estimated cash to close. Also identify taxes, insurance or assessments you must pay outside the mortgage payment.

  • Lender charges: Compare origination charges, points and lender credits across comparable loan offers. A lower upfront bill can come with a different interest rate. CFPB: comparing loan offers.
  • Title, settlement and recording: Obtain an itemized quote. Ask which title policy protects the lender, which protects you, and what exclusions and optional coverage apply.
  • Prepaids and initial escrow: Prepaid interest or an insurance premium is different from money deposited into an escrow account for future bills. Confirm the period covered by each item rather than combining everything under “taxes.”
  • Adjustments and credits: Have the settlement agent explain tax prorations, your deposit credit, agreed seller credits and charges paid before closing. Check the final amount instead of relying on an early percentage estimate. CFPB: closing fees and who pays.

The NJ transfer-fee change buyers should know

Older advice about a flat buyer-paid “mansion tax” is outdated. NJ Treasury says the Graduated Percent Fee is legally imposed on the grantor (seller) for covered deeds recorded on or after July 10, 2025. It applies above $1 million, with rates from 1% to 3.5% based on total consideration; the applicable rate is applied to the full amount, not only the excess. NJ Treasury’s July 2, 2025 notice.

Do not automatically add 1% to a buyer’s cash budget. Have your attorney and settlement agent confirm applicability, exemptions and any contract provision allocating the economic cost. This guide concerns an ordinary home purchase, not transfers of interests in business entities.

A cash worksheet without duplicate fees

Create one row per charge. Add columns for the provider, written quote, due date, amount already paid, amount still due and where it appears in the closing documents. Keep receipts with the worksheet.

Illustrative arithmetic — not a fee quote

Assume a $600,000 purchase with a chosen 10% down payment: $60,000.

Assume final buyer closing charges total $18,000, including a $600 appraisal already paid.

Assume a $10,000 deposit already paid and a $3,000 approved seller credit.

Remaining cash to close: $60,000 + $18,000 − $600 − $10,000 − $3,000 = $64,400.

These invented inputs demonstrate the calculation only. They are not NJ averages, a required down payment, or an estimate for your loan. Actual settlement adjustments and loan rules can change the result. Add separately paid inspections and your chosen moving/repair reserves to the overall savings plan, not to the lender’s final cash-to-close amount.

After closing: build a property-specific ownership budget

  • Property taxes: Obtain the current municipal tax bill and ask the tax office about pending assessments or changes affecting the property. Use the address’s records instead of a statewide rate multiplied by the purchase price. The NJ property-tax rate guide explains the distinction.
  • Insurance: Get an address-specific quote, deductibles and coverage details before finalizing the budget. Ask the insurer and lender about flood coverage and other exclusions.
  • Association costs: For a condo or association property, request current dues, approved assessments, transfer charges and the documents needed to understand upcoming obligations. Do not treat a low monthly fee as proof of low total costs.
  • Repairs and moving: Price the inspection items you intend to address, utility setup, moving and essential equipment. Separate immediate work from improvements you can defer.

Then make a monthly version: mortgage payment, costs not escrowed, utilities, dues and planned maintenance savings. Check that annual bills are not counted both inside the mortgage escrow payment and again as separate monthly spending. Keep an emergency cushion distinct from money already committed to repairs.

Questions to resolve before sending closing funds

  1. Does every deposit and approved credit appear correctly?
  2. Which charges have already been paid, and are they marked accordingly?
  3. What changed since the Loan Estimate, and why?
  4. Which taxes, insurance bills or assessments will I pay directly after closing?
  5. How much cash remains after the transaction and immediate moving or repair costs?

For most mortgage purchases, the Closing Disclosure must arrive at least three business days before closing. Review it with the lender or settlement agent and raise unexplained differences before signing. CFPB: what to expect at closing.

Buyer budget questions

Is cash to close the same as closing costs?

No. Cash to close includes the remaining down payment and closing charges, adjusted for deposits, credits and other settlement items. Use your lender’s final calculation rather than adding the full down payment twice.

Does an NJ buyer owe a flat 1% mansion tax?

For covered deed transfers recorded from July 10, 2025, the Graduated Percent Fee is legally imposed on the seller. Ask your attorney to review any negotiated allocation in the contract.

How much should I reserve for repairs?

Build the reserve from the inspection findings, contractor quotes, insurance deductibles and your household budget. Keep it separate from cash to close; a single statewide dollar amount cannot describe every property.
