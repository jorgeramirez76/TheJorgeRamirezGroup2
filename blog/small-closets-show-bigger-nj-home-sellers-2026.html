Small Closets in Older NJ Homes: How to Make Them Show Bigger to Buyers

The 1920s colonial in Westfield, the Tudor in Maplewood, the Cape in Cranford: gorgeous houses, tiny closets. Buyers open every one. Here's how to make them show bigger without a contractor or a custom closet system.

The closet problem every older NJ home has

Most of the homes I sell in Westfield, Summit, Maplewood, Montclair, and Cranford were built between 1900 and 1960. People owned fewer clothes back then, and the builders gave them closets to match: a single rod, one shelf, a door that barely clears the dresser. The houses are beautiful. The closets are an apology.

Buyers know this going in, especially buyers coming from Hoboken, Jersey City, or Manhattan. But knowing it and seeing it are different. When a buyer opens a closet in a Chatham colonial and clothes are pressed against the door, the house gets a label: "no storage." That label follows them through the rest of the showing and shows up in the offer, if there is one.

The size of the closet is fixed. What the buyer sees when they open it is not. Professional organizers have a short list of moves that make a small closet feel bigger, and for sellers they're cheap, fast, and worth doing in every closet in the house.

Jorge's observation: A half-empty closet in an older home reads as "enough." A packed closet of the exact same size reads as "we'll need an addition." Same closet, two very different offers.

Six moves that make small closets show bigger

  1. Follow the half-empty rule You're moving. Start packing now. Off-season clothes, extra bedding, suitcases, the formalwear you wear twice a year: box it, label it, and get it to the garage or a storage unit. Every closet should be no more than half full when the listing goes live. Buyers need to see rod space between the hangers, part of the back wall, and the floor. That empty space is what they picture their own things filling.
    Cost: Boxes and a weekend · Impact: The biggest change buyers notice
  2. Switch to one style of slim hanger A mix of wire, plastic, and wood hangers eats rod space and looks chaotic. Slim velvet or matching wood hangers, all facing the same direction, take up about half the room and make the closet look intentional. Group by type, then by color. It sounds fussy, but it photographs like a boutique, and buyers notice.
    Cost: $20–$40 for a set of 50 · Time: One hour per closet
  3. Add a second rod and use the vertical space A single rod with dead air above the shelf and three feet of dead air below the hems is the classic older-home closet. A hanging double rod that hooks over the existing one doubles the space for shirts and pants without a single screw. Above the shelf, add matching lidded bins. Below, a low shoe rack. Now the closet is working top to bottom.
    Cost: $15–$30 for a hanging rod · Impact: Effectively doubles hanging space
  4. Use the back of the door An over-the-door organizer for shoes or accessories pulls a shelf's worth of stuff off the floor. Keep it tidy and keep it to one door. Six overloaded door racks tell the buyer you've run out of room. One clean one tells them you're organized.
    Cost: $10–$25 · Impact: Clears the closet floor
  5. Light it, paint it, and make it smell like nothing Closets in older homes are often dark and a little musty. A battery-powered motion-sensor LED puck light changes everything for under $20. A coat of white paint on scuffed closet walls makes the space feel bigger and cleaner. Then deal with smell: wipe down the shelves, run a dehumidifier for a few days, and put a small charcoal bag inside. Buyers who smell mildew in a closet immediately start thinking about the basement.
    Cost: $20–$50 · Impact: Removes the two biggest older-home closet red flags
  6. Stage the linen and hall closets too Buyers open the hall closet, the linen closet, and the coat closet, not just the bedrooms. Fold towels in thirds, stack sheets inside their pillowcases, and leave one shelf visibly empty. Coat closets hold a few coats and nothing on the floor. These are the closets sellers forget, and buyers open them on the way to the door, when the final impression is forming.
    Time: 30 minutes each · Impact: Consistency across the whole house

Photo via Unsplash — A calm, organized bedroom with a half-empty closet tells buyers this older home has room to grow into.

When it's worth spending a little more

Most sellers don't need a custom closet system. But two modest upgrades can pay off. If the primary closet still has one rod and one shelf, an adjustable wire or laminate kit, installed for a few hundred dollars, makes it look finished in listing photos. And if a bedroom has no closet at all, which happens in older homes from Union to Morristown, a freestanding wardrobe that matches the room answers the question before the buyer asks it.

What I'd skip: expensive built-ins in a house already priced at the top of its range. You won't get the money back.

Small closets are part of the charm of an older NJ home, and the buyers who love these houses accept them. What they won't accept is a closet that looks like a storage problem. Frequently asked questions

Do small closets hurt the sale price of an older NJ home?

Small closets rarely kill a sale on their own, but overstuffed small closets do cost money. Buyers who open a closet and cannot see the back wall assume the house has a storage problem and start pricing in a closet system, a built-in, or an addition. A half-empty, organized closet of the same size reads as adequate, and the buyer moves on to the next room without a deduction in mind.

How empty should closets be when a NJ home is on the market?

About half. Move off-season clothing, extra bedding, and anything you will not need for the next two or three months into labeled boxes in the garage, basement, or a storage unit. Leave enough hanging to show the closet is used, with visible rod space between hangers and visible floor. Buyers read that empty space as room for their own things.

Is it worth installing a closet system before selling a NJ home?

Usually not a full custom system. A clean, well-organized closet with a double rod, matching hangers, and a few shelf bins delivers most of the visual benefit for a fraction of the price. If a reach-in closet still has only a single rod and one shelf, a basic adjustable wire or laminate kit is a reasonable, modest upgrade that photographs well and makes the closet feel finished.

What do NJ buyers look for when they open a closet?

They are looking for three things: how much room there is, whether the closet is dry and clean, and whether it smells fine. They notice the floor, the back wall, the rod, and any signs of moisture or mustiness, which are common in older homes across Union, Essex, and Morris counties. A closet that is half empty, freshly wiped down, well lit, and odor free answers all three questions in about five seconds.

Should closets be included in listing photos for a NJ home?

A walk-in or an especially well-organized primary closet can be worth a photo, because storage is one of the top concerns for buyers moving from apartments in Hoboken, Jersey City, and New York. A cramped reach-in should not be photographed, but it still needs to be organized, because buyers will open it in person and the impression sticks.
