What's Actually Changed in NJ's Market

The national data understates the NJ commuter belt story, in both directions. NJ's premium commuter towns never corrected the way some national markets did. Summit, Westfield, Chatham, Maplewood, and Cranford maintained strong demand because the NYC commuter buyer pool held steady — and hybrid work schedules kept buyers looking for more space in walkable suburbs within 60 minutes of Manhattan.

What has changed in NJ since the peak:

  • Days on market have increased. The 8-12 day average in the hottest markets is gone. Most Union County homes are now seeing 18-28 days before an accepted offer. Essex County is similar.
  • Multiple offers are less automatic. Well-priced, move-in-ready homes still get them. Overpriced homes don't. This is a meaningful change from 2022-2023 when almost anything got multiple offers.
  • Buyers are doing inspections again. In the peak market, buyers routinely waived inspections. That practice has largely ended in 2026. Expect inspections, expect inspection-contingent offers, expect requests for credits or repairs.
  • Condition matters more. When buyers had limited options, they accepted deferred maintenance. With more choices, they're choosing the better-condition home at the same price. Or asking for credits that reflect what needs to be done.

What NJ Sellers Need to Do Differently Right Now

Pricing

Price to the Last 60-90 Days, Not the Last 12 Months

This is the most common mistake I'm seeing NJ sellers make right now. They pull comparable sales from 2025 and early 2026 and price accordingly. But the market has shifted enough that those numbers overstate current values in some NJ towns. The right comps are the last 60-90 days, in your specific neighborhood, for homes of similar condition and size.

I've watched sellers overprice by $50,000-$100,000 in Cranford, Madison, and parts of Essex County this year and sit for 60-90 days before reducing to where they should have started. That's money left on the table — not because the market was bad, but because the pricing was wrong. An overpriced listing accumulates days on market, which signals to buyers that something is wrong with the home, which generates lower offers than a correctly-priced listing generates on day 5.

Condition

Fix What Buyers Will Ask for Credits on Anyway

Every deferred maintenance item in your home is a negotiating chip for the buyer at inspection. A buyer who sees a roof approaching end of life, a water heater past its expected lifespan, a cracked driveway, or wood rot at the deck doesn't think "I'll just credit that." They think "what else hasn't been maintained?" and it affects their entire confidence level in the home.

I recommend that sellers in NJ get a pre-listing inspection — a full home inspection done before you list, so you know what's there. Then you choose: fix it and take the negotiating chip away, or disclose it and price accordingly. Either way, you're in control. The worst outcome is finding out at the buyer's inspection, when their inspector's findings become the basis for a renegotiation that weakens your position.

Presentation

This Is Not the Market to List Without Preparation

In 2022, buyers were waiving appraisals, waiving inspections, and writing love letters to win homes. They were overlooking unpainted walls, cluttered rooms, and dated kitchens. That market rewarded sellers who did nothing to prepare, which meant a lot of sellers learned the wrong lesson.

In July 2026, a competing home that is staged, freshly painted, and move-in ready will get the offer that your un-prepared home does not — even if your home has better bones. Buyers who have options choose the easier option. Make your home the easier option.

The Mistake That's Costing NJ Sellers Real Money Right Now

I want to be direct about something: the sellers who are getting hurt in the current NJ market are the ones waiting for conditions to return to 2022. They're not returning. The sellers who are winning are pricing accurately to today's market, presenting their home well, and moving decisively when a real buyer appears. A correct offer from a qualified buyer on day 15 is better than a "better" offer that never materializes while your home accumulates 90 days on market and requires a price reduction.

The NJ Seller's Advantage That Hasn't Changed

Here is what I want NJ sellers to understand clearly: the fundamentals that made NJ real estate strong have not changed. Proximity to Manhattan. Excellent school systems. The Midtown Direct. Union County's and Essex County's infrastructure. Limited buildable land that keeps supply constrained. These don't disappear in a slower national market.

What changes is the margin for error. In a hot market, sellers could overprice by 10%, list without preparation, and still get an offer. In today's market, those same mistakes mean sitting for 90 days and ultimately selling for less than a correctly-priced, well-presented listing would have achieved. The opportunity is still there. The execution has to be better.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it still a seller's market in NJ in July 2026?

In the best NJ commuter towns — Summit, Westfield, Chatham, Maplewood, Cranford — yes, with qualifications. Inventory has increased modestly but demand from NYC commuters remains strong. Homes priced correctly and presented well still receive multiple offers in these towns. The market has become more competitive, meaning overpriced homes now sit rather than selling despite being overpriced. Sellers should no longer assume any price is acceptable.

How long are homes staying on the market in NJ in 2026?

Days on market in NJ's top commuter towns have increased from the 2022-2024 lows of 8-15 days to a more typical 18-30 days in mid-2026. Homes that are priced right, staged well, and in move-in condition still go fast — often under 2 weeks with multiple offers. Homes that are overpriced or need significant work are taking 45-90 days and often require price reductions.

Should I wait to sell my NJ home or sell now in 2026?

Nationally, price growth is projected at 1.2% for 2026 — below inflation. In NJ's best commuter towns, appreciation has been stronger but is moderating. Sellers who are waiting for prices to spike again are likely to be disappointed in the near term. The summer and fall 2026 window is competitive with adequate buyer demand. Waiting for conditions that may not return is a worse strategy than selling correctly into the current market.

Are NJ home prices dropping in 2026?

Not in the NJ commuter towns I work in. Prices in Summit, Westfield, Chatham, Maplewood, and Cranford are flat to modestly positive year-over-year in mid-2026. What has changed is that overpriced homes are now being corrected by the market rather than selling anyway — which is a different dynamic than outright price declines.

How do I price my NJ home correctly in the July 2026 market?

Price to the comparable sales from the last 60-90 days, not the last 12 months. A good listing agent will pull the most recent comparable sales, adjust for condition and location, and price to generate showings in the first week — not to maximize the list price. In a balanced market, overpricing is a much bigger mistake than it was in 2022-2024. An overpriced listing sits, accumulates days on market, and eventually sells below what a correctly priced listing would have achieved.
