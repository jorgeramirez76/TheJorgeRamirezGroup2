Selling a NJ Home With Pets: The Cleaning Routine That Keeps Buyers From Noticing

Most buyers say they love dogs. Almost none of them want to smell yours. House Outlook’s list of pet-owner cleaning products is a good starting kit. Here is how to use it when the house is on the market.

The kit House Outlook recommended

House Outlook published a roundup of the cleaning products pet owners say they cannot live without. The categories are the ones I would expect: an enzyme cleaner for accidents, a rubber brush or roller for hair, a vacuum built for pet hair, an odor absorber that is not a scented spray, washable furniture covers, and a carpet stain remover. That is a solid kit for living with a pet.

Selling with one is a different job. When your house in Westfield, Summit or Montclair is listed, the goal is not a clean house. It is a house where a buyer with no pets cannot tell one lives there. Here is how the same kit gets you there.

Why pets cost sellers more than they think

Buyers walk in nose first. Sellers with pets have stopped smelling their own house, and that is true of everyone, myself included. Odor is the first thing a buyer notices and the last thing they mention. It shows up as “we didn’t connect with it” and no second showing.

After odor comes evidence: hair on the sofa, nose prints on the slider, a worn patch of lawn along the fence in Scotch Plains, scratched trim at the back door of a Cranford colonial, and a litter box in the powder room. None of it is a defect. All of it tells a buyer the house has been used hard, and they start subtracting for floors whether or not the floors need it.

The pre-listing pass, step by step

  1. Find every accident, including the old ones. Walk the house at night with a UV flashlight. Treat every spot with enzyme cleaner, let it dry fully, and repeat. Enzyme takes time, not scrubbing.
  2. Wash everything soft. Slipcovers, throws, dog beds, curtains in the rooms the pet uses, and the rugs, professionally if they are wool. Odor lives in fabric. My couch-cleaning guide covers the upholstery you cannot wash.
  3. Vacuum like you are being graded. HEPA vacuum on floors, stairs, baseboards and sofa cushions. Change the HVAC filter the week before photos and again before the first open house.
  4. Repair, do not hide. Scratched doors get filled and painted. A urine-damaged subfloor gets sealed or replaced, not carpeted over. That is a disclosure question in New Jersey, and it is also a smell that comes back in July.
  5. Move the pet’s footprint. Crate, bowls, litter box, toys and the bed come out of the photos. During showings they go to the garage, the car or a single low-traffic room with the door open and the box clean.

Jorge’s tip: Skip the plug-in air fresheners and the scented candles. Experienced buyers read heavy fragrance as a cover-up, and so do inspectors. Open windows for twenty minutes before every showing, run the bathroom fans, and let the house smell like nothing.

Showing day with a dog or a cat

Take the dog with you. A crated dog barking through a showing in Maplewood or Millburn shortens the visit to four minutes, and some buyers will not enter a room with a loose animal. Cats can stay, but put a note on the door so the buyer’s agent does not let one out. Brush the sofa and stairs, wipe the nose prints off the glass, put the bowls in a cabinet, open the windows, and leave ten minutes early. My evening reset checklist covers the rest of the routine.

The yard counts too

Buyers in Edison, Woodbridge and Union walk the yard, and the yard tells the truth. Pick up every day, not showing day. Reseed the worn track along the fence in early fall, the right time for cool-season grass in New Jersey anyway. Fill the dug-out spot by the gate, and take down a temporary dog run. The backyard mistakes guide covers what else buyers notice out there.

For buyers: what pet ownership tells you about a house

If you are touring in Hoboken, Jersey City or Morristown and you can smell a pet, do not walk away and do not ignore it. Look under rugs for stains, check door bottoms for scratches, and ask the inspector about the carpet pad and subfloor in any room that smells. A well-managed pet means a careful owner. A pet with the run of the place is a repair conversation, so price the offer that way.

Questions about pets, cleaning and showing a NJ home

Does having a pet lower my home’s value?

The pet does not. Pet damage and pet odor do, because buyers price them as repairs and because odor cuts the number of second showings. A house that has been cleaned with enzyme products, vacuumed with a HEPA machine and aired out before each showing gives buyers nothing to subtract.

What is the most important cleaning product for a seller with pets?

An enzyme cleaner. Ordinary cleaners and air fresheners cover pet odor for an hour; enzymes break down the proteins that cause it. Use it on every spot you know about, then on the areas around them, because accidents spread further than the visible stain.

Should the dog be home during showings?

No. Take the dog with you, or arrange for daycare or a neighbor on showing days. A crated dog barking through a showing shortens the visit, and some buyers will not enter a room with a loose animal. Remove the crate, bowls and bed from the photos and, if you can, from the showings.

How do I handle the litter box while my NJ home is listed?

Scoop it twice a day, change it fully every few days, and move it out of the main living areas to a laundry room, mudroom or basement with a mat under it. Never put it in a closet a buyer will open. For photos and showings, a clean box in a plain location is fine; a box in the kitchen or a bathroom is not.

Do I have to disclose pet damage when selling in New Jersey?

Known damage to floors, subfloors, carpets or trim is a condition issue, and New Jersey sellers are expected to disclose known material defects. Clean and repair what you can, but do not paint over or carpet over a urine-damaged subfloor and hope. Talk to your agent and attorney about how to describe anything you could not fully fix.
