Before You Turn On the Heat: The Furnace Check Every NJ Seller and Buyer Should Make This Month

House Outlook’s reminder this week is simple: before the first cold night, replace the furnace filter. It is the cheapest thing you can do for a heating system and the one most people skip. If your house in Westfield, Chatham or Montclair is listed or about to be, the stakes are a little higher. The first time the heat runs this fall will probably be in front of a buyer, and the first person to pull that filter will probably be their inspector.

The House Outlook reminder

A clogged filter restricts airflow. The blower works harder, the heat exchanger runs hotter, the system cycles on and off, and the dust the filter was supposed to catch ends up on your furniture and in your lungs. The article’s advice is to swap the filter before the heat goes on, and then on whatever schedule the filter size calls for. One-inch filters need changing often; the thick media filters last longer.

I would add one thing: write the date and the filter size on the cardboard frame in marker. It takes five seconds and it answers the first question a buyer’s inspector asks.

Why it matters more when the house is for sale

Three things happen in a listed house that do not happen otherwise.

First, the smell. The first run of the season burns the summer’s dust off the heat exchanger and the ductwork, and the house smells like a hot iron for an hour. If that hour lands during a Saturday showing in Summit or Cranford, buyers walk in and ask what is burning. Run the heat for twenty minutes with the windows cracked before the first showing, not during it.

Second, the filter is evidence. Inspectors pull it. A gray, bowed, year-old filter tells them the furnace has not been looked at, and they write the rest of the report with that in mind. A clean filter with a date on it tells them the opposite.

Third, the age question. Buyers in New Jersey ask how old the furnace is before they ask almost anything else, because they know a replacement is a real number. If you cannot answer, the systems age guide shows where to find the date on the data plate. Know it before the open house.

The twenty-minute pre-season check

  1. Replace the filter and date it. Leave two spares on the shelf next to the furnace.
  2. Run the heat before anyone else does. Windows open, twenty minutes, so the dust smell is gone before the first showing.
  3. Clear three feet around the furnace and water heater. Nothing leaning on the cabinet, nothing stored against the flue. Inspectors need access. The unsafe storage spots post covers what else to move.
  4. Look at the vent connections. You are checking that the flue pipe is connected and not rusted through. If something looks wrong, call an HVAC contractor.
  5. Test the carbon monoxide and smoke alarms. New Jersey towns require a smoke and carbon monoxide certificate at sale, and the inspector for that certificate will test them anyway.
  6. Check the thermostat. Fresh batteries, a sensible program, and no error message on the screen. A “replace battery” warning in a listing photo is a small thing that looks big.
  7. Book a service if it has been more than a year. Keep the receipt. A tune-up invoice dated this fall is one of the most useful pieces of paper in your house handbook.

Jorge’s tip: If the furnace is old and working, do not replace it to sell. Service it, document it, price the house knowing a buyer may ask for a credit, and let that negotiation happen with the inspection report in hand. Replacing a working furnace in the month before listing rarely returns what it costs. Replacing a dead one is a different conversation; we should have it before the house goes on the market.

For buyers: what to ask while the heat is running

If you are touring houses in Morristown, Maplewood or Edison this month, the heat will be on, and that is useful. Stand near a supply register and feel whether warm air is actually moving. Listen for the system cycling on and off every few minutes, which can mean a restricted filter or something more. Ask for the furnace’s age, the fuel type and the last service date, and ask whether there is or was an oil tank. An older home that was converted from oil to gas needs the oil tank sweep conversation before attorney review ends, not after.

Then let your inspector do the real work. A home inspection covers the furnace’s operation and visible condition, not a full HVAC evaluation. If the system is old or anything is flagged, a separate HVAC inspection before the contingency expires is money well spent. The inspection questions guide has the rest of the list.

What this does for the sale

None of this raises the appraised value of the house. It removes a reason for a buyer to hesitate, and in a fall market where buyers in Union and Morris counties have time to compare, that is most of the job. A house that is warm when they walk in, smells like nothing, and comes with a dated filter and a service receipt reads as cared for, and that carries into the negotiation.

Questions about furnaces, heat and selling a NJ home

Do I need to service the furnace before listing my NJ home?

There is no rule that requires it, but a service visit within the past year, with the invoice in the house handbook, answers the inspector’s questions before they are asked. If the furnace has not been serviced in several years, a fall tune-up is usually the cheapest way to avoid a repair credit conversation later.

Why does the house smell like something is burning when the heat first comes on?

It is usually summer dust burning off the heat exchanger and ductwork, and it clears in an hour with the windows cracked. Run the heat once before the first showing so that hour does not happen in front of buyers. A smell that persists, or anything electrical or plastic, needs an HVAC contractor before the next showing.

Will a buyer’s inspector check the furnace filter?

Yes. Pulling the filter is routine, and its condition shapes how the inspector reads the rest of the system. A clean filter with the size and date written on the frame, plus a spare on the shelf, is a small detail that signals the house has been maintained.

Should I replace an old furnace before selling in New Jersey?

If it works, generally no. Service it, document its age and condition, and price the house with the possibility of a credit in mind. Replacing a working furnace right before listing rarely returns its cost. A furnace that does not work is a different situation and should be addressed before the home is marketed.

What should a buyer ask about the heating system during a NJ showing?

The furnace’s age and fuel type, the last service date, whether the home ever had an oil tank, and whether there are rooms that never warm up. Feel for airflow at a register and listen for short cycling. Then have your home inspector evaluate it, and consider a separate HVAC inspection if the system is old.
