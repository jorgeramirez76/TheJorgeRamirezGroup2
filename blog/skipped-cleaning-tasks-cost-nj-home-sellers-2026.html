9 Cleaning Tasks NJ Homeowners Skip That Cost Thousands at Sale Time

These aren't chores. They're future inspection findings, repair credits, and utility bills. Ten minutes today, or a negotiation at closing — here's the list I hand every NJ seller before we go live.

The inspection report is where skipped chores get expensive

Across Union, Morris, Essex, Hudson, and Middlesex counties, I've read a lot of inspection reports. Very few of the findings that cost sellers money are surprises. Most of them are the result of something that should have been cleaned or serviced years ago and wasn't — a dryer vent packed with lint, a furnace that's never seen a filter change, gutters that haven't been touched since the previous owner.

Here's what sellers in Westfield, Livingston, and Metuchen don't always appreciate: the buyer isn't just reading each finding on its own. They're reading the pattern. Three or four "deferred maintenance" items together tell a story that the home hasn't been cared for, and once buyers believe that story, they negotiate harder on everything — including things that are perfectly fine.

The nine tasks below are the ones I see most often. Every one of them is cheap to handle now and expensive to explain later.

Jorge's observation: A buyer who finds a clean dryer vent, a fresh furnace filter, and clear gutters on inspection day stops looking for problems. A buyer who finds the opposite starts looking for more. That shift in mindset is worth more than any single repair.

The 9 tasks that quietly cost NJ sellers money

  1. Cleaning the dryer vent and exhaust duct Lint buildup in the duct forces the dryer to work harder, which means longer cycles, higher electric bills, and a motor that wears out early. It's also a fire hazard — the National Fire Protection Association lists failure to clean as the leading cause of dryer fires. Inspectors in NJ check this on nearly every home, and a clogged vent almost always turns into a repair request. A professional cleaning runs $100–$200. The lint trap is not the vent; clean both.
    Cost now: $100–$200 · Cost later: Inspection finding + fire risk
  2. Wiping ceiling fan blades and HVAC registers Dust on fan blades doesn't stay on the blades. It gets blown into the air, pulled into the return, and clogs the HVAC filter faster — which makes the system work harder and costs you on the utility bill. On inspection day, a dusty system is a system that isn't running at its best while someone is measuring how well it runs. Wipe blades with a pillowcase (slide it over the blade and pull), vacuum the registers, and replace the filter.
    Time: 30 minutes · Impact: HVAC performs better on inspection day
  3. Vacuuming refrigerator condenser coils The coils under or behind the fridge dissipate heat. When they're coated in dust and pet hair, the compressor runs longer, uses more electricity, and fails earlier — and a failed compressor on a built-in fridge in a Chatham or Short Hills kitchen is a four-figure repair. Pull the fridge, vacuum the coils with a brush attachment, and do it every six months. Ten minutes.
    Time: 10 minutes · Prevents: Premature compressor failure
  4. Cleaning gutters and downspouts Clogged gutters overflow. Overflowing gutters dump water against the foundation, rot fascia boards, and — in the NJ freeze-thaw cycle — feed ice dams that push water under the shingles. Water intrusion is the single most expensive category of inspection finding. Clean gutters twice a year, especially after the fall leaf drop, and check that downspouts discharge at least four feet from the foundation.
    Cost now: $150–$300 · Cost later: Foundation, fascia, and roof findings
  5. Degreasing the range hood filter A grease-saturated hood filter stops pulling air, which means cooking grease settles on the cabinets, the ceiling, and the walls — a smell and a film that buyers notice immediately. Saturated filters are also a fire risk over a gas range. Most metal mesh filters go through the dishwasher or soak in hot water with degreaser. Charcoal filters get replaced.
    Time: 20 minutes · Impact: Kitchen smells clean, hood actually works
  6. Clearing the dishwasher filter and washing machine gasket A clogged dishwasher filter leaves food film on dishes and a smell that fills the kitchen during showings. A washing machine rubber gasket that's never been wiped grows mildew that buyers can smell from the hallway. Both take five minutes. Both make buyers assume the appliances are old and failing when they aren't.
    Time: 10 minutes total · Impact: No appliance smells during showings
  7. Flushing the water heater Sediment settles at the bottom of a tank water heater and insulates the burner from the water, so it takes more energy to heat and the tank fails years early. Inspectors note the age and condition of the water heater on every report. A tank that's rumbling or popping is a tank that's full of sediment, and buyers ask for a credit toward replacement. An annual flush is a homeowner task; a first-time flush on a neglected tank is worth a plumber visit.
    Cost now: $0–$150 · Cost later: $1,500–$3,000 replacement credit
  8. Cleaning bathroom exhaust fans A dust-choked bath fan doesn't move air, so moisture stays in the bathroom. That's where mildew on grout, peeling paint on the ceiling, and — worst case — mold behind the drywall come from. Buyers see mildew and think water problem. Pop the cover, vacuum the fan blades and housing, and confirm the fan actually pulls a sheet of toilet paper to the grille when running.
    Time: 10 minutes per fan · Prevents: Moisture and mildew findings
  9. Servicing the furnace and changing filters on schedule An HVAC system that's never been serviced is the finding I see most often in Morris and Union County homes over 20 years old. A dirty filter restricts airflow, strains the blower, and can crack a heat exchanger over time — which is a system replacement, not a repair. Annual service runs $100–$200 and gives you a dated receipt that answers the buyer's biggest question about the most expensive system in the house.
    Cost now: $100–$200 · Cost later: The most-negotiated item on NJ inspections

How I sequence this before listing

Thirty to sixty days before photos, we schedule the three vendor tasks: dryer vent, gutters, and HVAC service. That lead time matters because those visits sometimes uncover something else — a disconnected duct, a rotted fascia board — and you want that fixed before a buyer's inspector finds it, not after.

A week before photos, the homeowner tasks happen in one weekend: fridge coils, range hood filter, dishwasher filter, washer gasket, ceiling fans, bath fans, and a fresh furnace filter. Then we keep the receipts from the vendor visits in a folder with the seller's disclosure. When a buyer's agent in Cranford or Summit asks when the furnace was last serviced, the answer is a dated invoice, not a shrug.

None of this raises your list price. What it does is protect it. The homes I've sold at or above asking in this market share one trait: they inspected cleanly, and the buyer never got a reason to start renegotiating. That's what these nine tasks buy you.

Frequently asked questions

Which skipped cleaning task costs NJ sellers the most at closing?

The dryer vent. A lint-clogged duct is a fire hazard flagged on almost every NJ inspection, usually with a request for a professional cleaning or a credit — and it raises the question of what else hasn't been maintained. A $100–$200 cleaning before listing removes the finding entirely.

Do home inspectors in NJ really flag dirty ceiling fans and vents?

Inspectors don't grade cleanliness, but they photograph what they see, and buyers read those photos as evidence of how the home was cared for. Heavy dust also affects how the HVAC system performs on inspection day — which is what the inspector is actually testing.

How far in advance of listing should I do these tasks?

Ideally 30 to 60 days before photos for the vendor tasks — dryer vent, gutters, HVAC service — so there's time to fix anything they uncover. The quick homeowner tasks can be done in one weekend a week before photos.

Will doing these tasks raise my sale price?

They protect it more than raise it. In markets like Summit, Westfield, Montclair, and Cranford, buyers pay full price for homes that inspect cleanly. Every finding is a reason to ask for a credit, and several together tell a story that the home hasn't been maintained. Removing that story is worth thousands.

Should I hire a professional or do these myself?

Hire out the dryer vent, gutters on a two-story home, and the annual HVAC service. Fridge coils, range hood filter, dishwasher filter, ceiling fans, bath fans, and the washer gasket are homeowner tasks with basic tools. Jorge can refer trusted NJ vendors for the rest.
