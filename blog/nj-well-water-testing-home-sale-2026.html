Well Water Testing in a New Jersey Home Sale: What Buyers and Sellers Should Expect

Last updated  ·  August 2026

Quick answer: In New Jersey, a home served by a private well generally has to be tested by a state-certified lab as part of the sale, with the results shared with the buyer before closing. The law is built around testing and disclosure rather than requiring a specific repair, so what happens after an unfavorable result is usually negotiated between the parties.

By Jorge Ramirez | August 2026

Most conversations about water in a house start with the wrong question. People ask whether the water tastes good. The question that actually shapes a real estate transaction is simpler and less romantic: where does the water come from, and who is responsible for proving it is safe?

If you are selling a New Jersey home that runs on a private well, that second question has a legal answer attached to it. And in my experience, it is one of the most common reasons an otherwise smooth deal turns into a scramble two weeks before closing.

First, Figure Out Which Kind of House You Have

A large share of New Jersey is on public water. If your home is in Union, Essex, or Hudson County, odds are good a utility delivers your water and bills you for it quarterly. Private wells become far more common as you move west and north — much of Morris County, the western half of Somerset, and the more rural stretches of Hunterdon, Sussex, and Warren. Middlesex is a mix depending on the town.

You can usually tell in a minute. If there is a water bill from a utility, you are on public water. If there is a pressure tank in the basement, a well cap somewhere in the yard, and no water bill at all, you have a well. Plenty of longtime owners genuinely are not sure, especially if they inherited the property or bought it decades ago. It is worth confirming before you list rather than discovering it during attorney review.

What New Jersey Asks of a Sale With a Private Well

New Jersey's Private Well Testing Act applies to real estate transactions involving homes served by a private well. In practical terms, it means the well needs to be sampled by a laboratory certified by the state, and the results have to be provided to the buyer before the sale closes. Both parties are expected to review and acknowledge them.

Two details matter more than most people realize. First, the panel of contaminants required is not identical statewide — the list varies by region, because different parts of New Jersey sit on different geology with different naturally occurring risks. Second, the law is fundamentally a testing and disclosure requirement. It does not order a seller to install a treatment system or hand over a credit. What happens after a result comes back high is a negotiation, and that negotiation happens inside your contract, not inside the statute. Your attorney is the right person to confirm the current requirements for your specific county and situation.

What the Lab Is Actually Looking For

A private well is untreated groundwater. Nobody is monitoring it daily on your behalf, so the test is looking at a handful of categories:

  • Bacteria. Total coliform and E. coli indicate whether surface contamination is reaching the well — often through a damaged well cap, a cracked casing, or a well set too low in the yard.
  • Arsenic. This is the one that surprises New Jersey sellers most. Arsenic occurs naturally in certain bedrock formations across parts of central and northwestern New Jersey. It has nothing to do with how well you have maintained the house. It is geology.
  • Nitrates. Frequently associated with septic systems, agricultural runoff, or fertilizer reaching the aquifer.
  • Lead. Usually a plumbing issue rather than a groundwater one, but it shows up in the results either way.
  • Volatile organic compounds. Relevant near historic industrial or commercial sites, of which New Jersey has plenty.
  • Iron, manganese, and hardness. These are aesthetic and nuisance issues rather than health thresholds, but they are the reason a house has orange staining in the tubs or a persistent metallic taste.

Radon in water and emerging contaminants such as PFAS come up in some transactions as well, depending on the area and how thorough the buyer wants to be.

When a Result Comes Back High

Here is the part worth internalizing: an unfavorable well result is rarely a deal killer on its own. Most of the common issues have well-established, well-priced remedies. Bacteria is often addressed with shock chlorination and repairing whatever let the contamination in. Arsenic is typically handled with a treatment system. Water softeners and filtration handle iron and hardness.

What actually kills deals is timing. A result arrives four days before a scheduled closing, nobody has a quote, the buyer's lender gets nervous, and now two families with moving trucks booked are negotiating a system they have not priced. The problem was never the arsenic. It was the calendar.

Something else worth knowing: many well homes are also on septic. If the well test surfaces bacteria or nitrates, buyers reasonably start asking about the septic system too. Those two conversations tend to arrive together, so it helps to be ready for both.

If You Are on Public Water, You Are Not Entirely Off the Hook

Homes on municipal water are not subject to the private well requirements, but water still comes up. Buyers ask about lead service lines — New Jersey has pushed water systems to inventory and replace them, and buyers in older neighborhoods have gotten noticeably more informed about it. Your utility publishes an annual water quality report, and there is no downside to knowing what it says about your system before a buyer reads it first.

Keeping This Off the Critical Path

The fix is unglamorous: move the test to the front of the process. Sampling, lab work, and a final written report often take a couple of weeks end to end, and longer if a retest is needed. That is a comfortable window if you start when you list, and a genuine emergency if you start after attorney review.

If you are selling, test early. If a result needs attention, you will have priced the remedy and can present it as a solved problem rather than a discovered defect. Sellers who hand a buyer a clean file tend to hold their price better than sellers who hand a buyer a surprise.

If you are buying, read the actual report rather than the summary line, and ask what treatment equipment is already in the house. A softener or filtration system that nobody has serviced in six years is not the same as a working one.

A Few Questions Worth Sitting With

What is your timeline actually built around — a school year, a job, a family situation? That shapes how much slack you have if a result needs follow-up. And how much of the house's history do you actually have on paper right now? Owners are often surprised by how little documentation they have on a well they have relied on for twenty years.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to test my well before selling my house in New Jersey?

New Jersey's Private Well Testing Act applies to real estate transactions involving homes served by a private well. The well generally needs to be sampled by a state-certified laboratory and the results provided to the buyer before closing. Your real estate attorney can confirm the exact requirements that apply to your county and transaction.

Who pays for the well water test — the buyer or the seller?

There is no single rule. It is a negotiated term in the contract, and practice varies by region and by deal. What matters more is that someone orders it early enough that the results are back well before the closing date.

What happens if my well water fails the test?

In most cases it is a solvable problem rather than a deal breaker. Bacteria is often addressed by disinfecting the well and repairing whatever allowed contamination in; arsenic and other contaminants are typically handled with treatment equipment. Because the law centers on testing and disclosure rather than mandating a specific repair, how the cost is handled is worked out between buyer and seller.

Why is arsenic such a common issue in New Jersey well water?

Arsenic occurs naturally in certain bedrock formations found across parts of central and northwestern New Jersey. It reflects the geology under the property, not how the home has been maintained, which is why it can appear in well-kept houses and surprise longtime owners.

How long does well water testing take?

From sampling to a final written report, it often runs a couple of weeks, and longer if a retest or follow-up sampling is needed. That is why starting when you list rather than after attorney review makes such a difference to your closing timeline.

Does any of this apply if my home is on public water?

The private well testing requirements do not apply to homes on a municipal system. Water still comes up in those transactions, though — buyers increasingly ask about lead service lines in older neighborhoods, and your utility publishes an annual water quality report worth reading before a buyer does.
