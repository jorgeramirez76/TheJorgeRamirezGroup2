6 House Sounds NJ Buyers and Sellers Can't Ignore

A quiet house and a well-maintained house are not the same thing. But when your home talks, it's telling you something — and in a real estate transaction, sounds become inspection findings.

Sounds Are Data

I've walked into a lot of houses over 15 years. The ones that need attention usually announce themselves before you reach the second floor. A groan from the pipes when a faucet runs. A scurrying sound in the wall when the house is quiet. A click-click-click from the electrical panel every time the dishwasher kicks on.

Sellers often tune these sounds out. They've lived with them for years. Buyers — and buyers' agents — hear them fresh. And home inspectors document them in writing.

In NJ's older housing stock — and most of the single-family homes in Summit, Maplewood, Chatham, Cranford, Montclair, and Madison were built between 1920 and 1975 — these sounds carry more weight than in newer construction. Older homes have systems that were installed under very different standards. When they start making noise, it usually means something is wearing out or was never right to begin with.

Here are the six house sounds that I tell every NJ seller to address before listing — and that I tell every NJ buyer to listen for at a showing.

1 Scratching or Scurrying Inside Walls or Ceilings

Repetitive scratching, scurrying, or soft thumping inside walls, ceilings, or above the first-floor — particularly after dark — almost always means rodents. Mice, rats, squirrels, or in some cases raccoons nesting in attic spaces. In NJ, gray squirrels are extremely common in older homes; they enter through deteriorated soffit vents and gable vents and nest in attic insulation.

This is not just a nuisance. Rodents chew electrical wiring. Squirrels in an attic are a documented fire risk in NJ home inspections. Mice leave droppings, urine trails, and nesting material that NJ inspectors are specifically looking for in attics and crawl spaces. Finding any of this under contract means a credit request — sometimes a significant one if electrical damage is found.

If you hear it in your home: hire a licensed wildlife removal and exclusion company before you list. Seal entry points. Have the attic inspected for wiring damage. Document the remediation.

What it costs to fix: $350–$1,200 for exclusion and sealing, plus wiring inspection if needed.

2 Banging or Hammering Pipes When Water Stops or Starts

A sharp bang — sometimes loud enough to shake the walls — when you shut off a faucet or the toilet finishes filling is water hammer. It's a pressure surge caused by water in motion stopping abruptly. It's more common in homes with older galvanized steel supply lines (the standard in NJ homes built before 1970) and in houses with high water pressure.

Water hammer is flagged in NJ home inspections. It's not catastrophic, but left unaddressed it stresses pipe connections over time and can cause joints to loosen or fittings to fail. In an older home with galvanized pipe, it's also a signal that buyers will note: this is original plumbing.

Water hammer arrestors (installed on supply lines near the affected fixtures) cost $15–$25 each and are a straightforward plumber fix. While the plumber is there, have them assess the water pressure — above 80 PSI requires a pressure-reducing valve, which is also a standard inspection callout in NJ.

What it costs to fix: $150–$400 for water hammer arrestors plus labor.

3 Clicking, Buzzing, or Humming From the Electrical Panel

Your electrical panel should be silent. A circuit breaker making a clicking, buzzing, or humming sound is trying to tell you something is wrong. It's either a breaker that's failing to hold its position, a panel with loose connections generating heat, or — in the case of Federal Pacific Electric (FPE) Stab-Lok panels and Zinsco panels — a design that has been documented as a fire risk.

FPE and Zinsco panels were installed in enormous numbers of NJ homes from the 1950s through the 1980s. Both have documented failure rates: the breakers can fail to trip when overloaded, meaning the circuit stays live during a fault condition that should have cut the power. NJ home inspectors flag them by brand name. Buyers routinely request panel replacement as a condition of closing.

If your panel is FPE or Zinsco, get an electrical evaluation before you list. Replacement runs $2,500–$4,500 for a standard 200-amp service upgrade, more for a 400-amp service or homes with complex wiring. Addressing it proactively removes a major buyer negotiation point.

What it costs to fix: $2,500–$4,500 for panel replacement; $150–$400 for individual breaker repair if the panel is acceptable.

4 HVAC Rattling, Banging, or Persistent Clicking at Startup

Your furnace or air handler should start up, run, and shut down without drama. A rattle on startup can be a loose panel or filter misalignment — easy fixes. A persistent banging or clunking when the blower runs is a different story: it could indicate a failing blower motor, a cracked heat exchanger (serious), or ductwork that has come loose.

A cracked heat exchanger in a gas furnace is a carbon monoxide risk. NJ home inspectors routinely call for combustion analysis and heat exchanger inspection in older furnaces. If your HVAC is more than 15 years old and making noises it didn't used to make, schedule a furnace inspection before listing. A tune-up and inspection costs $100–$200. Discovering a cracked heat exchanger under contract is a $3,000–$6,000 problem.

Also: persistent short cycling (the system turns on and off rapidly without completing a full heating or cooling cycle) is a red flag that inspectors note. It typically indicates a sizing mismatch, a refrigerant issue, or a control problem. Each warrants professional diagnosis before listing.

What it costs to fix: $100–$200 for inspection and tune-up; $800–$3,000 for component repairs; $5,000–$12,000 for full system replacement.

5 Gurgling Drains or Bubbling Toilets

When water drains from a sink or tub and you hear gurgling — a bubbling, sucking sound — from the same drain or from a nearby toilet, that's a drain venting problem. Drain systems need air to flow smoothly; the vent pipes that run up through the roof allow air in so water can flow out without creating vacuum. When a vent is blocked (leaves, birds' nests, ice in NJ winters), water draining elsewhere pulls air backward through the nearest drain trap.

In NJ's older homes, cast iron drain lines are common. Cast iron lasts 50–75 years; the oldest installations in 1940s and 1950s NJ homes are reaching or past their service life. A gurgling drain in an older NJ home is worth a camera inspection of the sewer line before listing. A sewer line with deteriorated cast iron that collapses under contract becomes a $8,000–$20,000 problem depending on depth and length.

Roof vent blockage is a $150 fix. Sewer line repair is not. Know which one you're dealing with before the inspector's camera tells the story for you.

What it costs to fix: $150–$400 for vent clearing; $400–$800 for sewer camera inspection; $6,000–$20,000 for sewer line replacement if needed.

6 Cracking, Popping, or Creaking From Walls and Ceilings

Some settling sounds in a house are normal, especially seasonal expansion and contraction in wood-framed structures. But cracking or popping sounds from walls — particularly if they're new, louder than they used to be, or accompanied by new cracks in plaster or drywall — are worth paying attention to.

In NJ, freeze-thaw cycles put significant stress on foundations and masonry. Repeated expansion and contraction of saturated soil against foundation walls can cause step-cracking in block or brick foundations and hairline cracks in poured concrete. Sounds from the structure itself — a popping in a floor joist, cracking near a wall corner — can indicate moisture-related wood movement or, in older homes, framing that has shifted beyond normal settling.

Cosmetic cracks in plaster are expected in NJ's older homes. Diagonal cracks at window and door corners, wide cracks (more than 1/4 inch), or cracks that reappear after patching are what NJ inspectors flag as potential structural indicators. If you're hearing sounds and seeing cracks you haven't seen before, a structural engineer consultation ($300–$600) before listing gives you an answer before a buyer's inspector raises the question.

What it costs to assess: $300–$600 for structural engineer consultation; repair costs range from minimal to significant depending on findings.

What NJ Buyers Should Do at Showings

When you're touring a home, turn on every faucet and listen when you shut it off. Flush every toilet and listen to the pipes. Ask the seller's agent to run the HVAC and stand near the unit. Open the electrical panel and look at the brand (FPE, Zinsco, and Pushmatic panels are all on buyers' watchlists in NJ). Pause in each room and listen.

A busy Saturday showing with multiple groups touring at once is not ideal for this. Schedule a second look when the house is quiet if you're seriously interested. Your home inspector will check all of these systems, but you want to go in with your own observations documented so you can ask targeted questions during the inspection.

Sounds are information. The sellers have lived with them long enough not to notice. You're hearing them fresh — use that.

Frequently Asked Questions: House Sounds and NJ Inspections

What house sounds should NJ sellers fix before listing?

Prioritize anything an inspector will document: banging pipes when water stops (water hammer), scratching in walls or ceilings (rodents/wildlife), clicking or buzzing at the electrical panel (particularly FPE or Zinsco panels), HVAC noises that persist beyond startup, and gurgling drains (venting or sewer line issue). Each generates a written inspection callout that buyers use as negotiating leverage. Fixing them proactively is almost always cheaper than the credit a buyer will request.

Are Federal Pacific electrical panels common in NJ homes?

Yes — very. FPE Stab-Lok panels were standard in NJ new construction from the late 1950s through the early 1980s. If your NJ home was built in that window and has never had the panel replaced, there's a real chance it's FPE. Open the panel door and look at the brand name on the breakers and the inside of the door. If you see "Stab-Lok" or "Federal Pacific," schedule an electrical evaluation before you list. NJ inspectors flag them consistently, and buyers often make panel replacement a condition of sale.

Should NJ buyers be listening for sounds at a showing?

Absolutely. Turn on faucets and listen when they shut off (water hammer). Listen to the walls when the house is quiet (scratching/scurrying). Ask to run the HVAC and stand near the unit. Pause in the basement and listen to the pipes. A busy showing isn't ideal — consider scheduling a second visit when the house is quiet if you're seriously interested. Your inspector will document everything, but going in with your own observations makes your inspection more targeted.

How common are plumbing issues in NJ homes built before 1970?

Extremely common. Pre-1970 NJ homes routinely have galvanized steel supply lines (which rust internally, reducing water pressure and potentially leaching iron and lead into the water), cast iron drain lines (which are nearing or past their service life), and water heaters that have never been replaced. The galvanized supply line issue is the most frequently flagged plumbing item in NJ inspections of older homes. Full replacement runs $6,000–$15,000 and is a common buyer credit request in the sub-$800K market.
