Buying a House With a Pool in New Jersey: What to Check Before You Write the Offer

Last updated  ·  August 2026

Quick answer: A pool in New Jersey is usable for only part of the year, so the decision comes down to how much you will genuinely use it against the year-round cost and liability. Before you offer, confirm the pool was permitted, get it inspected separately from the house, and call your insurer.

By Jorge Ramirez | August 2026

This question comes up every August, right when a backyard pool looks its most convincing. A family walks a listing on a warm Saturday, the water is blue, the deck is dry, and by the time they get back to the car they have already pictured three summers there.

I am not going to talk anyone out of that. Plenty of my clients love their pools and would not trade them. But a pool is one of the few features where the emotional reaction at the showing and the practical reality of ownership can drift a long way apart, and New Jersey has some specifics worth knowing before you write the offer.

Start with the season, not the pool

Our swim season is genuinely short. Realistically you are looking at late spring through early fall, and the shoulder weeks on either end depend entirely on the weather and whether the pool is heated. Everything else — the opening, the closing, the chemicals, the covering, the repairs — happens across the full twelve months.

So the first honest question is not whether you like pools. It is how many days a year you will actually be in it. Families with young kids who are home all summer tend to get real use out of one. Two working adults who travel in August often find it becomes something they maintain rather than something they enjoy.

What does your summer usually look like? That answer tells you more about whether this is the right house than anything the listing says.

In-ground and above-ground are different decisions

An in-ground pool is part of the property. It is a permanent improvement, it generally requires permits, it may affect how the property is assessed, and removing it later is a real project with real cost.

An above-ground pool is much closer to a large piece of equipment. It is cheaper to walk away from, it typically does not carry the same permanent value, and in many cases the sellers may be willing to take it with them or remove it as part of the negotiation. If an above-ground pool is the only thing giving you pause about an otherwise great house, that is often a solvable problem rather than a dealbreaker.

Confirm the pool was permitted

This is the item I would put at the top of the list, because it is the one that quietly derails closings.

Pools, pool barriers, and the decking around them generally require permits, and New Jersey enforces barrier and gate requirements through the construction code. Requirements and enforcement vary from town to town, so the reliable move is to have your agent or attorney check what is actually on file with the municipal construction office — permits pulled, inspections passed, final approvals issued.

Unpermitted pools and unpermitted decks around them are more common than you would think, particularly with work done years ago by a previous owner. Discovering it during attorney review is inconvenient. Discovering it after closing means you have inherited the problem, and bringing it into compliance is on you.

Call your insurance agent before you go under contract

Most buyers wait until they are already committed to shop homeowners insurance. With a pool I would flip that order and make one call early.

Carriers ask about pools. Many want to see a compliant fence and a self-latching gate. Some will ask you to carry higher liability limits, and a fair number have their own rules about diving boards and slides — occasionally declining to write the policy at all with certain features in place. None of this is necessarily expensive, but it is much better learned in week one than in week four.

Have the pool inspected separately

A standard home inspection is not a pool inspection. Most general inspectors will note the obvious and specifically exclude the pool system from their scope, which is entirely reasonable — it is a specialty.

Hire someone who does pools and have them look at the shell or liner, the pump, the filter, the heater if there is one, the plumbing and any visible leaks, the electrical bonding and grounding, and the condition of the deck and coping. Ask about the age of each major component, because a heater or liner nearing the end of its life is a meaningful number that belongs in your budget.

If the pool is closed for the season when you are shopping, say so early. An inspection on a covered, winterized pool is limited, and you may want to negotiate for a credit or a post-closing inspection contingency instead of accepting the unknown.

The costs that continue every year

Without inventing numbers for your specific situation, here is what to actually price out with a local pool company before you commit: professional opening and closing each season, chemicals and testing, the electricity to run the pump, heating if applicable, a safety cover, and a reserve for repairs. Liners, heaters, pumps, and filters all have finite lifespans, and they do not fail at convenient moments.

The other cost is time. Even with a service, a pool asks something of you every week during the season. Some people find that satisfying. Others find it is one more thing.

What a pool does when you eventually sell

Here is the part I try to be straight with buyers about. A pool rarely returns its full installation cost, and more importantly, it narrows your buyer pool rather than widening it.

Some buyers will pay a premium and search specifically for a pool. Others — families with toddlers, buyers who do not want the upkeep, people who simply want the yard — will screen the house out or mentally deduct for it. Neither group is wrong. It just means that when your turn comes to sell, you are marketing to a narrower slice of the market than the house next door.

That is not an argument against buying it. It is an argument for buying it because you want the pool, not because you think it is an investment.

Questions worth asking the seller

  • How old is the pool, and who installed it?
  • Has it been opened and closed professionally, and by whom?
  • When were the liner, heater, pump, and filter last replaced?
  • Are there maintenance records or service invoices?
  • Were permits pulled for the pool, the barrier, and the surrounding deck?
  • Have there been leaks, structural repairs, or insurance claims?
  • What does a typical season cost to run?

Sellers who have taken care of a pool are usually glad to answer all of this. Hesitation on the permit question in particular is worth paying attention to.

So should you buy the house with the pool?

If you have thought honestly about how much you will use it, verified the permits, priced the upkeep, and talked to your insurer — and you still want it — then yes, buy the house and enjoy it. The buyers who regret a pool are almost always the ones who bought the feeling at the showing without doing any of that.

If you are somewhere in the middle on a specific house right now, I am glad to walk through it with you. What is the part you keep going back and forth on? That is usually where the real answer is hiding.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a swimming pool add value to a home in New Jersey?

Sometimes, but rarely its full installation cost. New Jersey's swim season is relatively short, so a pool tends to narrow the group of interested buyers rather than widen it. Some buyers pay a premium for one, while others deduct for the maintenance and liability. Buy a pool because you want to use it, not as an investment.

Do I need a permit for a pool in New Jersey?

Pools, barriers, and surrounding decks generally require permits, and barrier and gate requirements are enforced through the state construction code. Specifics and enforcement vary by municipality. Before closing, have your agent or attorney confirm with the local construction office that permits were pulled and final approvals were issued.

Does a standard home inspection cover the pool?

Usually not. Most general home inspectors specifically exclude pool systems from their scope. Hire a pool specialist to inspect the shell or liner, pump, filter, heater, plumbing, electrical bonding, and deck condition, and to give you the age and remaining life of the major components.

How does a pool affect homeowners insurance in New Jersey?

Carriers will ask about it. Many require a compliant fence and self-latching gate, some ask for higher liability limits, and a number have restrictions on diving boards and slides. Call your insurance agent before you go under contract rather than after, so there are no surprises during your attorney review period.

Can I buy a home in New Jersey and have the pool removed?

Yes, though the difficulty depends on the type. An above-ground pool is often removable and can sometimes be negotiated into the contract for the seller to take or remove. Removing an in-ground pool is a real project involving demolition, fill, compaction, and usually permits, so price it out before assuming it is a simple fix.
