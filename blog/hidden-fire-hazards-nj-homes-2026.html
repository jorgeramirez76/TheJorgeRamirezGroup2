8 Hidden Fire Hazards in NJ Homes That Buyers and Sellers Both Miss

Published  ·  July 2026

These aren't theoretical fire risks. They're the things NJ home inspectors document every week — and when they show up in an inspection report, sellers lose negotiating leverage fast. Here's what to look for and fix before they become someone else's problem.

Why Fire Hazards Matter More Than You Think at Sale Time

In fifteen years of selling homes across Union, Essex, Morris, and Middlesex County, I've sat through a lot of inspection reports. The items that generate the most buyer anxiety — and the most aggressive credit requests — are rarely the big structural things. They're the small, overlooked hazards that signal to a buyer that the home hasn't been maintained carefully. Fire-related items top the list.

The reason is simple: buyers can live with a dated kitchen. They cannot live with a dryer duct that's been packed with lint for five years. When an inspector flags a fire hazard, it changes the tone of the entire negotiation. Suddenly buyers are thinking about safety, not features. And in that headspace, they negotiate harder and demand more.

The good news: most of these hazards cost almost nothing to fix. A dryer duct cleaning is $150. Proper battery storage is free. New bulbs for a closet fixture cost $12. The return on addressing these items before listing is not subtle — it's the difference between a clean inspection and a $5,000 credit conversation.

Here are the eight hidden fire hazards I see most commonly in NJ homes — whether you're getting ready to list, buying your next home, or just doing a responsible safety check on the house you're already in.

The 8 Hidden Fire Hazards

1 Overloaded Outlets in the Kitchen

Coffee maker, toaster, air fryer, and an Instant Pot all running off the same counter outlet through a power strip. That's a real fire risk, and it's extremely common in NJ kitchens that were built before modern small appliance standards. Each of those appliances pulls significant amperage. Combine them on a single circuit and you've got a recipe for a tripped breaker at best, an electrical fire at worst.

The Fix:

Distribute your appliances across different circuits. If your kitchen has only one counter outlet run (common in older Colonials), have an electrician add a dedicated circuit. It's usually a half-day job and typically costs $200–$400.

2 Grease Buildup Around the Stovetop

Most people clean the stovetop surface. Almost nobody cleans the grease that accumulates on the hood vent filter, the back wall behind the burners, and the underside of the range hood. In NJ's older kitchens with open flame gas ranges, that accumulated grease is fuel waiting for a flare-up. Inspectors look at range hoods. Buyers' agents walk through with their eyes open. A filthy hood tells a story.

The Fix:

Deep clean the hood, replace the grease filter if it's dark gray or black, and wipe down all surfaces behind and above the range. Takes about an hour and costs under $20 in supplies.

3 Lint-Packed Dryer Exhaust Ducts

This is the one that surprises most sellers. You clean the lint trap — everybody does. But lint also accumulates deep inside the exhaust duct, especially where it bends or runs a long distance to the exterior vent. That interior buildup is where most dryer fires actually start. In NJ's older ranch homes and split-levels, the laundry is often in a basement utility room with a long exhaust run. Those ducts can go years without a proper cleaning.

The Fix:

Have your dryer duct professionally cleaned once a year if you do multiple loads per week. Cost: $100–$175. A secondary indicator that your duct needs attention: if your dryer takes two cycles to fully dry a load, the exhaust is probably restricted.

4 Space Heaters Too Close to Flammables

Portable space heaters are everywhere in NJ homes, especially in older houses with drafty rooms or unfinished basements. They're also a leading cause of residential fires when left unattended or placed too close to curtains, area rugs, upholstered furniture, or stored clothing. The minimum clearance is three feet on all sides. Most people don't come close to that.

The Fix:

Before you list, remove any space heaters from areas where they've been stored too close to flammables. If you have a finished basement with a space heater, create obvious clearance. Inspectors look at where these are stored, not just where they're used.

5 Exposed Bulbs in Closets

This is particularly common in NJ's older home stock — bare incandescent or CFL bulbs in closet fixtures, tucked right next to clothing, cardboard boxes, and linens. Exposed bulbs generate significant heat. When a cardboard box full of old tax records is stacked six inches from a bare 60-watt bulb, you've got a legitimate hazard. Inspectors flag it. Buyers notice it.

The Fix:

Replace bare bulb closet fixtures with enclosed LED fixtures. Cost: $15–$40 per fixture. LEDs also run much cooler than incandescent bulbs, which eliminates the hazard almost entirely even in close quarters.

6 9-Volt Batteries in Junk Drawers

This one gets overlooked because it sounds trivial. A 9-volt battery rattling loose in a junk drawer alongside keys, paper clips, coins, and other metal objects can short-circuit on contact and generate enough heat to start a fire. It happens. The positive and negative terminals on a 9-volt battery are right next to each other at the top, which makes them easy to accidentally bridge with a metal object.

The Fix:

Store 9-volt batteries in their original packaging or cover the terminals with electrical tape. Takes 30 seconds. Costs nothing. Not something an inspector will flag, but worth knowing before you hand the house over to a buyer.

7 Combustibles Near Gas Water Heaters

The area around a gas water heater needs to be clear of combustible materials. That means no stored boxes, no extra clothing, no cleaning supplies, no paper products. Gas water heaters have open-flame burners that cycle on and off. Anything combustible within proximity is a real risk when the burner kicks on. In NJ homes where the utility area doubles as storage, this is a very common inspection flag.

The Fix:

Clear a 24-inch minimum zone around your gas water heater before the inspector comes — or before a buyer's walkthrough. Move storage to shelving units on the opposite wall or to another area of the basement entirely.

8 Overgrown Vegetation Against the Home Exterior

NJ sellers spend a lot of time on curb appeal. Mulch, landscaping, flowering bushes against the foundation — it looks great. The problem is when those plants become dense enough to accumulate dry material and sit directly against the siding. In a drought year or late-summer dry spell, that's fuel. Additionally, dense overgrowth near vents and soffits can trap moisture and attract pests, which creates a different kind of structural problem. Most fire marshals recommend an 18-inch non-combustible zone directly against the foundation.

The Fix:

Trim any plantings back from direct contact with the siding. Clear accumulated dry leaves and debris from foundation plantings. Use stone or gravel mulch nearest to the house rather than wood chips.

The Real Estate Angle: Why Sellers Should Fix These First

Here's the math I've seen play out dozens of times: a seller doesn't address known issues before listing, the buyer's inspector finds five items in the report, and the buyer comes back asking for a $7,500 credit. Three of those items are fire-hazard related and cost $400 combined to fix. The seller now negotiates from a defensive position, the buyer has documentation, and the credit ends up at $4,500 because the seller can't argue against a safety concern flagged in writing.

Compare that to the seller who spends two weekends addressing the obvious stuff before listing. The inspection still happens — it always does — but the report comes back with minor items. No leverage. Clean negotiation. The seller controls the conversation.

If you're thinking about listing your NJ home in the next 6–12 months, a pre-listing inspection is one of the smartest $400 you'll spend. I recommend them to almost every seller I work with. You find out what the buyer will find before the buyer finds it. And you have the option to fix, disclose, or price accordingly — on your timeline, not under contract pressure.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do fire hazards affect home sales in NJ?

Yes, directly. When NJ home inspectors document fire hazards, buyers use the report as negotiating leverage to request credits or price reductions. Most fire hazards cost $50–$500 to fix before listing. Leaving them for the inspection typically costs sellers $2,000–$7,500 in post-inspection negotiations.

What fire hazards do NJ home inspectors most commonly flag?

The most common flags are: overloaded electrical outlets, grease buildup around stovetops, lint-clogged dryer exhaust ducts, improper space heater placement, exposed bulbs near flammable storage, combustibles near gas appliances, and overgrown vegetation against the home exterior. Every one of these is fixable before listing for under $500 total.

Can fire hazards affect my homeowners insurance in NJ?

Yes. NJ homeowners insurance underwriters assess fire risk as part of policy pricing. Buyers who discover fire hazards during inspection may also renegotiate price or request a seller credit. Address these before listing to keep both the inspection and the insurance transition clean.

How often should I have my dryer duct cleaned in NJ?

Annually if you do multiple loads per week. In NJ's older housing stock, ducts often run long or make sharp turns, so lint accumulates faster. A blocked duct is also the leading reason dryers take two full cycles to dry a single load — if that's happening in your home, get the duct cleaned before you dismiss it as a dryer problem.

Should I fix fire hazards before selling my NJ home?

Absolutely. Addressing these before listing removes buyer negotiating leverage and signals responsible maintenance. Most fire hazards cost $50–$500 to fix. Leaving them for the inspector to find typically costs sellers far more in post-inspection credits and price reductions — and occasionally kills deals entirely.
