Dents in Your Hardwood Floors? What NJ Sellers Should Fix, Leave Alone and Disclose Before Listing

House Outlook linked an article this week on fixing dents in hardwood floors, with the honest admission that no number of rugs or felt pads prevents them. True. Every colonial in Montclair, Maplewood and Cranford with original oak has dents, and buyers expect some. What they do not expect is to wonder whether a dent is a dent or a soft spot. Here is how I sort floors before a listing, and what a buyer should look for while the seller’s rugs are still down.

The House Outlook fix, and where it works

The repair most articles describe for a dent in solid hardwood is steam. A dent is wood fiber that has been compressed, not removed, and a damp cloth under a hot iron for a few seconds swells the fibers back toward level. It works best on solid wood with an oil or wax finish, or on a floor you are about to refinish anyway. On a polyurethane finish, the heat and moisture can cloud or blister the coating, so the dent goes away and a white patch takes its place. Test in a closet first. On engineered flooring, where the real wood is a thin veneer over plywood, do not try it at all.

For a gouge, where fiber is actually missing, steam does nothing. Those take a color-matched wax fill stick or wood filler, and in a listing I would rather see a clean filled gouge than a gouge with a rug over it.

Sorting the floor before you list

Walk every room in daylight with the rugs rolled back and a notepad. You are putting each mark in one of four columns.

  1. Leave it. Shallow dents and the general patina of a 1920s oak floor in Westfield or Summit. Buyers in those towns are paying for character; the old house details post explains why sanding it to new can actually cost you.
  2. Fix it yourself. A handful of deeper dents in a traffic path, a gouge from a dropped pan, a scratch from a chair leg. Steam, fill or touch-up marker, one afternoon, done before the photographer comes.
  3. Screen and recoat. If the dents are fine but the finish is dull, scuffed and worn thin in front of the sink and the back door, a floor contractor can abrade the top coat and lay down a fresh one in a day without sanding to bare wood. This is the most cost-effective floor work I see sellers do, and the one most skip.
  4. Full refinish. Boards worn gray, pet stains that have gone black, finish worn through to bare wood, or a previous owner’s orange stain in a house full of buyers who want natural. A full sand is a real cost and days out of the house, so it only makes sense when the floor is the thing holding the price down.

When a dent is not a dent

This is the part the home articles leave out. Press on the mark with your heel. If the board gives, if it is spongy, if the boards around it are cupped or lifting, that is not a dent. That is water, from a radiator, a dishwasher, a plant, or a window that leaked for years before you bought the house. In New Jersey that becomes a disclosure conversation, not a cosmetic one, and the disclosure guide explains how it is handled. Have a flooring contractor open it up and find the source. A buyer’s inspector will find the soft spot in five minutes, and a soft spot discovered by the inspector costs more in negotiation than one you addressed and documented.

The same goes for a line of dents along a wall that turns out to be a sag, or boards that have separated in winter and closed in summer. Movement is a structure question before it is a floor question, and the foundation warning signs post covers what to look for.

Jorge’s tip: Do not buy new rugs to cover the floor you are worried about. Buyers in Morristown and Millburn lift the corner of every rug that looks like it was placed yesterday. If the floor under it is fine, you have spent money hiding an asset. If it is not, you have taught the buyer not to trust the rest of the house. Fix or disclose, then let the floor show.

For buyers: reading floors at a showing

Look along the floor toward the window, not down at it; low light shows dents, cupping and finish wear that overhead light hides. Lift rug corners. Press near radiators, under the dishwasher line and at exterior doors. Ask whether the floors are solid or engineered and when they were last refinished, because an engineered floor may only have one more sanding in it, or none. Then hand the question to your inspector, and if the house has the soft spots I described, ask for a flooring contractor’s opinion before the inspection contingency runs out.

What this does for the sale

A floor with a few honest dents and a fresh coat of finish reads as a cared-for house. A floor hidden under new rugs reads as a question. The money is in knowing which of your marks belong in which column before a buyer does the sorting for you, and that is a conversation worth having before the pre-listing inspection, not after the offer.

Questions about hardwood floors and selling a NJ home

Does the steam-and-iron trick work on finished hardwood floors?

It works on solid wood with an oil or wax finish, or on a floor about to be refinished. On polyurethane the heat and moisture can cloud or blister the coating, leaving a white patch where the dent was. Test in a closet first, and never try it on engineered flooring, where the wood layer is a thin veneer.

Should I refinish my hardwood floors before selling in New Jersey?

Only when the floor is holding the price down: boards worn gray, black pet stains, finish worn through, or a dated stain color. If the dents are minor but the finish is dull, a screen and recoat refreshes the top coat in a day for far less. A floor with normal wear and a sound finish usually needs nothing.

Do I have to disclose dents and scratches in the floors?

Ordinary cosmetic wear is not a defect. Soft spots, cupping, lifting boards or stains from a past leak are a different matter, because they point to water or movement, and those belong in your disclosure conversation with your agent and attorney. Have the source identified and documented rather than covered with a rug.

Will buyers really look under my rugs?

Yes, especially a rug that looks newly placed. Buyers and inspectors lift corners, and a hidden problem costs more in negotiation than one you addressed up front. If the floor under the rug is fine, you have hidden an asset; if it is not, you have given the buyer a reason to doubt the rest of the house.

What should a buyer check about hardwood floors at a NJ showing?

Sight along the floor toward the window to catch dents and cupping, lift rug corners, and press near radiators, exterior doors and the dishwasher. Ask whether the floors are solid or engineered and when they were last refinished. Then have your inspector evaluate any soft spots before the contingency period ends.
