6 Outdated Laundry Habits That Are Damaging Your Washer — And Your NJ Home's Value

These habits are aging your appliances faster than they should. When you go to sell, NJ buyers notice.

Why the Laundry Room Matters More Than Sellers Think

I have walked through thousands of NJ homes over fifteen years. The laundry room is one of the most overlooked spaces by sellers and one of the most carefully examined by buyers and inspectors.

A buyer walks into your laundry room and smells something musty coming from the washer drum. They open the door and see a gray film on the rubber gasket. Right then, the conversation shifts from "I love this house" to "what else hasn't been taken care of?" That leap — from a smelly washer to deferred maintenance throughout — happens fast and it costs sellers money at the offer stage.

Here's the thing: the washer and dryer in most NJ homes are not old. They're just being used the wrong way. These six habits are aging your appliances faster than they should and creating exactly the kind of impression you don't want buyers walking away with.

The 6 Habits to Quit Now

Fix Before You List

1. Overloading the Drum

Packing the washer as full as it will go feels efficient. It's not. When the drum is stuffed, clothes don't move freely, which means they don't get properly cleaned or rinsed. Detergent residue stays in the fabric. More critically, the overloaded drum puts constant strain on the motor, bearings, and suspension. Over time, you get a machine that vibrates, bangs against the wall during the spin cycle, and leaves laundry smelling like it needs to be washed again.

Before listing, run a few maintenance cycles and listen for grinding, vibration, or uneven spinning. A washer that rattles and shakes during a buyer walkthrough is a red flag.

The fix: Fill the drum about three-quarters full. Smaller, more frequent loads are easier on the machine and get clothes cleaner.

Fix Before You List

2. Using Too Much Detergent

More soap does not mean cleaner clothes. Modern high-efficiency washers — front-loaders and most top-loaders sold in the last decade — are designed to work with very small amounts of detergent. The recommended amount for a full load in most HE machines is about two teaspoons of liquid or one pod. Most people use three to four times that amount.

The result is a soap residue buildup inside the drum, on the gasket, and in the dispenser drawer. That residue grows mold. It also redeposits onto your clothes during subsequent wash cycles — which is why clothes smell clean coming out of the dryer but develop an odd odor after a few hours of wear.

When a buyer or inspector opens your washer, residue buildup is visible. It communicates one thing: this machine has not been properly maintained.

The fix: Use HE-rated detergent in the amount specified on the label for your load size. Less than you think.

Fix Before You List

3. Using Chlorine Bleach on Everything

Chlorine bleach has one job: whitening. That's it. It does not disinfect effectively at normal household concentrations, it doesn't deodorize better than alternatives, and it causes serious damage to anything that isn't 100% cotton white. Synthetic fabrics — polyester, spandex, nylon — react with chlorine bleach to produce permanent yellowing. It also degrades rubber components inside the washer over years of use, which shows up as cracked drum seals and gaskets that hold odors even after cleaning.

Oxygen bleach (sodium percarbonate, sold as OxiClean or similar) does everything most households need it to do — whitens, brightens, removes organic stains — without the corrosive side effects.

The fix: Reserve chlorine bleach for white cotton only, used rarely. Switch to oxygen bleach for everything else.

Fix Before You List

4. Leaving Wet Clothes in the Washer

Everyone does it. You start a load, get busy, come back hours later. The clothes have been sitting wet in a sealed drum. You can smell it before you even open the door.

A damp, sealed washer drum is a mold and mildew factory. The odor transfers into the fabric, and if you've been doing this for years, it's also transferring into the drum walls and rubber seals. That's why some washers smell bad even after a cleaning cycle — the mold is embedded in the gasket.

In NJ, a musty washer smell during a showing is memorable in the worst way. Buyers remember it walking into the kitchen. They remember it in the backyard. It colors the entire showing.

The fix: Move clothes to the dryer within 30 minutes of the cycle ending. Leave the washer door cracked open after every use so the drum dries out between loads.

Fix Before You List

5. Never Running a Drum Cleaning Cycle

Your washer needs to be washed. This is the part nobody thinks about. Detergent residue, fabric softener buildup, hard water mineral deposits, and organic material from clothes accumulate inside the drum over months and years. That buildup is what causes the smell and the gray film buyers see when they open the door.

Modern washers have a dedicated "Drum Clean" or "Tub Clean" cycle. If yours has it, run it monthly with a washer cleaner tablet or two cups of white vinegar and a half cup of baking soda. If it doesn't have a dedicated cycle, run the hottest water cycle empty with the same cleaning agents.

On front-loaders specifically: the rubber door gasket is where mold hides. Wipe it down manually after every few loads. Pull back the folds of the gasket and look inside. If you see black spots, that is mold and it needs to be addressed before your listing goes live — not after a buyer's inspector finds it.

The fix: Run a drum cleaning cycle now. Then monthly after that. Clean the gasket by hand.

Fix Before You List

6. Overdoing Fabric Softener

Fabric softener works by coating fabric fibers with a thin layer of lubricant. That makes clothes feel soft and reduces static. It also coats the inside of your drum, the agitator, and the drainage pump with the same residue over time. The buildup reduces machine efficiency, creates an environment for mildew, and can eventually clog drainage components.

Dryer sheets have a similar effect on dryer lint screens — the coating is invisible but measurable. Run your lint screen under water. If water beads up and sits on top instead of running through, the screen is coated. That coated lint screen is reducing dryer airflow and increasing dry times, which increases energy use and wear on the heating element.

From a real estate standpoint, a dryer that takes two cycles to dry a load of towels is a problem buyers will notice during a home inspection.

The fix: Use fabric softener sparingly or switch to dryer balls. Clean the lint screen with a brush and warm soapy water periodically to remove coating buildup.

What This Means When You're Selling an NJ Home

Appliance condition is one of those details that gets overlooked in pre-listing prep because sellers focus on the kitchen countertops and the master bathroom. The laundry room gets a quick vacuum and a wipe-down. But buyers and inspectors look at it differently.

A functional, clean laundry room — working appliances, no odors, no visible mold or residue — adds to the picture of a well-maintained home. That picture influences how buyers write their offers. It's not a line item on the appraisal, but it changes the emotional math that drives every real estate transaction.

In the NJ markets I work — Union, Essex, Morris, Middlesex, and Hudson counties — most homes include the washer and dryer in the sale. If you're including yours, make sure they're in a condition you'd be comfortable showing. If they're not, a few cleaning cycles and a change of habits in the weeks before listing can make a measurable difference in how buyers perceive the home overall.

The harder truth: if your washer is genuinely at end-of-life — 12 or 15 years old, vibrating loudly, draining slowly — replacing it before listing may be worth the investment. A new washer runs $700 to $1,100. One credit demand from an inspector referencing a failing appliance can cost more than that.

The Bottom Line for NJ Sellers

Stop overloading the drum. Reduce detergent to what the label actually says. Leave the washer door open after each load. Run a drum cleaning cycle every month. These are 10-minute changes that protect a $900 appliance and prevent a $1,500 credit demand after inspection. More importantly, they protect the perception of your home from the moment a buyer opens that laundry room door.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do buyers check the washer and dryer when buying a NJ home?

Yes. NJ buyers and their inspectors test included appliances during the home inspection. A washer with a mold smell, drum residue, or visible neglect raises questions about how the whole house has been maintained. A well-kept laundry room with clean appliances is a quiet selling point — most sellers underestimate how much it matters.

Does laundry room condition affect home value in New Jersey?

It affects buyer perception, which drives offers. A clean, functional laundry room with properly maintained appliances signals a well-cared-for home. A smelly washer or lint-clogged dryer signals the opposite — and buyers use those signals to justify lower offers or repair credits after inspection.

How do I clean a front-load washer before selling my NJ home?

Run a hot drum cleaning cycle with a washer cleaner tablet or two cups of white vinegar. Wipe the rubber gasket seal thoroughly — that's where mold hides in front-loaders. Leave the door open after the cycle to air dry. Clean the detergent dispenser drawer, which collects residue and grows mold. Start at least two weeks before your listing goes live so the smell has time to fully clear.

Should I leave my washer and dryer when selling my NJ home?

It depends on age and condition. Newer units (under 5 years old) in clean working condition are a selling point — include them and note it in the listing. Older units that are noisy, inefficient, or show wear may be better excluded and negotiated separately. Never leave appliances that have visible mold, drain issues, or performance problems — they become liability items in buyer negotiations.
