7 Home Storage Spots That Concern NJ Buyers and Inspectors

Storage Habits That Cost NJ Sellers Money

Most NJ sellers think about decluttering in terms of what they own too much of — too many clothes, too much furniture, too much stuff in the basement. That's the right instinct. But there's a second layer to this that most sellers miss: it's not just how much you have, it's where you've been storing it.

In my full-time New Jersey real estate work since 2017, I can walk into a basement or garage and predict with reasonable accuracy where the inspection report is going to flag something before the inspector has even arrived. Because certain storage habits are nearly universal in NJ's older housing stock, and inspectors know exactly where to look.

A storage habit that was perfectly harmless while you lived there becomes a negotiating point the moment a buyer's inspector documents it. And in NJ's $700K-$1.5M market, buyers don't walk away from inspection issues — they use them. A single paragraph in an inspection report about electrical panel access or combustibles near the water heater can support a $3,000-$8,000 credit request. Often from a buyer who would have offered the same price anyway if the issue hadn't been documented.

Here are the seven storage locations I clear in every pre-listing walkthrough before we go to market.

The 7 Storage Spots NJ Inspectors Document Every Time

1

In Front of or Surrounding the Electrical Panel

Why inspectors flag it: The National Electrical Code requires a minimum 36 inches of clear working space in front of electrical panels. Boxes, storage shelves, or anything else blocking panel access gets documented every time. In NJ homes with basement utility areas, this is the single most common storage-related inspection note I see — ahead of everything else on this list.
What to do: Clear everything within 36 inches of the front of the panel and 18 inches on either side. Don't just move it three feet away — move it out of the utility area entirely if possible. A clear panel photographs better, inspects better, and signals to buyers that the home has been properly maintained. While you're at it, make sure the panel is labeled. Unlabeled breakers are a separate inspection flag that costs nothing to fix with a $3 label kit and 20 minutes.
2

Within 18 Inches of the Water Heater (Especially Gas)

Why inspectors flag it: Gas water heaters require combustion air and produce a small flame at the burner. Flammables stored near them — paint cans, cardboard boxes, solvents, cleaning products, paper goods — create a documented fire hazard. NJ inspectors note any combustibles within the clearance zone, and this translates directly into buyer concern about safety and maintenance habits.
What to do: Remove all storage from within 18 inches of a gas water heater, and keep flammables (paint, solvents, gasoline for lawn equipment) in a separate detached shed or at minimum on the opposite side of the garage. If your water heater is in a utility closet, the closet should be clear enough that the door opens fully and the unit is visible and accessible. Electric water heaters have less stringent clearance requirements but should still be clear enough for a full inspection.
3

In the Crawl Space

Why inspectors flag it: Crawl spaces in NJ are already moisture-sensitive environments — especially in older homes in Essex, Union, and Middlesex counties where encapsulation is uncommon. Storing items there traps moisture, encourages mold, and provides harborage for pests. More critically: a crawl space packed with boxes is a crawl space that cannot be fully inspected. Buyers and inspectors assume the worst when they can't see the foundation, vapor barrier, and structural elements because they're buried under storage.
What to do: Remove everything from the crawl space before listing. Every box, every old flooring, every tool, every bag of mulch. The crawl space should be empty, with a visible vapor barrier, accessible access hatch, and nothing impeding a full visual inspection. If you discover moisture issues or mold when you clear it out — which happens regularly in NJ homes — address it before listing rather than having it discovered at inspection.

A clear utility area signals maintenance discipline — buyers and inspectors both notice. Photo via Unsplash

4

Under Kitchen and Bathroom Sinks

Why inspectors flag it: The under-sink cabinet is opened at every showing and every inspection. In NJ homes — where drain pipes and supply lines in older homes can have slow weeps, corroded connections, or prior leak staining — a cabinet packed with cleaning products, spare parts, and assorted clutter hides what's back there. Inspectors check for moisture, active leaks, and evidence of prior water damage. Buyers assume that anything hidden under the sink is hidden for a reason.
What to do: Clear under every sink entirely. Remove all cleaning products, spare parts, and storage. Once cleared, check the drain connections and supply lines yourself. Look for moisture staining on the cabinet floor. If there's any discoloration, install a replacement cabinet liner (under $15 at any hardware store). A clean, dry under-sink cabinet with visible, clearly un-leaked plumbing is a small trust signal that adds up across a showing.
5

In the Attic Near Electrical Connections or HVAC Equipment

Why inspectors flag it: Attic storage is common and expected in NJ homes. The problem is when boxes and belongings are stored directly against or over junction boxes, near recessed light cans (which require clearance from insulation), around the HVAC air handler if it's attic-mounted, or in ways that make wiring, insulation, and structural elements inaccessible. Inspectors need to see attic wiring, insulation R-value, and roof sheathing. Storage that blocks any of these becomes a report footnote.
What to do: Don't try to clear the entire attic — reasonable storage on floored sections is expected and fine. Do clear anything within 12 inches of junction boxes, recessed light housings, HVAC equipment, and the perimeter where the roof meets the floor. Leave a clear walking path from the access hatch to all mechanical equipment. And make sure the attic access is itself clear and accessible — an attic hatch blocked by stored items in the hallway closet is a flag that costs you nothing to fix.
6

In Front of the Furnace or HVAC Air Handler

Why inspectors flag it: Like the electrical panel, the furnace and air handler require clear access for service and inspection. Boxes, storage bins, and household items stacked against or in front of furnace units — common in NJ basements where the furnace doubles as a storage wall — prevent inspectors from fully assessing the unit. They will note the obstruction. And buyers who see their inspection report flagging the furnace will ask questions about its service history that you'd rather not have to answer under pressure.
What to do: Clear 24 inches of access in front of all HVAC equipment. Remove storage from directly above the unit. While you're clearing it, check the filter condition and service date sticker. A recently serviced furnace with a current filter and clear access is one of the highest-value, lowest-cost signals of maintenance discipline you can provide to buyers.
7

In Garage Areas That Block the Emergency Egress Path

Why inspectors flag it: NJ building codes and fire safety standards require clear egress from all habitable spaces and attached garages. A garage packed wall to wall with storage — where the path to the side door or the entry door into the home is partially obstructed — creates both an inspection note and a visible safety concern for buyers. In garage fires, seconds matter. Buyers with children notice blocked egress pathways instinctively, even if they can't articulate why the garage made them uncomfortable.
What to do: Ensure clear walking paths to all garage exits: the service door to the outside and the fire door into the house. Both doors should open fully. The fire door between the garage and living space must be a solid-core door with a self-closing hinge — this is a code requirement in NJ and an inspection point. If you have a standard hollow door there, replace it before listing. A solid-core pre-hung fire door costs $150-$300 installed and is far cheaper than the credit a buyer will request when the inspector flags it.

The Connection to Offer Price

I want to be direct about something. These storage issues are, individually, minor. None of them are structural problems. None of them are code violations that will stop a transaction. But they are all things that show up in inspection reports, and inspection reports are the primary tool buyers' agents use to renegotiate after contract — particularly in NJ's $700K-$1.5M range where buyers are sophisticated and their attorneys are actively involved.

A buyer who pays $950,000 for a home in Chatham or Westfield expects the home to have been maintained. When their inspector documents six items in the report — electrical panel access, combustibles near water heater, crawl space clutter, under-sink moisture concerns, furnace obstruction, garage egress — each item individually might warrant a $1,000-$2,000 credit request. Six items together add up to a $10,000-$12,000 credit negotiation that the seller didn't need to have.

None of those items required a contractor. They required a weekend of clearing storage and being thoughtful about where things live in the house. That's the whole point.

Pre-listing walkthrough: Before I list any home in NJ, I walk it with the mindset of a buyer's inspector. Every one of these seven spots gets checked. Most sellers have two or three of them. The ones who get the cleanest inspections and the fewest post-contract credit requests are the ones who dealt with these first.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can storing boxes in front of the electrical panel cause NJ inspection issues?

Yes — this is one of the most commonly flagged items in NJ residential inspection reports. The NEC requires 36 inches of clear working space in front of electrical panels. Any obstruction gets documented. Clear all storage from the panel area before listing.

Should I clear my NJ crawl space before listing?

Yes, completely. Anything stored in a crawl space traps moisture, encourages mold, and blocks inspection of foundation, vapor barrier, and structure. A packed crawl space that cannot be inspected will generate buyer concern regardless of actual condition. Empty and clean it before listing.

What do NJ home inspectors check in garages?

NJ inspectors check fire door condition (must be solid-core, self-closing), clearance around water heater and furnace if located in the garage, egress path clarity, garage door safety sensors and auto-reverse, and evidence of moisture or pest intrusion. A well-organized garage signals maintenance discipline — buyers notice it.
